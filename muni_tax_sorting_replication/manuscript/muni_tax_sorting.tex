\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
%==========================================================

Taxes on wealth and income have been a growing discussion not only at the federal level but across states and local governments in the United States. Much of this debate revolves around growing concerns over inequality and revenue shortfalls. Recently, states such as Massachusetts and California have changed or proposed new taxation rules on the top earners in their states. Massachusetts recently abandoned its flat 5\% rate with a 4\% tax on income over \$1 million \citep{vermeerMassachusettsSurtax2022}. Washington State, which has never levied a broad income tax, enacted a 9.9\% rate on income above \$1 million in 2026 that does not take effect until 2028 \citep{waSB6346_2026}, and California has proposed a one-time 5\% levy on billionaires' net worth for the 2026 ballot \citep{walczakCaliforniaWealthTax2026}. Meanwhile, other states have also followed suit by either raising their income taxes or floating the idea of creating an income tax at all. 

It is not only state governments but local governments as well looking for funding sources as their municipal budgets get squeezed by inflation and shrinking government transfers. Local income taxation is more common than is often recognized: roughly $5{,}000$ jurisdictions across sixteen states levy some form of local income or wage tax \citep{walczakLocalIncomeTaxes2023}. New York City has levied its own income tax since 1966 \citep{walczakLocalIncomeTaxes2023}, and the current administration has proposed raising it on residents earning more than \$1 million a year to close a projected two-year budget gap \citep{nycMayorsOfficePrelimBudget2026}. Because the city's taxing authority is delegated by the state, the increase would require approval in Albany, a dependence on state enabling legislation that is characteristic of local income taxation, in general.

Most of this debate happens around the top earners in these states and localities, with much of the common criticism saying that the earners will simply move to avoid the new and higher taxes. That objection is long-standing in public finance, and it is testable: it is a claim about how much of the tax base relocates when a rate changes, and how far a jurisdiction can raise rates before the base it is taxing moves away. The evidence that speaks to it, however, comes almost entirely from variation in top marginal rates, and that variation does not exist below the state level, where local income taxes are generally flat and fall on every earner alike.

Prediction on the sorting of individuals given variation in taxes has a long history in economic theory. The Tiebout hypothesis predicts that residents sort across
jurisdictions which offer different fiscal amenities at different costs and residents will 'vote with their feet' and choose specific bundles of amenities for a given amount of taxation, this is called the Tiebout margin \citep{tiebout1956}. This has been tested widely in at the state level but not less so for municipalities. The literature has focused mostly on state tax adjustments on high-earners
\citep{young2016, young2011, agrawalRelocationRichMigration2019}. The research shows that
top-income households are generally mobile and sensitive to higher rates. Some studies have looked at specific higher income groups or niche domains such as star scientists, foreign workers, or even professional athletes \citep{morettiEffectStateTaxes2017, kleven2020, agrawalStateTaxationNonresident2024}. Specifically, \citet{agrawalStateTaxationNonresident2024} look at professional golfers' response to changes in nonresident income and find that they respond to changes in state taxation of nonresident income by changing where they work rather than where they live.

Few states permit local income taxes and the evidence in the United States is a handful of studies. The closest existing research comes from
\citet{yangRESPONSIVENESSINCOMELOCAL2017}, which finds that county-level taxable income in Indiana does not respond to small local rate changes, and \citet{yushkovLocalIncomeTaxes2025}, who finds county migration flows in Indiana and Maryland that are signed as expected but rarely significant. Pennsylvania's EIT has been studied for compliance rather than for sorting \citep{bagchiEffectsIntroducingWithholding2022}, and the nearest Pennsylvania precedent is \citet{livasLocalTaxesSuburbanization2026} on Philadelphia's wage tax and suburbanization. The strongest sub-state sorting evidence is from Switzerland, where municipalities set their own income tax rates and household location follows the resulting differences \citep{schmidheiny2006, schalteggerTaxCompetitionIncome2011, basten2017}.The imporant difference between the local level from the state level is that residence and workplace are often in different jurisdictions, meaning assigning taxation to residents or non-residents provides important variation for identifying migration effects. Municipalities may have an incentive to tax nonresidents in order to not burden their own residents but are constrained by both needed to raise revenue and not push away those who work but do not live in the municipality. Pennsylvania's municipalities often have to weigh who to tax, so a household is faced with a set of decisions in order to mitigate their tax bill such changing jobs, schools, or home.

A survey of the literature conducted by \citet{agrawalLessonsStateLocal} highlights the current findings of state and local income taxation and identifies sub-state (county, municipality, school districts) income-tax sorting as one of the field's open questions. The consensus of the survey is that the literature on local income taxes in the United States is almost non-existent and that what evidence exists is primarily from Switzerland. That matters because Swiss municipalities tax income on a purely residential basis while local systems in the United States assign income using non-residential rules, so the Swiss estimates do easily translate to the US. The notes that whether mobility appears as households moving or as jobs moving depends on the source of the taxation itself.

This paper estimates municipal-level migration effects among workers in Pennsylvania from 2002--2019, by merging local income tax data with the LEHD Origin-Destination
Employment Statistics (LODES), which links each worker's residence to their workplace annually and at sub-municipal geography. In Pennsylvania there are roughly $2{,}500$ municipalities and $500$ school districts (SD) that set
their own Earned Income Tax (EIT) rates
\citep{bagchiEffectsIntroducingWithholding2022,agrawalLessonsStateLocal},
producing a deep cross-section of multiple sub-state income-tax variation. The EIT offers two additional dimensions of variation that my
empirical design exploits. First, the EIT is set independently by two
overlapping rate-setting jurisdictions (municipalities and school
districts) and a resident's home-side tax bill is the sum of the
municipal resident rate and the school-district rate. This paper's primary
focus is the municipal resident rate while the school district rate enters the empirical
design but is treated as secondary.
Second, the EIT operates using a piecewise maximization rule where each worker's bill is
$T = \max(\tau^{\text{home}}, \tau^{\text{work,muni}})$, where
$\tau^{\text{home}} = \tau^{\text{res,muni}} + \tau^{\text{SD}}$, the
sum of the municipal resident rate and the school-district resident
rate, and $\tau^{\text{work,muni}}$ is the
work rate of the worker's workplace municipality. The workplace rate is also referred to as the nonresident rate, however, I refer to it as the work rate because the primary outcome variable of is the number of people that live and work within a municipality, which is the only variable that has the cleanest causal identification to the work and home-stack rate changes A statutory home-side rate
change only moves the bill if the home stack is on the binding side
of the max. Act~32 of 2008 (effective 2012) consolidated EIT
collection into county-based Tax Collection Districts and mandated
employer withholding at the higher of the two rates, which made the
max-rule mechanically enforced.
\citet{bagchiEffectsIntroducingWithholding2022} estimates that Act~32 resulted in a $14 \%$ increase in compliance and much higher local revenues. My paper does not analyze compliance but rather estimates
residential-sorting and provides additional analysis pre-post Act~32.

Beyond contributing to the general literature on local tax rate changes and residential sorting, this paper makes two methodological contributions. The first uses the piecewise structure of the EIT to split rate-change events by whether the home stack is on the binding side of the maximization function. Increases that move a worker's bill and increases that leave it untouched are statutory changes of the same size, so the non-binding cell is a placebo the design generates rather than one imposed from outside. This within-design heterogeneity is what \citet{agrawalLessonsStateLocal} identifies as missing from the existing U.S. local-income-tax literature.

The second is the treatment definition. Work on local income taxes codes a rate change as a binary indicator and recovers the average effect of having changed the rate at all \citep{yangRESPONSIVENESSINCOMELOCAL2017, yushkovLocalIncomeTaxes2025}. I use the size of the change, so the estimate is a response per percentage point rather than a response to the average change happened to be in one sample. That matters for two reasons. A binary coefficient is the per-percentage-point effect scaled by the realized distribution of rate changes, so it cannot be compared across settings with different distributions, and pooling changes of different magnitudes under a single indicator treats them as one treatment when they are several, which is the hidden-variations half of the stable unit treatment value assumption \citep[ch.~1]{imbensCausalInferenceStatistics2015}. The cost is a stronger identifying assumption, because reading the dose coefficient as a causal response requires trends to be parallel across dose magnitudes and not only between treated and control municipalities. Section~\ref{subsec:realized-dose-es} reports the event study that tests it.

The decomposition is implemented at the municipality-year level in
a continuous stacked difference-in-differences design that uses
never-changed municipalities as the control pool. The
school-district rate enters the design as a co-treatment on the
home-side stack but is not used as a separate unit of analysis.
Section~\ref{sec:theory} sets out the binding decomposition and what
each group implies for the worker's bill.

Municipalities that raised their resident rate saw the number of workers who both live and work there fall by about $9\%$. The binding decomposition shows that this average is carried entirely by the municipalities where the increase actually moved a bill. Where the home stack was already binding (Inc-bind), a typical increase reduces the count of workers who live and work in the municipality by about $10\%$. Where the increase pushed a home stack that had been sitting at the work rate above it (Inc-becomes), the effect is also about $10\%$, reached through a larger typical increase and a smaller response per percentage point. Where the rate rose but the home stack stayed below the work rate and no bill moved (Inc-nobind), the estimate is null. That cell is a placebo the design generates rather than one imposed from outside, and its null is the sharpest evidence that the response is to the tax bill and not to rate-setting behavior in general.

\section{Institutional Background}
\label{sec:background}
%==========================================================

Pennsylvania has a long history of setting their own income tax rates with approximately 2{,}500 municipalities and 500 school
districts each with the power to levy their own Earned Income Tax on
all earned income earned in or by Pennsylvania residents
\citep{bagchiEffectsIntroducingWithholding2022,agrawalLessonsStateLocal}.
The EIT falls directly on workers and varies across
municipal boundaries, creating
sharp spatial discontinuities in tax burden that can influence where
individuals choose to live and work.

This variation is the product of many decades of government statutes, influencing who may levy the tax, how high the rate may go, and who may be
taxed, all of which are key for the design in Section~\ref{sec:strategy}. Figure~\ref{fig:law_timeline} places the many statutes on a timeline that the next section walks
through in order.

\subsection{Pennsylvania Earned Income Tax}
\label{subsec:pennsylvania}

Local income taxation in Pennsylvania begins with the Sterling Act of
1932 \citep{paSterlingAct_1932}, the first local income tax legislation
in the country, under which Philadelphia became the first municipality in
the United States to impose an income tax in 1939. Philadelphia still
operates under the Sterling Act and remains
outside the framework governing the rest of the state. Its
residents pay the city resident rate regardless of work location, and nonresidents working in the city pay the city work rate. Philadelphia does not enter the estimation sample. Its
resident rate declines steadily across 2002--2019, and the design
identifies off increase-side events, so the city is dropped along with
every other municipality whose rate only falls.

Local income taxation outside Philadelphia was expanded in 1965 under the Local Tax
Enabling Act \citep[Act 511;][]{paAct511_1965}, which authorized
municipalities and school districts throughout the state to levy
an earned income tax on wages, salaries, commissions, and net profits. The
act enables a municipality and is not mandatory, therefore a municipality may levy no EIT at
all. Where a municipality and an overlapping school district both levy,
Act 511 caps their combined resident rate at one percent and splits it
fifty-fifty by default, unless the two taxing bodies agree otherwise.
School districts may not tax nonresidents. Act 511 also
keeps a commuter's two tax bills from stacking, and it does so by giving
the place of residence priority over the place of employment. That
priority, rather than any single rate schedule, generates the piecewise
structure the paper exploits.

Each Pennsylvania municipality therefore has three EIT rates:
%
the municipality resident rate $\tau^{\text{res,muni}}$ levied on residents of the municipality, the municipality work rate $\tau^{\text{work,muni}}$ levied on workers employed in the municipality, and the school-district rate $\tau^{\text{SD}}$ levied on residents only.
Two governments set these three rates. The municipality sets the resident
and work rates, and the school district sets its own. I refer to the two
throughout as the rate-setters, so a rate-setter is a government rather
than a rate, and the municipality is one rate-setter even though it
controls two of the three rates.

That priority means a worker's total EIT bill
is determined by the maximum of the home-side rate stack and the
work-location rate, and the bill is split piecewise
between home and work jurisdictions:
%
\begin{equation}
\bigl(\tau^{\text{home}},\ \tau^{\text{work}}\bigr) =
\begin{cases}
\bigl(\tau^{\text{res,muni}}_j + \tau^{\text{SD}}_j,\ 0\bigr)
   & \text{if } \tau^{\text{res,muni}}_j + \tau^{\text{SD}}_j \geq \tau^{\text{work,muni}}_i \\[4pt]
\bigl(\tau^{\text{res,muni}}_j + \tau^{\text{SD}}_j,\ \tau^{\text{work,muni}}_i - \tau^{\text{res,muni}}_j - \tau^{\text{SD}}_j\bigr)
   & \text{if } \tau^{\text{res,muni}}_j + \tau^{\text{SD}}_j < \tau^{\text{work,muni}}_i
\end{cases}
\label{eq:piecewise}
\end{equation}
%
where $j$ is the worker's home municipality and $i$ is the work municipality.
The home municipality and school district always collect their full
stack, and the work municipality collects only the amount by which its
nonresident rate exceeds that stack.
When the home stack is the larger of the two the work municipality collects
nothing
\citep{paDeskbook2025}. The worker's total bill is the higher of the two stacks:
%
\begin{equation}
\text{Bill}_{ji} = \max\!\bigl(\tau^{\text{res,muni}}_j + \tau^{\text{SD}}_j,\ \tau^{\text{work,muni}}_i\bigr).
\label{eq:bill}
\end{equation}

Equation~(\ref{eq:piecewise}) implies which margin a worker can use to
respond to a rate increase. When the home stack binds
($\tau^{\text{home}}_j > \tau^{\text{work,muni}}_i$), the worker can lower
the bill only by moving their residence to a lower-rate municipality or school district.
When the work rate binds, the worker can lower the bill
only by changing jobs to a lower-rate work location, while moving homes
without changing jobs has no effect. For workers who live and work in
the same municipality, the home stack and work rate are both set by that municipality, and the bill is determined by the home stack on the binding side.

A worker who lives in municipality $j$ and moves to a lower-rate municipality $j'$
while keeping the same job at $i$ saves at least the SD rate at $j$ where
the new bill is $\max(\tau^{\text{home}}_{j'}, \tau^{\text{work,muni}}_i)$,
which is at most the old bill $\tau^{\text{home}}_j$ minus
$\tau^{\text{SD}}_j$. The empirical design follows this logic directly by using both the treatment
$\Delta\tau^{\text{muni}}$ and the binding classification
built from the worker's home stack. 

State law allows the Act 511 cap to be exceeded in a small number of
circumstances. Municipalities adopting a home
rule charter under the Home Rule Charter and Optional Plans Law
\citep{paHomeRule} are no longer confined to the statutory limit on taxes
levied on their own residents, and some have raised the resident stacked rate
above one percent while the nonresident rate may not exceed the statutory limit for nonresidents.
Two distress regimes, Act 47 and Act 205, are the exception. A
municipality declared financially distressed under the Municipalities
Financial Recovery Act
\citep[Act 47 of 1987;][]{paAct47_1987} may petition to raise its EIT above the Act 511 limit as part of an adopted
recovery plan, renewable by annual petition, and Act 47 does not bar
extending the increase to nonresidents. A municipality certified as
having a financially distressed pension system under the Municipal
Pension Plan Funding Standard and Recovery Act
\citep[Act 205 of 1984;][]{paAct205_1984} holds the same above-cap
authority as a pension-recovery remedy, available only once the
municipality is already at the maximum rate otherwise allowed, and
Act 205 likewise permits extension to nonresidents.

This paper does not estimate the effect of nonresident increases on sorting due to the lack of binding rate changes of the workplace tax over the home-stack. For the work side to bind, a single jurisdiction must exceed the home-stack, which under the standard Act 511 rate structure almost never occurs: across 2002–2019, the work rate is the binding side in roughly $0.2\%$ of municipality-years. The handful of exceptions are the Act 47 and Act 205 distress municipalities just described, the only ones holding statutory authority to exceed the Act 511 cap on nonresidents. Because there are few work-binding events, a work-side binding decomposition is not identifiable.

School districts can go above the cap through referendum. Act 50 of 1998
\citep{paAct50_1998} first authorized a district to levy the EIT at up to
1.5\% with voter approval, offset by reducing real estate taxes through a
homestead exclusion; those EIT provisions were later repealed, and the
Taxpayer Relief Act \citep[Act 1 of 2006;][]{paAct1_2006} is now the route
by which a district puts an increase to voters in exchange for homestead
property tax relief \citep{paDeskbook2025}. A third route runs through both
levels of government: the Optional Occupation Tax Elimination Act
\citep[Act 24 of 2001;][]{paAct24_2001}, now Chapter 4 of Act 511, lets a
municipality or school district retire its occupation tax and raise the EIT
by the revenue that tax had produced, a swap rather than a net increase, and
it was extended to municipalities in 2002
\citep{shapiroCommonwealthPennsylvaniaa}. Together with home rule, Act 47 and
Act 205 on the municipal side, these are the statutory routes behind the
above-cap rates observed in the DCED register.

Act 32 of 2008 \citep{paAct32_2008} brought the largest change to the
local income tax system since the Local Tax Enabling Act. Signed in 2008 and effective for tax years beginning January 1, 2012, it
created 69 county-wide Tax Collection Districts, cutting the number of
collectors in the state from roughly 560 and created tax collection
committees to oversee collection and distribution within each district.
The statute further required uniform regulations, forms and procedures to ensure
consistent collections statewide. One district exists per county with two
exceptions: Philadelphia is outside the Act 32 framework entirely and has
no district, and Allegheny County is divided into four
\citep{paDeskbook2025}.

The provision that matters for identification is the withholding rule.
Act 32 requires every employer with a Pennsylvania worksite to deduct
from each employee the greater of that employee's resident tax or
nonresident tax, read off the official tax register that the Department
of Community and Economic Development
maintains. Each address is
associated with a six-digit Political Subdivision (PSD) code identifying
its (municipality, school district) pair. Workers report this code on the
Residency Certification Form, and employers use it to determine
withholding obligations.

This had two consequences.
First, it made the EIT mechanically binding where the rate changes now
appear in every worker's paycheck through employer withholding,
regardless of whether the worker resides in the taxing municipality.
Second, it eliminated the possibility of avoidance
of the work-location claim through weak enforcement.
After Act 32, a worker could no longer effectively avoid the
work-rate portion owed to the work municipality.

However, Act 32 did not eliminate all tax savings from
residential relocation. The piecewise rule in
Equation~(\ref{eq:piecewise}) continues to imply that a worker whose
original home stack binds can reduce the bill by moving to a
sufficiently lower-stack home jurisdiction where the new bill is
$\max(\tau^{\text{home}}_{j'}, \tau^{\text{work,muni}}_i)$, which falls
below the old home stack $\tau^{\text{home}}_j$ whenever
$\tau^{\text{home}}_{j'} < \tau^{\text{home}}_j$ and the original
home stack was the binding side. Act 32 thus changed enforcement
and made the max-rule mechanically binding through withholding and it
did not eliminate the residential-relocation margin that the
mechanism in this paper exploits.
\citet{bagchiEffectsIntroducingWithholding2022} highlighted the impact of Act 32 by showing that consolidated employer
withholding increased PA EIT compliance thus raising reported earned
income in the rate-raising municipality. The present paper complements that work by isolating
the residential-sorting response to the same withholding-enforcement
mechanism exploiting the variation in whether a given
rate change actually moves the worker's bill (the binding
decomposition) and a pre/post-Act-32 split in
Section~\ref{sec:robustness} shows the binding-cell sorting
response is strengthened (the effect existed prior to Act 32) after Act 32, complementing the
compliance-response finding.

Figure~\ref{fig:act32_cdf} documents the institutional break at
Act 32. Before 2012, fewer than 60\% of PA municipalities reported a
work rate to DCED, and after 2012, when consolidated withholding
made the max-rule mechanically binding, coverage rises sharply.
This is the pre-/post-Act-32 enforcement break used in the
heterogeneity test of Section~\ref{subsec:rob-act32}.

School districts levy their EIT under the same Act 511 authority. Since every census block sits in both a municipality
and a school district, workers face two overlapping EIT rates
that can change independently, providing a natural decomposition
of the residential sorting response into municipal and school
district components.

\subsection{Data Sources}
\label{subsec:sources}
% subsec:sample / subsec:pipeline kept as aliases: cross-references elsewhere
% in the draft still point at them.
\label{subsec:sample}
\label{subsec:pipeline}

The analysis merges the LEHD  residence--workplace dataset and the  Pennsylvania EIT rate records to create a panel of rate changes and workforce data over space and time. The two datasets do not share common identifiers, and was bridged using TIGER~2020 block geometry, which
nests blocks within county subdivision code (cousubs). A
municipality-name crosswalk maps each DCED municipality ID to its
corresponding COUSUBFP code, matching $2{,}496$ of $2{,}505$ PA municipalities
($99.6\%$). The remaining $9$ are small boroughs that have either dissolved or
merged during 2002--2019 and are dropped from the analysis.

The raw 2002--2019 municipality panel holds
$45{,}291$ muni-years across $2{,}559$ municipalities. I first drop
municipalities whose rate history moves in both directions over the
sample, because a municipality that raises and later cuts its rate cannot
be assigned a single binding-cell classification, along with
municipalities whose first event is a decrease, which are outside the
paper's scope and serve as neither treated units nor controls. The test
is applied to each of the three rates a municipality faces, its own
resident rate, its school district's resident rate, and its own
nonresident rate, so a municipality with a clean resident-rate history is
still removed if one of the other two reverses. The nonresident rate
accounts for $554$ of the $795$ municipalities dropped at this step, and
because that rate is not itself estimated here the restriction is
conservative rather than identifying. That
leaves $31{,}744$ muni-years across $1{,}764$ municipalities. I then drop
muni-years missing any fiscal control, which is the sample the regressions
select through listwise deletion, leaving $24{,}229$ muni-years across
$1{,}763$ municipalities. The second restriction removes years rather than
places, since AFR filing coverage is uneven early in the panel. Because
the outcomes are logged, each regression drops the additional muni-years
in which the relevant count is zero, so per-outcome sample sizes appear in
Table~\ref{tab:summary_muni} row by row.

The residence--workplace data come from LEHD LODES Version~8,
2002--2019 \citep{lehdLODES}. The Census Bureau applies
synthetic noise infusion at the block level to protect respondent
confidentiality and is calibrated so that aggregation to tract and larger
geographies approximates ground truth
\citep{grahamDESIGNCOMPARISONLODES}. The sample period of 2002--2019
reflects LODES coverage beginning in 2002 and
2020--2023 to not include disruptions in the data resulting from the COVID Pandemic.

The origin--destination file records job counts by residence block and
workplace block, which I aggregate to the municipality and then transform into the
four outcomes used. The first variable, Live-and-work, are workers whose residence
and workplace municipalities are the same, I argue this is the outcome a resident rate change
acts on most directly. The second outcome variable is in-commuters which are jobs located in the municipality
but held by residents of another. Third, total jobs is the total number of jobs in a municipality, a proxy for the measure of how well a municipality's economy is functioning. Lastly, live-and-work
share divides live-and-work workers by total jobs, so its denominator is
the local job base rather than the resident labor force. A coefficient on
that share therefore answers how the composition of a municipality's
workforce shifts, and it can move because residents leave or because
in-commuting grows.

Municipal and school-district earned income tax (EIT) rates come from the Pennsylvania
Department of Community and Economic Development (DCED) MunStats
portal \citep{padcedMunStats}, and is scraped for annual values. In some DCED reports, the flat-dollar Local Services Tax is filled in the same field
as the EIT in some years, so rates above $5\%$ are treated as
data-entry errors and dropped except for Home Rule cities. The municipality and SD assignment uses the SD rates reported for each muni in
the DCED rate panel ($97.6\%$ of municipalities are single-SD), so
each muni-year carries a single $\tau^{\text{SD}}$ co-treatment. For $2.4\%$ of municipalities that have multiple school districts that overlap within its borders I take the max of the set.

Municipal fiscal controls come from the PA DCED Statewide Municipal
Annual Financial Report (AFR) database \citep{padcedAFR}. School-district
fiscal controls come from the Census Annual Survey of School System
Finances (F-33) \citep{censusF33}, and the NCES \texttt{LEAID}-to-DCED
SD ID crosswalk covers all $500$ school districts. Municipality regressions use
four DCED AFR shares, three of expenditure (police services, streets,
debt service) and one of revenue (intergovernmental revenue), together
with the three effective property-tax rates described below. All fiscal
controls are share-based and time-varying. Two AFR shares are deliberately left out. First, the EIT-revenue share is a function of
the EIT rate, so it sits between the treatment and the outcome and
conditioning on it absorbs part of the effect being estimated. Second, the
real-estate-tax share is a revenue-side proxy for the same property burden
that the effective property rates measure directly so it is also not used in the estimation.

The property tax for municipal, school-district, and county real-estate
millage come from the same DCED MunStats register, and is scraped for annual values
\citep{padcedMunStats}. The county tax register reports general-purpose
and total millage for all $66$ assessing counties.

Millage is the statutory rate, one mill being one dollar of tax per
thousand dollars of assessed value. Assessed value is not market value.
Pennsylvania counties assess at widely different fractions of market
value and reassess rarely, so identical millage implies very different
burdens across counties and the statutory rate is not comparable on its
own. The common level ratio bridges the two. Pennsylvania law defines it
as the ratio of assessed value to current market value prevailing in a
county, determined annually by the State Tax Equalization Board from
validated sales data \citep{padcedSTEB}. County ratios come from the
Department of Revenue's historical factor series \citep{padorCLR}. The property tax is therefore calculated as:
%
\begin{equation}
\tau^{P} = \frac{\text{mills}}{1000} \times \text{CLR},
\label{eq:eff_property}
\end{equation}
%
I compute each jurisdiction separately since the municipality is the estimating unit and the three
governments set rates independently.

However, the tax calculation is not always accurate due to infrequent assessments. In a
reassessment the county revalues every property at current market value,
so assessed value rises to the market value and the ratio between them
should rise with it. Because the tax base has grown, each government
lowers its millage in proportion, and the effective tax bill the property owner
actually pays should not change. Where a government's millage was
never restated on the new basis, the old millage is paired with the new
ratio, and the computed burden jumps by the size of the reassessment and
stays high for the rest of the panel.

I correct the millage rather than dropping the observations. Two rules do the work. The first looks for
the reassessment. A reassessment is recognized by the ratio moving to
one, which is what assessing at full market value means, rather than by
how far the ratio moved, because ordinary market drift moves it every
year and some genuine reassessments move it very little. In a
reassessment year a government that failed to restate its millage is one
whose millage did not fall when the base grew, and the size of that
failure is also the correction. The second rule looks across
municipal governments. Every municipality in a county shares one ratio, so a
municipality whose computed burden sits far above its neighbors' for most
of the panel is on the wrong basis even when no reassessment inside the
sample reveals it. The correction touches a small share of observations,
leaves no municipal or county burden at an implausible level, and makes
the series smoother from year to year. Every corrected value is written
to an audit file recording what it was, what it became, and which rule
fired.

The school-district layer resists both rules and some districts remain
implausibly high. Those are transcription errors in the dataset, and districts whose
millage never matched their county's basis anywhere in the sample meaning
there is no break for the rules to find. I leave them rather than
dropping the variation. The school district enters the
municipality-level specification only as a control, and the headline
estimate barely moves when it is left out altogether, so what remains
does not affect the results.

\section{Empirical Strategy}
\label{sec:strategy}
%==========================================================

I estimate two specifications using a continuous stacked
difference-in-differences design. First, I run a pooled-increase specification (Section~\ref{subsec:pooled-spec}) that groups all
rate-increase events into a single
group. Second, I use the piecewise function in defined in
Section~\ref{sec:theory}, isolating the rate changes that move the
worker's bill from those that do not, called the binding
decomposition (Section~\ref{subsec:stacked-continuous}). Both specifications are
estimated at the municipality-year level. I also estimate an event study for both specifications. Pre-trends are tested
separately by a realized-dose Sun--Abraham event study
(Section~\ref{subsec:pretrend-diagnostics}). This section focuses on the causal parameters estimated,
the stacked difference in differences designs, identification, and diagnostics.


\subsection{Causal Parameters: ACR, ACRT, and ATT}
\label{subsec:causal-params}
%==========================================================

This paper estimates a stacked continuous difference-in-differences
design, specified in detail below. This section sets out what a
continuous treatment buys and what it costs. Continuous DiD is a fast
moving corner of the econometrics literature, and recent work has
sharpened considerably what has to hold before a given causal parameter
can be recovered.

In a standard 2 x 2 design the treatment is binary and, under the
usual assumptions, the coefficient is the average treatment effect on the
treated. A continuous treatment changes that, because the effect now
depends on how large the rate change was. One coefficient becomes three
related objects. Where the binary case asks whether a municipality
raised its rate, the continuous case asks by how much, and the additional
variation supports a greater number of parameters to estimate.

The clearest way to understand the three is as a curve. In \citet{bakerDifferenceinDifferencesDesignsPractitioners2025}, the authors plot the
effect against the dose $d$, running from zero upward. This paper hypothesizes a negative effect, so the same curve is drawn declining rather than rising, the mirror image of theirs. Each point on the curve is
an $\mathrm{ATT}(d)$, the total effect of a rate change of size $d$
measured against never-changers, so a municipality that raised its rate
by $d$ percentage points ends up $\mathrm{ATT}(d)$ log points away from one
that did not. The slope of the curve is $\mathrm{ACRT}(d)$, the average causal
response on the treated, which asks what one further percentage point
does to a municipality that has already raised its rate by $d$. The
average of that slope across the rate changes municipalities actually
chose, weighted by how common each one is, is the average causal response
(ACR). A linear specification returns exactly this last quantity
\citep{bakerDifferenceinDifferencesDesignsPractitioners2025,
dechaisemartin2024}. It is the headline estimand of the paper, and it
plays the role for a continuous treatment that the ATT plays for a binary
one.

The dose-response figures in this paper are fit to a natural spline in the dose, with interior
knots at the terciles of the positive treated doses and boundary knots at
zero and the largest observed dose. The spline is a visual device rather
than an estimating equation, and it is deliberately more flexible than
the data can support so that any bending is visible if it is there. Some
of it is, but not where it would matter most. A quadratic in the dose
rejects a constant marginal effect for the pooled increase group on the
count of workers who live and work in the municipality ($p = 0.004$) and
on their share of local jobs ($p = 0.024$). Fitted within cells the
rejection belongs to the partial pass-through group ($p = 0.012$) and not
to the binding group, where the squared term is nowhere near significant
on any outcome ($p$ between $0.60$ and $0.78$). Pooling groups whose
linear slopes differ will produce apparent curvature even when each group
is straight, so the pooled rejection is at least as much a statement about
mixing binding states as about the shape of any one response.

It is worth being explicit about what this means for the estimated model.
Under a linear specification the marginal effect cannot vary, so
$\text{ACRT}(d)$ is constant and equal to the ACR at every dose. The two
parameters are the same number, and the distinction between them is
visible only once the dose is allowed to enter flexibly. That is the work
the spline figures do, and it is the reason they are reported alongside a
linear headline rather than instead of one.

Curvature does not undermine the headline estimand, because the ACR is
defined as a dose-weighted average of marginal effects that vary with
dose. It is the case the ACR exists to summarise, and a linear
specification recovers that average whether or not the underlying curve
bends. What curvature rules out is reading the coefficient as a constant
marginal effect. The reported number is the average response across the
rate changes municipalities actually chose, not the effect of one further
percentage point at any particular starting point.

Section~\ref{subsec:rob-acr}
therefore re-estimates the effect with the de Chaisemartin and
D'Haultf\oe uille estimator, which recovers a weighted average of
marginal effects without imposing linearity and without the negative
weights a two-way fixed effects regression can place on some
municipality-year cells when effects are heterogeneous. Agreement between
the two is evidence that the linearity restriction is not driving the
result.

Besides the general assumptions of a DiD model, three assumptions are relied on for causal interpretation. The first is the strong parallel trends assumption which states that parallel trends must hold not
only between municipalities that raised their rate and those that did
not, but across the size of the increase, so that municipalities choosing
large increases would have followed the same path as those choosing small
ones had neither acted. The strong parallel trends assumption is what permits interpreting the slope of the dose-response curve as
causal \citep{callawayDifferenceindifferencesContinuousTreatment2024}.
Section~\ref{subsec:realized-dose-es} tests it directly by using an event study to tag each
municipality with the rate change it eventually takes and asks whether
municipalities headed for large changes were already on different
trajectories. The second assumption is no anticipation, meaning that outcomes in the years
before a rate change do not already respond to it. Rate changes are set
in public budget processes with some lead time, so this is an assumption
about how far in advance workers act rather than about whether the
change could have been foreseen at all. Section~\ref{subsec:realized-dose-es}
tests it jointly with parallel trends, since pre-event coefficients
respond to either failing. Third, the stable unit treatment value
assumption (SUTVA), which has two components. The first is the standard assumption of no spillovers from the treated unit to the control group or vice-versa. The second is that there cannot be hidden variations of treatment, such that a given treatment level does not stand for several different interventions. It is the second component that a continuous treatment satisfies and a binary coding of the same policy does not. In the first requirement a household leaving one municipality may move to a
neighboring one that may sit in the control pool, which would bias the effect toward zero. In the second component, the dosage is no more or less than what is observed such that if a municipality raised its EIT by half a percentage point it did not in fact raise it by one, nor do we assign a binary treatment where
every increase is condensed into a single (binary) treatment where very different
magnitudes would be treated as the same intervention \citep{imbensCausalInferenceStatistics2015}. Lastly, stacking adds one
further requirement, that never-changing municipalities are clean
controls throughout each event window, which the classification enforces
by admitting only municipalities whose rates never move over the whole
sample.

\subsection{The Binding Decomposition}
\label{sec:theory}
% subsec:cells kept as an alias: the old subsubsection was merged into
% this section, and a cross-reference downstream still points at it.
\label{subsec:cells}
%==========================================================

The piecewise tax allocation in Equation~(\ref{eq:piecewise}) drives the causal identification of my empirical design. A worker's bill is
the larger of two rates. The home rate
$\tau^{\text{home}} = \tau^{\text{res,muni}} + \tau^{\text{SD}}$ is set by where the worker lives such as the municipality and the school district
together. The work rate $\tau^{\text{work,muni}}$ is set by the
municipality where the worker is employed and applies to people who work
there without living there. As Equation~(\ref{eq:piecewise}) sets out,
the home side always collects its full stack and the work side collects
only the amount by which its rate exceeds that stack, so the worker's
total is the larger of the two. A rate change therefore moves that total
only if it moves the side that is already larger, or if it lifts the home
rate past the work rate.

An increase can therefore land in one of three ways, and these are the
groups the design estimates. Where the home rate already exceeds the work
rate, the increase passes through in full and the bill rises by the whole
rate change (Inc-bind). Where the home rate starts below the work rate
and ends above it, only the portion above the work rate reaches the
worker, so the bill rises by less than the rate change (Inc-becomes).
Where the home rate rises but stays below the work rate throughout, the
bill does not move at all (Inc-nobind). Municipalities
that never change a rate form the control pool (NC). Three mirror
decrease groups exist by construction but are not estimated here, because
the decrease cohort is thin ($4$ Dec-bind, $3$ Dec-becomes-non, $11$
Dec-nobind) and the realized-dose pre-trend rejects parallel trends on
every decrease outcome.

This design produces three testable predictions across coefficients. Each coefficient should be descending in effect size from Inc-bind where the effect is the largest and exceeds
Inc-becomes effects, which in turn exceed Inc-nobind effects, with
the last close to zero and serving as the
within-design placebo. A tax salience argument would predict a larger
Inc-becomes response than pass-through alone implies, since that is the
group where the home stack crosses the work rate and the work
municipality stops collecting from these workers altogether, which is a
discrete change in who taxes them even though the total moves only
partially. However, the Inc-bind is taxed the most already and adding an additional tax on top of their already high tax may prove to be a stronger effect than Inc-becomes. If Inc-nobind, being the in-design placebo, has a larger and statistically significant effect, the response is not the binding
rule, because an Inc-nobind increase leaves the worker's total bill
untouched. Some other aspect such as municipal spending on specific goods and services may be driving the outcome. In the decomposition model the piecewise function predicts that only a subset of
increases should generate a response, however, I also run a pooled model where every increase is considered treatment event to understand if the simple case of increases induce migration.

\subsection{Pooled Specification}
\label{subsec:pooled-spec}

In this section I report an aggregate specification that pools the
treated Increase cells by rate-setter into a single group
$\text{Inc}$ (any resident rate increase), with never-changers (NC) as the control group. The pooled
regression answers, what is the average per-percentage-point (pp)
effect of a rate hike on residents, ignoring whether or not the
tax increase binds?

For each unit-time observation $(i, t)$ on the stacked panel, the
per-pp pooled regression is

\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
Y_{\tilde i \tilde t} \;=\; \alpha_{\tilde i} \;+\; \gamma_{\tilde t}
&+ \beta^{M}_{\text{Inc}}\;\tau^{M}_{it}\,\mathbf{1}\!\left\{d^{M}_{i} = \text{Inc}\right\} \\
&+ \beta^{SD}_{\text{Inc}}\;\tau^{SD}_{it}\,\mathbf{1}\!\left\{d^{SD}_{i} = \text{Inc}\right\} \\
&+ \mathbf{X}'_{it}\boldsymbol{\phi}
\;+\; \varepsilon_{\tilde i \tilde t}.
\end{split}
\label{eq:pooled}
\end{equation}

The dependent variable $Y_{\tilde i \tilde t}$ is the outcome of
interest. At the municipality level the
outcomes are the logs of live-and-work workers (residents employed
in their own municipality), in-commuters, log total jobs, and the
share of workers living in their own municipality. The unit $i$ is a municipality and $t$ a calendar year. The subscripts $\tilde i = (i, c)$ and
$\tilde t = (t, c)$ are the unit-by-stack and year-by-stack
groupings where each treated unit $i$ appears in exactly one
sub-experiment (its first-event cohort $c$), while each NC unit
appears in every cohort. Cohort $c$ is defined as the first year that the rate-setter changed $\tau$. Each sub-experiment runs on the window
$t \in [c-5,\, c+5]$, so a cohort contributes eleven years centred on its
own event. The fixed effects
$\alpha_{\tilde i}$ and $\gamma_{\tilde t}$ are for each unit and cohort pair. The directional grouping
$d^{s}_{i} \in \{\text{NC}, \text{Inc}\}$ classifies
unit $i$'s rate trajectory on rate-setter $s \in \{M, SD\}$, with
NC the omitted reference as they are the control group. The
treatment variable $\tau^{s}_{it}$ is the statutory rate itself,
in percentage points, entered in levels. The unit fixed effects
$\alpha_{\tilde i}$ absorb each unit's own rate level, so the
coefficient is identified from within-unit movement in the rate and
reads as a response per percentage point of rate change. The coefficient of interest is $\beta^{M}_{\text{Inc}}$, which is interpreted as the 
per-percentage-point effect on $Y$ of a municipal resident-rate increase.
Because the rate enters linearly, it is an average causal response,
weighted by how common each rate change is. $\beta^{SD}_{\text{Inc}}$ is its school-district
counterpart, included so the municipal effect is estimated separately of what
the school district did instead of absorbing it.
This design is close to \citet{yushkovLocalIncomeTaxes2025}, who studies
local income tax changes with a binary treatment indicator. I extend that
approach to the continuous variation, using the size of each rate change
rather than its occurrence. The control vector
$\mathbf{X}_{it}$ holds four municipal fiscal shares (police services,
streets and debt service as shares of expenditure, intergovernmental
revenue as a share of revenue) and the three effective property-tax
rates described in Section~\ref{subsec:sources}.
$\boldsymbol{\phi}$ is the
coefficient vector on these controls and $\varepsilon_{\tilde i \tilde t}$
is the error term. Standard errors are clustered at the municipality,
the jurisdiction that sets the rate and therefore generates the
variation being used.

\subsection{Binding Decomposition}
\label{subsec:stacked-continuous}

The binding decomposition is the main specification of the paper, and it is
Equation~\ref{eq:pooled} with now decomposed by the binding principles outlined above. The one Inc group is split
into the three binding groups of Section~\ref{subsec:cells}, so Inc-bind,
Inc-becomes and Inc-nobind each receive their own coefficient and NC
remains the control. Decrease groups are not estimated, for the
reasons given in Section~\ref{sec:strategy}. Everything else is
unchanged, including the panel, the fixed effects, the rate in levels,
the controls, and the clustering.

\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
Y_{\tilde{i}\tilde{t}} \;=\; \alpha_{\tilde{i}} \;+\; \gamma_{\tilde{t}}
&+ \sum_{k \in \mathcal{C}_{-\text{NC}}} \beta^{\text{muni}}_k \;\tau^{\text{muni}}_{it} \cdot \mathbf{1}\{\text{cell}^{\text{muni}}_i = k\} \\
&+ \sum_{k \in \mathcal{C}_{-\text{NC}}} \beta^{\text{SD}}_k  \;\tau^{\text{SD}}_{it}   \cdot \mathbf{1}\{\text{cell}^{\text{SD}}_i = k\}   \\
&+ \mathbf{X}'_{it}\boldsymbol{\phi}
\;+\; \varepsilon_{\tilde{i}\tilde{t}}.
\end{split}
\label{eq:headline}
\end{equation}

For each rate-setter $s$ and outcome $Y$ the index $k$ runs over the three estimated groups,
$\mathcal{C}_{-\text{NC}} = \{\text{Inc-bind, Inc-becomes, Inc-nobind}\}$,
and $\text{cell}^{s}_{i}$ is the group municipality $i$ was assigned on
rate-setter $s$ by its first rate change. That label is fixed once
assigned and does not vary over time.

The indicator
$\mathbf{1}\{\text{cell}^{\text{muni}}_i = k\}$ equals one when
municipality $i$ belongs to group $k$ and zero otherwise. The municipality's own rate is then multiplied by the
coefficient belonging to its group $k$. For example, an Inc-bind municipality contributes
$\beta^{\text{muni}}_{\text{Inc-bind}}\,\tau^{\text{muni}}_{it}$, an
Inc-becomes municipality contributes
$\beta^{\text{muni}}_{\text{Inc-becomes}}\,\tau^{\text{muni}}_{it}$, an
Inc-nobind municipality contributes
$\beta^{\text{muni}}_{\text{Inc-nobind}}\,\tau^{\text{muni}}_{it}$, and a
never-changer is set to zero because it is the comparison group. The sum is
therefore a compact way of writing three separate slopes, one per group,
estimated in the same regression against a shared set of controls. The second summation does the same on the
school-district side.

I interpret each $\beta^s_k$ as an average causal response, the
dose-weighted average per-percentage-point effect of rate $s$ on $Y$
within group $k$, rather than the marginal effect at any single dose
\citep{bakerDifferenceinDifferencesDesignsPractitioners2025,dechaisemartin2024}.
Section~\ref{subsec:rob-acr} shows this ACR is recovered with non-negative
weights and is not distorted by dose curvature.
$\beta^{\text{muni}}_{\text{Inc-nobind}}$ is the within-design placebo,
where the increase leaves the bill untouched, so it should be
statistically indistinguishable from zero.

\subsection{Realized-Dose Event-Study Diagnostic}
\label{subsec:pretrend-diagnostics}
\label{subsec:realized-dose-es}

Equations~\ref{eq:pooled} and~\ref{eq:headline}  cannot test for pre-treatment leads
directly because $\tau^s_{it}$ does not move before the event, so once
the unit fixed effect removes each unit's own level there is no
pre-event variation left for a lead coefficient to be estimated from. I therefore run a separate
realized-dose Sun--Abraham event study on the same stacked panel
to test for strong parallel trends. I design the event study as recommended
for continuous-treatment DiD designs by
\citet{callaway2021} and
\citet{dechaisemartin2024} which states that units with
larger eventual rate changes should not be on differentially sloped
pre-event trajectories relative to NC controls. For each cell on
rate-setter $s$ I restrict the panel to units in (NC, cell) and
run:

\begin{equation}
Y_{it} = \alpha_{\tilde i} + \gamma_{\tilde t}
+ \sum_{k \neq -1} \beta^M_k\, \mathbf{1}\{\ell_{it} = k\}\,
\widetilde{D}_i
+ \mathbf{X}'_{it}\boldsymbol{\phi} + \varepsilon_{it}.
\label{eq:realized-dose-es}
\end{equation}

Two variables are new relative to Equation~\ref{eq:headline}. The
event-time index $\ell_{it} = t - c^M_i$ counts the years between
calendar year $t$ and unit $i$'s first muni-rate event year $c^M_i$,
with $\ell = -1$ (one year before the event) as the omitted
reference year and in the sum below $k$ denotes a value of $\ell$, an
event-time, in contrast to Equation~\ref{eq:headline}, where $k$
indexed the binding cells. The realized-dose tag
$\widetilde{D}_i = \tau^M_{i, c+5} - \tau^M_{i, c-1}$ is a single number per unit where $i$'s eventual cumulative rate
change is the observed rate five years after its first event minus the
rate the year before and held fixed across all event-time years rather
than moving only at the event as $\tau^M_{it}$ does in
Equation~\ref{eq:headline}. It enters the regression only through the
interaction $\mathbf{1}\{\ell_{it}=k\}\,\widetilde{D}_i$, which equals
$\widetilde{D}_i$ in the single year unit $i$ is at event-time $k$ and
zero in all its other years because that varies within unit, the
unit fixed effect does not
absorb it. The outcome $Y_{it}$, the unit-by-stack and year-by-stack
fixed effects, the control vector $\mathbf{X}_{it}$, the units of
observation, and the policy-jurisdiction clustering are the same
as in Equation~\ref{eq:headline}. I report the
joint Wald $p$-value as a single pre-trend
statistic for each cell-outcome combination.

This section describes the event study results. The event study was modeled using the realized dose over the sample period. I elected for this event study design over a binary because it is able to answer whether the response
appears when the rate changes rather than before it and whether
municipalities that eventually make large increases were already moving
differently from those that make small ones.
Figure~\ref{fig:feols_es_bycell} plots the event-time paths, and the full
set of leads and lags behind it is reported cell by cell in
Tables~\ref{tab:es_full_pooled} through~\ref{tab:es_full_incnobind}.

The leads are the coefficients at event
times $-5$ through $-2$, each measuring how far apart large-dose and
small-dose municipalities already were in that year relative to the year
before the rate changed. The joint Wald test asks whether all four leads
are zero at the same time. A large $p$-value means the test cannot detect
a pre-existing divergence, which is the desired outcome, and a small $p$-value indicates cities are moving on different paths prior to treatment.

reaching $-0.789$ per percentage point by the fifth year. Its four leads
average $-0.116$ and the joint Wald returns $p = 0.053$, which is a narrow pass. Inc-becomes shows the same post-event shape at roughly a

rejects at $p = 0.029$. Only the cell where the bill was already moving

($p = 0.293$), nor does the pooled specification ($p = 0.259$), while
Inc-nobind again rejects below $0.001$.

The two commuter-side outcomes present a different set of results. In-commuters and total jobs Inc-bind is the cleanest cell in the table,
with Wald values of $0.775$ and $0.783$, while Inc-becomes rejects on both
at $p < 0.001$ and Inc-nobind at $0.005$ and $0.001$. Inc-bind's leads on
these outcomes are positive and imprecise and should be noted that in figure~\ref{fig:feols_es_bycell} the commuter-side shows a general decline over the entire sample from $-5$ to $+5$.

Across the four outcomes the Wald test does not provide a single verdict on whether the strong parallel trends assumption is satisfied or not. Visually, the pre-tends do look to satisfy the assumption as well as looking at the pre-period coefficients where only a few a statistically significant across outcome variables. In Section~\ref{subsec:rob-battery} runs the joint
Wald alongside a linear pre-slope, which tests the alternative that
actually threatens the design, namely treated and control municipalities
drifting apart at a steady rate before the tax changes. The joint Wald
rejects on any departure in any shape, including one stray year. Across
the sixteen cell-by-outcome rows the Wald rejects on nine, and in the slope test on only
three. Where both the Wald and the linear pre-slope reject, the estimate should be interpreted cautiously and 
not causally, which is the case for in-commuters and total jobs in
Inc-becomes.

For Inc-bind on the resident workforce no lead is individually
significant, the linear pre-slope is $+0.024$ and indistinguishable from
zero ($p = 0.47$), and the joint Wald sits at $p = 0.053$. Extrapolated
forward its pre-period predicts a mild increase rather than the decline
that follows.
What the battery establishes is which estimates survive being looked at
from several directions, and the Inc-bind effect on the resident workforce
is the one that does.

The de Chaisemartin and D'Haultf\oe uille estimator in
Section~\ref{subsec:rob-acr} supports these results on both counts. Its effect estimates line up with the ones above once the two
are put on the same scale, and its placebo detects no pre-trend on any of
the four outcomes, with $p$-values between $0.23$ and $0.69$. That is a
friendlier outcome than the one this section reports, and the reason is
that the two tests ask different questions and therefore estimate differently. The placebo compares
municipalities that switch against those that have not switched yet, which
is average parallel trends and is what a binary design requires. The
realized-dose test tags each municipality with the rate change it will
eventually take and asks whether the size of that change predicts the
pre-event path, which is strong parallel trends and is what a continuous
treatment requires
\citep{callawayDifferenceindifferencesContinuousTreatment2024}. Strong parallel trends is stricter than average parallel trends, and is the one required for this design.
Section~\ref{subsec:rob-acr} takes the comparison up in full.

liability increases which is a stronger predicter than the rate increase itself, for example, Inc-nobind raised rates by
more than any other cell and shows no statistically significant effect. Third, the identification
is secure for one cell and one outcome and weaker elsewhere. The Inc-bind
effect on the count of workers who live and work in the municipality
survives every diagnostic applied to it, though the joint Wald only
narrowly. The remaining cells and outcomes carry pre-trends the design
cannot rule out, and I read them further validating evidence.

resident workforce, $-0.218$ against $-0.212$ on in-commuters and $-0.243$
against $-0.233$ on total jobs, slightly larger each time rather than
smaller.

I do not have a clear understanding of why the inc-bind estimates are vastly different. Some possible reasons are that each model is
run on its own subsample of never-changers, the headline is stacked, with
repeated never-changer copied and stack-interacted fixed effects, while
the dCDH estimator runs on the unstacked panel. The headline coefficient is an
average post-treatment level shift per unit of rate, while the figure here
accumulates effects across periods before normalizing by the treatment
received. Additionally, the two do not use the same number of units, since Inc-bind is
identified off $17$ municipalities (switchers) here against $24$ municipalities in the
headline.

The joint placebo test in the table is this estimator's own pre-trend
check. It estimates how the periods before the treatment were either similar or moving independently, and tests whether they are jointly zero.
A large $p$-value means it cannot detect a divergence and is the
favourable outcome. Inc-bind passes on the three count outcomes, at
$0.553$ on the resident workforce, $0.218$ on in-commuters and $0.296$ on
total jobs, and fails on the share at $0.000$. Inc-becomes passes on the
resident workforce at $0.445$ and fails on the other three, at $0.000$ on
the share, $0.037$ on in-commuters and $0.005$ on total jobs.

Figure~\ref{fig:dcdh_bycell} plots the event studies that connect to the $p$-values summarized. The two commuter outcomes decline
steadily after the rate change in every group and most steeply for
Inc-becomes, which reaches $-0.208$ on in-commuters and $-0.223$ on total
jobs by the fifth year. The pre-event periods, while not being statistically significant, are also in a general decline. Inc-becomes is also the group whose pre-event path diverges from the other groups. It runs positive through the first three
leads and then dips at $t-2$ and $t-1$, to $-0.164$ and $-0.107$ on
in-commuters. That dip is what the placebo rejects at $0.037$ and $0.005$.

The resident-side outcomes behave differently. Pre-event paths on the
count of workers who live and work in the municipality are flat in all
three groups, wandering near zero without direction, and the placebo
clears every one of them at $0.553$, $0.445$ and $0.258$.

The share is the exception, where all three groups sit near zero through the first four leads and then jump
in the period immediately before the rate changes, by $+0.009$ for
Inc-bind, $+0.026$ for Inc-becomes and $+0.016$ pooled. The jump in the share outcome and the variations in pre-periods is likely causing the rejection at $0.000$. The pooled group shows
the same jump and clears at $0.693$, but is generally smoother across pre-periods than Inc-becomes.

These outcomes and the realized-dose test of
Section~\ref{subsec:results-es} disagree at the pooled
level and agree on the rows that matter most. Both reject on the Inc-bind
share, at $0.000$ here against $0.005$ there. Both reject on the Inc-becomes
commuter outcomes, at $0.037$ and $0.005$ here against $0.000$ and $0.000$
there. Two tests built on
different comparisons and different parallel-trends assumptions flag the
same three rows. Where they do not agree is the resident outcomes, which
the realized-dose test rejects for Inc-becomes and this one does not, and
which this test clears for Inc-bind more than the realized-dose
test does. Only one of the two agreements is
visible in the figure. The Inc-becomes commuter panels climb through the
early leads and then swing negative, which is a trend an eye can find. The
Inc-bind share is flat on the page and rejects on precision alone.

\subsection{Pre/Post Act 32 Decomposition}
\label{subsec:rob-act32}

This section splits the specification at the Act-32
enforcement break (cohort first event year $<2012$ vs $\geq 2012$)
and tests whether the binding-cell effect strengthens under consolidated
employer withholding
(\hyperref[app:rob-act32]{see Appendix~\ref{app:rob-act32}}).

The results show that the effect actually weakens in the post-2012 period. Inc-bind is essentially unchanged on three of the four
outcomes, at $-0.544$ before the break against $-0.531$ after on the
resident workforce, $-0.337$ against $-0.362$ on in-commuters and $-0.432$
against $-0.415$ on total jobs. Each of those differences sits well inside
either standard error and every one of the six coefficients is significant
at five percent. The largest deviation is the live-and-work share, which halves
from $-0.068$ to $-0.043$ while remaining significant on both sides.

Inc-becomes attenuates modestly across the board, from $-0.296$ to
$-0.222$ on the resident workforce, $-0.218$ to $-0.209$ on in-commuters
and $-0.276$ to $-0.203$ on total jobs. The share outcome is the one coefficient
that changes sign, from $-0.043$ (significant to $10\%$) pre-2012 to $+0.004$ after and is not statistically significant. Inc-nobind has no pre-2012
estimate at all. Eleven of the municipalities that would be estimated for this cell fall on pre-2012 side of the
break and their dose term is collinear with the stack fixed effects on the
thin pre-enforcement subsample, so the estimator drops it.

The hypothesized effect was that consolidated withholding should have made the tax harder
to avoid and that the response should be larger in the post-period. The model shows the opposite. One reason this may occur is that Act~32 changed collection rather than tax liability, such that a household already paying its home rate faced the same bill before and
after and had the same reason to move.

\subsection{Pre-Trend Battery}
\label{subsec:rob-battery}

Table~\ref{tab:pretrend_battery} reports three pre-trend tests, each
sensitive to a different kind of failure. The joint Wald tests all four pre-event leads against zero
simultaneously. It rejects on any departure of any shape, which makes it
the most demanding of the three and also the one most easily upset by a
single stray coefficient or by a poorly estimated covariance matrix on a
thin cohort. A clean result is a large $p$-value.

The count of individually significant leads asks a narrower question: is
any one pre-event coefficient distinguishable from zero at five percent.
A clean result is zero of four.

The linear pre-slope fits a single trend through the pre-event window and
tests its slope on one degree of freedom. It is insensitive to an isolated
spike and identifies treated and control municipalities drifting apart at a steady rate
before the treatment event. A clean result is a slope indistinguishable from
zero.

The three tests do not all agree and drawing from the
distinction that the disagreement supports in Section~\ref{subsec:results-es}. The
joint Wald rejects on nine of the sixteen cell-by-outcome rows, the linear
pre-slope on three, and only five rows carry any individually significant
lead. Two rows fail on both the Wald and the slope, in-commuters
($p = 0.0003$ joint, $p = 0.001$ slope) and total jobs ($p = 0.0004$ and
$p = 0.001$), both in Inc-becomes. Of the remaining seven Wald rejections,
four are the whole of Inc-nobind, where no lead is individually
significant and the slope rejects on none of the four outcomes, and the
other three are the Inc-bind share, the Inc-becomes resident workforce and
the pooled resident workforce, each with a flat slope.

The table is not a certificate and is not built as one. It is a sorting
device, and what it sorts to the top is Inc-bind, which clears all three
tests on three of its four outcomes and fails the joint Wald only on the
share. What it sorts to the bottom is the pair of Inc-becomes commuter
outcomes, which fail all three and should be read as descriptive rather
than causal. Inc-nobind sits outside the sort, failing the joint Wald on
everything while passing the other two on everything, which is what
thirteen municipalities produce and is why the cell is treated as
uninformative rather than clean. The short version for a reader is that
the design supports a causal reading for one cell and corroborating
evidence elsewhere, which is a narrower claim than the count of
significant coefficients in Table~\ref{tab:headline_muni_combined} would
suggest.

\subsection{Fake-Timing Placebo}
\label{subsec:rob-faketiming}

Every test in Section~\ref{subsec:rob-battery} tests the same sample of pre-event
coefficients. This check is different in that each treated
municipality is assigned a placebo event three, four, and five years before
its real one, and only data from before the real event occurs is allowed in the
estimation. The design then estimates an effect where no rate
changed at all, meaning the coefficients
should be null. A failure here means the specification finds an
effect out of a pre-period where there should not be one, which is a different issue from treated
and control municipalities moving in different direction in the pre-period.

The Inc-bind cell passes on both live-and-work and share of residents working in a municipality. Its
live-and-work coefficients are $+0.053$, $+0.071$ and $-0.033$ across the
three shifts, none distinguishable from zero, and its share coefficients
are $0.000$, $+0.020$ and $+0.003$.

Inc-becomes does not pass. Its live-and-work placebo is
$-0.173$ at the three-year shift ($p = 0.013$) and $-0.179$ at five years
($p = 0.055$). Read together with that cell's failure on both pre-trend tests
in Section~\ref{subsec:rob-battery}, its coefficients carry the weakest
causal warrant in the paper. The non-binding cell's share fails at the four- and five-year shifts
($-0.074$ and $-0.065$).

The three panels overlap heavily and are consistency checks rather than
independent tests, and eligibility requires two placebo pre-years inside
the panel, so the municipality count falls as the shift lengthens.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

In the results, I find that general tax increases drive migration out of municipalities, but that result is mostly due to the binding decomposition. A municipality that raises a rate where the tax is already binding loses $0.537$ log points of resident workforce per percentage point.
One whose increase now pushes the home stack above the work rate (becomes binding) loses
$0.250$ log points of resident workforce per percentage point. One whose rate rises while the tax bill stays below the work rates finds no statistically significant effect. Only
the already binding and becomes binding changed what anyone owed, and are the only two to show a
response.

The comparison with the existing literature is directly comparable, because
no two of these papers share an outcome variable nor the same methodology.
\citet{yangRESPONSIVENESSINCOMELOCAL2017} study county-level aggregate
taxable income in Indiana, the total income reported on state returns filed
in a county, and conclude that taxpayers are unresponsive to small changes
in local rates. % what are their estimates? Do they use log or pp?
\citet{yushkovLocalIncomeTaxes2025} are the most similar to my design but use county-to-county
migration counts from IRS records, and finds inflows and outflows that are
negatively signed but not statistically significant in most specifications.
While the designs and outcome variables may not be directly comparable, the useful contrast between them is the sign of their estimate and statistical significance and not necessarily the magnitude of the effect. The binding-cell estimates are negative, which is the direction
the mechanism predicts, and in most cases statistically significant at the one percent level on the
resident-side outcomes, where \citet{yushkovLocalIncomeTaxes2025} and \citet{yangRESPONSIVENESSINCOMELOCAL2017} work returns coefficients that are not statistically significant. Two possible reasons for the differences are that: (1) LODES counts the workers who live in a municipality, so the
outcome variable is the population of interest that the resident rate actually falls on rather than
an aggregate. (2) I utilize a
continuous stacked difference-in-differences design that uses the size of each rate
change in the model, which keeps municipalities with small and large increases in the same sample without condensing the additional variation that provides into a single treatment. My pooled specification, which is
the closest in design to theirs, returns $-0.284$ against $-0.537$ in the
binding cell. The results show that averaging municipalities whose bills moved under binding or non-binding changes the pooled outcome.
% you pointed out two things actually: 1 being the decomposition, 2 being the continuous treatment vs the binary

The Pennsylvania setting has been studied before for compliance rather
than for sorting. \citet{bagchiEffectsIntroducingWithholding2022} shows
that Act~32's consolidated withholding raised compliance by about
fourteen percent. That finding does sits well with the findings in
Section~\ref{subsec:rob-act32}, which returns $-0.544$ before the break and
$-0.531$ after. If enforcement brought a larger share of residents into
actually paying the tax, a rate increase would mean a higher
coefficient after the break compared to the pre-period. However, before Act~32 employers were not
generally required to withhold the EIT, except
employees who lived and worked in the same municipality \citep{bagchiEffectsIntroducingWithholding2022}. The residents whose
compliance Act~32 changed were mostly those working somewhere other than
where they lived, and \citet{bagchiEffectsIntroducingWithholding2022} finds
the compliance gains smallest in districts where the most residents lived and
worked in the same place. The enforcement landed largely
outside the live and work outcome variable, so finding relatively little effect is not unsurprising. The two papers also do not share the same unit, where his
estimates are at the school district and mine at the municipality, so the
comparison is directional but completely comparable. What the split establishes
is that the effect is not an artifact of the enforcement regime, and not
that enforcement is irrelevant.

\citet{livasLocalTaxesSuburbanization2026} also study earned income taxes in Pennsylvania but specifically on Philadelphia's wage tax and the implications on
suburbanization.
Philadelphia's levy sits outside the Act~511 system, the tax rate is much higher, and is excluded from my sample. However, our research finds commonality in that what is estimated is the
response to a change in local income taxes in the same state and the resulting migration due to that tax increase. The two papers are complements in that they estimate responses to
different instruments at different scales, and the estimate in my paper is assessing what the effect for an average Pennsylvania municipality can expect from
a typical rate increase. %what does any of this actually mean?

While there is not a copious literature on local income taxes in the United States, there are many studies focusing on Switzerland. Comparing against the Swiss research the comparison is more difficult and an extrapolation between outcomes. \citet{basten2017} identifies the effect of tax
differences between municipal boundaries and finds a decrease in rents and high willingness to pay as incomes increase, while
\citet{schmidheiny2006} finds that higher income households are more likely to move to low-income municipalities, and
\citet{schalteggerTaxCompetitionIncome2011} finds similar results from the income
composition of communities across the canton of Zurich. One major difference between my paper and the Swiss papers is that Swiss municipal income taxes are progressive, so a
rate difference gives high-income households a stronger reason to move
than low-income ones, whereas my research looks at a flat tax. Pennsylvania's EIT, being flat does not offer the same incentive by income, and LODES worker counts do not identify income at a level that would let me test for one. However, the research may permit extrapolation in that it is possible the individuals in my sample are wealthier earners and that as taxes increase higher income individuals move out but as the dosage reaches a certain level that may stall and the last marginal individual willing to move is taxed out. 

There are three limitations worth discussing. First, the estimates are not equally well identified. The
binding cell passes the fake-timing placebo at all three shifts and shows
no linear pre-trend, while the inc-becomes cell fails both
pre-trend tests and the placebo on its commuter outcomes. Second, the sample is
restricted to municipalities whose rate paths do not reverse, which
removes Pittsburgh, Allentown, Reading, and Johnstown among others, so the
estimates describe monotone rate-setters rather than the full state of municipalities.
Lastly, in-commuters make up
$89$ percent of total jobs and sit in the denominator of the live-and-work
share, and the municipal resident rate never touches an in-commuter's
bill. I utilized the commuter side outcomes as a measurement of general labor market health.

In terms of policy, the statutory increase produces a large response, a smaller one, or
none at all depending on where a municipality's rate sits relative to the
work-side rate its residents face. A municipality raising a rate that
already binds is choosing a different policy from one raising a rate that
does not, and the revenue and residential
consequences of the two are not the same.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper estimates the movement of workers when a local income tax rises. In Pennsylvania, workers move when the increase is clearly changing their tax bill. This is identifiable by using the piecewise structure
of the Earned Income Tax to separate rate changes that alter the tax liability
from rate changes that do not, I find that municipalities raising a
binding rate lose resident workers at $0.537$ log points per percentage
point, that municipalities whose increase newly becomes binding lose
$0.250$, and that municipalities whose rate rises without affecting the tax
bill show no statistically significant response. At the average
increase, Inc-bind loses about twelve percent of
the workers who live and work there.

The identification in this paper is a novel approach to estimating the causal effect of local income tax increases on worker migration. While some cells and
outcomes do not remain perfectly identified given the plethora of diagnostics run in
Section~\ref{sec:robustness}, the results generally show a statistically significant and predictable response. The evidence
supports the response that tax liability experienced rather than statutory
rates, through the law determine whose bill moves is important to consider versus simply averaging over.

\section{Flexible Dose Specifications}
\label{app:flex-dose}

The headline enters the dose linearly, this appendix drops that restriction and estimates the same
model with a quadratic and with a spline in the dose.

The three specifications differ in the dose term and nothing else:
%
\begin{equation}
Y_{it} = \alpha_i + \gamma_t + \beta\,d_{it}
  + \mathbf{X}_{it}'\phi + \varepsilon_{it}
\label{eq:flex-linear}
\end{equation}
%
\begin{equation}
Y_{it} = \alpha_i + \gamma_t + \beta_1 d_{it} + \beta_2 d_{it}^{2}
  + \mathbf{X}_{it}'\phi + \varepsilon_{it}
\label{eq:flex-quad}
\end{equation}
%
\begin{equation}
Y_{it} = \alpha_i + \gamma_t + B(d_{it})'b
  + \mathbf{X}_{it}'\phi + \varepsilon_{it}
\label{eq:flex-spline}
\end{equation}
%
$d_{it}$ is the cumulative change in the municipal rate and
$\mathbf{X}_{it}$ the fiscal controls. $B(\cdot)$ in
Equation~(\ref{eq:flex-spline}) is a natural cubic spline with interior
knots at the terciles of the positive treated doses and boundary knots at
zero and the largest observed dose. Each dose term is interacted with the
binding cell, as in the headline. The sample, controls, school-district
co-treatment, stacked municipality and year fixed effects and clustering
are the headline's, and all three share one complete-cases set, so $N$ is
the same in every column.

One difference from the headline is deliberate. The headline regressor is
the rate level and these are the cumulative change. For a linear term the
two are the same once municipality fixed effects absorb the baseline rate,
which is why Equation~(\ref{eq:flex-linear}) reproduces the headline
exactly in Table~\ref{tab:flex_dose_acr}. They are not the same for a
nonlinear term, because $d^2 = b_i^2 + 2b_i c_{it} + c_{it}^2$ leaves a
baseline-by-change term the fixed effects cannot remove. Fitting
Equation~(\ref{eq:flex-quad}) in levels flips the sign of both
coefficients, so the choice matters. The change is what the paper's
dose-response describes.

the three specifications differ. ACRT$(d)$ is constant by construction
