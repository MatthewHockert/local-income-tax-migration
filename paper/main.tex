\title{Local Income Taxes and Residential Sorting: \\
\large Evidence from LEHD Origin-Destination Data}
\author{Matthew Hockert\thanks{University of Minnesota. Email: hocke043@umn.edu}}
\date{\today}

\begin{document}

\begin{abstract}
Do local income tax increases cause residential sorting across municipal
boundaries? Using LEHD Origin-Destination data linking each worker's
residence to their workplace at municipal resolution, I estimate the
effect of earned income tax rate changes on the locally-resident share
of a city's workforce in Ohio and Pennsylvania, 2002--2019. Rate
increases reduce this share by 0.5--0.8 percentage points, building
gradually over 7--8 years. The response is asymmetric: rate increases
push residents out, but rate decreases do not bring them back.
Pennsylvania's Act 32 (2012), which consolidated EIT collection and
mandated employer withholding, provides a natural experiment: the
sorting response is a precise zero before 2012 and highly significant
after, confirming that workers only respond to taxes they actually pay.
Tract-level decomposition reveals that the sorting operates through
cross-boundary relocation rather than within-city reshuffling, that
school district EIT rates attract residents (fully offsetting municipal
rate repulsion in high-SD areas), and that the boundary gradient is
null---sorting is city-wide, not concentrated at municipal edges. Despite
the behavioral response, rate increases are fiscally productive: EIT
revenue rises 67\% per percentage point, total tax revenue rises 30\%,
and population and property values are unaffected. The residential
sorting induced by local income taxes is real but too small to erode
the tax base.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
%==========================================================

The Tiebout hypothesis---that households ``vote with their feet'' by
sorting across jurisdictions offering different bundles of taxes and
public services \citep{tiebout1956}---remains a central organizing
principle in local public finance. A large empirical literature
documents that state-level tax differences influence the residential
choices of high earners \citep{kleven2020, young2016} and that local
sales taxes generate cross-boundary shopping responses
\citep{agrawal2015gradient}. Yet surprisingly little evidence exists
on whether \textit{local} income tax differences cause residential
sorting \textit{within} metropolitan areas, where moving costs are low
and tax boundaries are crossed by a short relocation.

This gap persists for two reasons. First, most jurisdictions that levy
local income taxes (municipalities in Ohio, Pennsylvania, and a handful
of other states) are too small to appear in standard migration datasets.
The IRS Statistics of Income tracks county-to-county migration, but
local income taxes vary at the sub-county level; the American Community
Survey measures place-level population but does not link workers to
their employers. Second, local income tax systems feature credit
mechanisms, overlapping school district taxes, and varying enforcement
regimes that complicate the mapping from statutory rates to effective
tax burden. A naive comparison of tax rates and population changes
conflates tax-motivated sorting with amenity valuation, fiscal capacity
differences, and enforcement quality.

This paper addresses both challenges using LEHD Origin-Destination
Employment Statistics (LODES), the only administrative data source
that links each worker's residence tract to their workplace tract at
annual frequency. From the approximately 24 million tract-pair-year
observations per state, I construct a municipal-level outcome---the
share of a city's workers who also live there---and a set of
disaggregated tract and city-pair panels that decompose the sorting
response into distinct adjustment channels. The two-state design
exploits complementary sources of variation: Ohio provides a clean
extensive margin (taxed municipalities versus untaxed townships),
while Pennsylvania's universal EIT with rate variation, overlapping
school district taxes, and the natural experiment of Act 32 (2012)
enforcement consolidation enables tests of mechanism and heterogeneity.

The main finding is that local income tax rate increases reduce the
locally-resident share of a city's workforce by 0.5--0.8 percentage
points, building gradually over 7--8 years. The response is
asymmetric---rate increases push residents out, but rate decreases do
not bring them back---and is entirely concentrated after 2012, when
Pennsylvania's Act 32 made employer withholding mandatory and the tax
became binding. These results are robust across estimators
\citep[Sun-Abraham,][]{sunab2021}, continuous treatment models
\citep{dechaisemartin2024}, and both states.

Decomposing the aggregate response reveals three findings that depart
from the canonical Tiebout prediction. First, the sorting operates
through cross-boundary relocation (workers leave the city entirely)
rather than within-city reshuffling, and the boundary gradient is
null---the effect is city-wide, not concentrated at municipal edges.
Second, school district EIT rates have the \textit{opposite} effect:
higher school district rates attract residents, completely offsetting
municipal rate repulsion in high-SD areas. Act 1 of 2006 authorized
SD rate increases specifically for property tax relief, though our
data cannot confirm that property taxes actually fell (the PDE fiscal
data postdates most SD rate changes). This explains why studies
using total EIT rates find null results. Third, commuter destination
choices are unrelated to the destination's municipal EIT rate,
exactly as the credit system predicts: workers who keep their job
gain no tax savings from moving.

Despite the real behavioral response, rate increases are fiscally
productive. EIT revenue rises 67\% per percentage point of rate
increase, total tax revenue rises 30\%, and population and property
values are unaffected. The residential sorting induced by local income
taxes is too small to erode the tax base. For municipal
policymakers considering a rate increase, the message is clear: you
will lose some residents at the margin, but you will keep the revenue.

\section{Institutional Background}
\label{sec:background}
%==========================================================

Local income taxes are one of the few fiscal instruments available to
municipalities in Ohio and Pennsylvania. Unlike property taxes, which
are paid by property owners and capitalized into home values, local
income taxes fall directly on workers and vary across municipal
boundaries within the same metropolitan area. This creates sharp
spatial discontinuities in tax burden that can influence where people
choose to live and work.

\subsection{Ohio Municipal Income Tax}
\label{subsec:ohio}

Ohio authorizes municipalities (cities and villages) to levy a municipal
income tax on earned income under Ohio Revised Code Chapter 718.
Unincorporated townships cannot levy the tax, creating a clean
extensive-margin contrast between taxed and untaxed jurisdictions
within the same county. As of 2019, approximately 685 municipalities
levy the tax at rates ranging from 0.5\% to 3.0\%, with 1.0\% and
1.5\% being the most common. Our panel (2002--2019) contains 302 rate
increases and 45 rate decreases.

Ohio operates a credit system: residents who work in a municipality
with a higher tax rate than their residence receive a credit against
their home municipality's tax for taxes paid to the work municipality.
However, this credit system has historically been administered at the
municipal level without centralized enforcement, and the credit is
often partial (capped at the home municipality's rate or a fraction
thereof). This means that for Ohio workers, the interaction between
home and work municipality rates determines the effective tax burden,
but imperfect crediting can leave workers paying more than the
statutory maximum of either rate alone.

\subsection{Pennsylvania Earned Income Tax}
\label{subsec:pennsylvania}

Pennsylvania's Earned Income Tax (EIT) is mandatory for all 2,500+
municipalities under the Local Tax Enabling Act (Act 511 of 1965).
The EIT applies to all earned income with no minimum threshold;
retirees are exempt. The standard cap is 1\%, typically split
0.5\% municipal and 0.5\% school district. Home Rule municipalities
may exceed this cap, with Philadelphia operating under a separate
Sterling Act authority at approximately 3.8\%. The EIT is the primary
revenue lever for most Pennsylvania municipalities: property tax
millage caps are binding in many jurisdictions, and EIT rate changes
are uncorrelated with property tax changes ($r = -0.04$). Our panel
contains 291 rate increases and 162 rate decreases.

\paragraph{The Credit System.}
A critical institutional feature is the credit mechanism governing
how EIT is allocated between a worker's municipality of residence
and municipality of employment. A worker's total EIT liability equals
the \textit{higher} of the resident rate and the work (nonresident) rate:
%
\begin{equation}
    \tau^{\text{total}}_w = \max\!\left(\tau^{\text{res}}_w,\; \tau^{\text{work}}_w\right)
    \label{eq:credit}
\end{equation}
%
If a worker lives and works in the same municipality, they simply
pay that municipality's rate. If they live in a low-rate municipality
but work in a high-rate one, the employer withholds the work rate;
the home municipality receives nothing beyond its own rate (or the
residual after the work municipality's claim). Crucially, if a worker
moves from a high-rate municipality to a lower-rate one while keeping
the same job, their \textit{total tax liability does not change}---they
continue to pay the work municipality's rate through employer
withholding, with the credit system ensuring the work municipality
collects the difference.

This has a direct implication for interpreting residential sorting.
Under the credit system, a worker who changes residence but not
workplace gains no tax savings from the move. The only way to reduce
one's EIT burden is to change both residence and workplace to a
lower-rate jurisdiction, or to move one's residence to a municipality
whose rate equals or exceeds the work rate (so the residence rate
governs and the work municipality receives nothing).

\paragraph{Act 32: Tax Collection Consolidation.}
Before 2012, EIT collection was radically decentralized: each of
Pennsylvania's 2,500+ municipalities appointed its own tax officer.
Nonresident EIT enforcement was weak because no centralized system
linked employers, workers, and municipalities. A worker who moved
out of a rate-raising municipality could, in practice, avoid paying
the nonresident portion to the work municipality even though they
legally owed it. The credit system described in Equation~\ref{eq:credit}
existed on paper but was not effectively enforced.

Act 32 of 2008 (effective January 1, 2012) transformed this system
by consolidating collection into approximately 67 county-based Tax
Collection Districts (TCDs), each with a single appointed collector.
The law mandated standardized employer withholding at the higher of
the employee's resident and work rate. This had two consequences.
First, it made the EIT \textit{binding}: rate changes now mechanically
appear in every worker's paycheck through employer withholding,
regardless of whether they reside in the taxing municipality.
Second, it eliminated the possibility of de facto tax avoidance
through residential relocation. Before Act 32, moving away from a
rate-raising city could effectively reduce a worker's tax burden
because the work municipality lacked the enforcement capacity to
collect from nonresidents. After Act 32, the credit system is
fully enforced, and moving away while keeping the same job provides
no tax relief.

\paragraph{School District EIT.}
School districts in Pennsylvania also levy an EIT under Act 511,
typically at 0.5\% (the other half of the standard 1\% cap).
Act 1 of 2006 (the Taxpayer Relief Act) additionally authorized
school districts to exceed the 0.5\% cap specifically to fund
property tax relief. This generated a wave of school district
rate increases in 2006--2011, creating rate variation that is
largely independent of municipal fiscal decisions: the correlation
between municipal and school district EIT rates across our panel
is $-0.12$. Since every census tract sits in both a municipality
and a school district, workers face two overlapping EIT rates
that can change independently, providing a natural decomposition
of the residential sorting response into municipal and school
district components.

\section{Conceptual Framework}
\label{sec:theory}
%==========================================================

We develop a framework for how local income tax changes affect the
spatial distribution of workers across municipalities. The key
institutional features described above---the credit system, Act 32
enforcement, and overlapping municipal and school district taxes---generate
distinct predictions about \textit{who} moves, \textit{where} they
go, and \textit{why}.

\subsection{Three Channels of Adjustment}
\label{subsec:channels}

Consider a municipality $A$ that raises its EIT rate. Workers
currently employed in $A$ can respond through three channels, each
with different implications for their tax burden:

\paragraph{Channel 1: Residential relocation without job change.}
A worker moves their residence from $A$ to another municipality $B$
but continues working in $A$. Under the credit system
(Equation~\ref{eq:credit}), this worker's total EIT liability is
$\max(\tau_B, \tau_A)$. If $\tau_A > \tau_B$---the typical case
when $A$ just raised its rate---the worker still pays $\tau_A$.
\textit{Moving provides zero tax savings.} This channel reduces
$A$'s share\_live\_in (our primary outcome) but does not reduce the
worker's tax burden.

\paragraph{Channel 2: Complete relocation (residence and job).}
A worker moves both residence and job from $A$ to municipality $B$,
where $\tau_B < \tau_A$. Their new liability is $\tau_B$, yielding
genuine tax savings. This is the canonical Tiebout sorting response.
This channel reduces both $A$'s resident worker count and total
employment. In our data, it appears as a decline in the
$(w\!=\!A, h\!=\!A)$ cell of the origin-destination matrix and an
increase in the $(w\!=\!B, h\!=\!B)$ cell for nearby municipalities $B$.

\paragraph{Channel 3: Compositional change in new hires.}
When $A$ creates new positions or replaces departing workers, the
new hires may choose not to live in $A$ given the higher rate. They
take the job in $A$ but live in $B$. Under the credit system, they
pay $\max(\tau_B, \tau_A) = \tau_A$, so this choice reflects
non-tax considerations (housing costs, school quality, neighborhood
preferences). This channel reduces $A$'s share\_live\_in even though
no existing resident moved.

\subsection{Predictions}
\label{subsec:predictions}

These channels generate testable predictions that differ from a
naive Tiebout model:

\paragraph{Prediction 1: Enforcement matters.}
If the sorting response is driven by workers optimizing their tax
burden (Channel 2), the effect should depend on whether the tax is
actually collected. Before Act 32, when nonresident enforcement was
weak, a rate increase was partly a paper change. After Act 32, it
mechanically reduces take-home pay. We therefore expect the sorting
response to be \textit{stronger} after 2012, not because moving
became more attractive (it became less so under full credit
enforcement), but because the rate increase itself became binding.
Workers only respond to taxes they actually pay.

\paragraph{Prediction 2: Destination rates should not matter for stayers.}
Workers who change residence but keep the same job (Channel 1) gain
no tax advantage from choosing a lower-rate destination. If the
rate gap between work and home municipality predicts commuter flows,
it reveals a non-tax channel: housing cost capitalization, amenity
perception, or fiscal quality signaling. If destination rates are
irrelevant to commuter flows, the credit system is operating as
designed and workers are not confused about their tax liability.

\paragraph{Prediction 3: Complete relocators should choose lower-rate destinations.}
Workers who change both residence and job (Channel 2) \textit{can}
reduce their tax burden by choosing a lower-rate municipality.
If cities near a rate-raising municipality experience gains in
resident workers, and those gains are larger for lower-rate
destinations, this confirms tax-motivated complete relocation.

\paragraph{Prediction 4: School district taxes should have opposite effects.}
Municipal EIT rate increases are typically associated with fiscal
stress---the municipality needs revenue. School district EIT rate
increases, particularly those authorized under Act 1 of 2006,
were designed to fund property tax relief and school spending.
Higher school district EIT rates may therefore signal a
\textit{valued amenity} (better schools, potentially lower property
taxes) rather than fiscal extraction.
If so, school district rate increases should \textit{attract}
residents, offsetting the repulsive effect of municipal rate
increases. This decomposition is possible because municipal and
school district rates vary independently ($r = -0.12$) and every
tract sits in both jurisdictions simultaneously.

\paragraph{Prediction 5: Sorting should occur at municipal boundaries.}
If EIT rate changes cause cross-boundary residential sorting,
the effect should be concentrated at the edges of municipalities
where moving a short distance crosses a tax boundary. Interior
tracts, far from any municipal boundary, should show weaker
responses. The absence of a boundary gradient would suggest that
the ``sorting'' is actually a city-wide population decline
unrelated to spatial tax arbitrage.

\section{Data}
\label{sec:data}

\subsection{Data Construction Pipeline}
\label{subsec:pipeline}

The analysis requires linking three administrative systems that do not
share common identifiers: LEHD/LODES employment records (Census tract
geography), Ohio municipal tax rates (Census place geography), and
Pennsylvania EIT rates (DCED municipality IDs). We construct the
analysis panels through the following steps:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Scrape PA DCED MunStats tax rates.}
    PA does not publish a downloadable panel of historical EIT rates.
    The only source is the DCED MunStats portal, which serves one XLS
    file ($\sim$12 MB) per year via an ASP.NET form. A Python script
    submits requests for each year 2000--2026, extracting resident and
    nonresident EIT rates, school district EIT rates, and Local
    Services Tax for all $\sim$2,500 municipalities.

    \item \textbf{Scrape PA DCED Annual Financial Reports.}
    Fiscal controls require municipality-level revenue and expenditure
    data. The DCED Annual Financial Reports (same portal, different
    report) provide 71 columns per municipality-year including tax
    revenue by type, police and streets expenditure, debt, population,
    and market value of real estate. Years 2002--2023.

    \item \textbf{Download Ohio rate table.}
    Unlike PA, Ohio publishes a complete rate history as a single CSV
    (``The Finder'') with start date, end date, municipality FIPS, and
    rate for every municipal income tax spell. Direct download.

    \item \textbf{Download LODES origin-destination files.}
    LODES provides the only administrative data linking each worker's
    residence tract to their workplace tract at annual frequency.
    We download main files (intra-state flows) and auxiliary files
    (cross-state flows where the workplace is in-state but the
    residence is out-of-state) for OH and PA, 2002--2021. The
    auxiliary files are necessary because excluding cross-state
    commuters would mechanically inflate share\_live\_in for border
    municipalities. Segments include all jobs (S000), earnings
    categories (SE01, SE03), age groups (SA01--SA03), and federal
    employment (JT04, used as a placebo).
    Reference date: April 1 of each year.

    \item \textbf{Expand Ohio rates to municipality $\times$ year.}
    The Finder reports rate spells (start/end dates), not a panel.
    We expand to municipality $\times$ year, where a rate is active
    in year $Y$ if it was in effect on April 1 of year $Y$---aligned
    to the LODES reference date so that the rate a worker faces and
    the OD flow that records their location refer to the same point
    in time. Compute year-over-year rate changes and flag each
    municipality's first increase year (the treatment cohort for
    Sun-Abraham).

    \item \textbf{Crosswalk PA DCED to Census geography.}
    LODES uses Census tract geography; PA rates use DCED municipality
    IDs with no Census equivalent. PA municipalities are county
    subdivisions (COUSUBFP), not Census places, so a standard
    place-level crosswalk does not work. We match DCED municipality
    names to TIGER/Line COUSUBFP codes using normalized name + county.
    Match rate: 99.6\%. The 9 unmatched municipalities are boroughs
    and townships that were merged or dissolved before the 2020 Census.

    \item \textbf{Build tract-to-municipality crosswalks.}
    LODES reports flows at the tract level, but treatment varies at
    the municipality level. We assign each tract to a municipality
    by spatially joining tract centroids to 2020 TIGER/Line boundaries
    (OH places, PA county subdivisions). This is necessary for
    aggregating tract-level OD flows to the municipality panel and
    for the tract-level decomposition in Stage~2.

    \item \textbf{Compute city-to-city neighbor pairs.}
    The relocation and commuter pair models require defining which
    municipalities are ``neighbors.'' We compute centroid-to-centroid
    distances between all municipality pairs and retain those within
    80~km. This radius captures the plausible commuting and relocation
    zone while excluding distant municipalities whose labor markets
    do not overlap.

    \item \textbf{Aggregate LODES to municipality-level OD panels.}
    The raw LODES data has $\sim$24 million tract-pair-year
    observations per state. We aggregate to work municipality $\times$
    year, counting total jobs, live-and-work (workers who also reside
    in the work municipality), commuters from other in-state
    municipalities, and cross-state commuters. This produces
    share\_live\_in, the primary outcome: the fraction of a city's
    workforce that also lives there.

    \item \textbf{Merge rates into OD panels.}
    The final step joins rates to OD panels, producing the
    analysis-ready municipality $\times$ year datasets.
    OH municipalities with no rate history are assigned rate = 0,
    forming the no-tax control group.
    PA municipalities without a DCED-to-Census match are dropped
    (9 of 2,505 municipalities, all merged or dissolved before 2020).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Data Sources}
\label{subsec:sources}

\paragraph{LODES Origin-Destination Data.}
LEHD LODES Version 8, 2002--2019. Tract-level residence--workplace
links derived from state Unemployment Insurance wage records and the
Quarterly Census of Employment and Wages. We trim 2020--2021 due to
COVID-related disruptions in both employment patterns and LODES
coverage. Noise infusion is applied at the census block level;
aggregation to the municipality level substantially reduces
measurement error.

\paragraph{Municipal Tax Rate Panels.}
Ohio: Department of Taxation ``The Finder'' CSV. Rate spells with
start/end dates for all 685 taxing municipalities. Direct PLACEFP
join to LODES.
Pennsylvania: DCED MunStats, scraped annually. Resident and
nonresident municipal EIT rates, school district EIT rates for all
$\sim$2,500 municipalities. Name-based crosswalk to Census COUSUBFP
(99.6\% match rate).

\paragraph{Municipal Financial Reports.}
PA DCED Annual Financial Reports, 2002--2019 (trimmed from 2002--2023
to exclude COVID/ARPA fiscal distortion). 71 columns per
municipality-year including EIT revenue, real estate tax revenue,
police expenditure, streets expenditure, total debt, state
intergovernmental aid, population, and assessed market value of real
estate.

\paragraph{Crime.}
FBI Uniform Crime Reports, Offenses Known and Clearances by Arrest
(Jacob Kaplan concatenated files), 2002--2019. Monthly agency-level
counts of Part I index crimes (murder, robbery, aggravated assault,
burglary, larceny-theft, motor vehicle theft). Aggregated to annual
totals per agency. Matched to PA municipalities via \texttt{fips\_place\_code}
(93.5\% agency match rate).

\paragraph{Building Permits.}
Census Bureau Building Permits Survey (BPS), place-level annual files,
2002--2019. Total housing units permitted by structure type (1-unit,
2-unit, 3--4 unit, 5+ unit). PA municipalities matched via
\texttt{fips\_mcd\_code} = COUSUBFP (93.3\% MCD match rate).

\subsection{Sample Restrictions and Transformations}
\label{subsec:sample}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Sample period: 2002--2019.}
    LODES coverage begins in 2002. We trim at 2019 because COVID
    disrupted both commuting patterns (LODES 2020--2021) and municipal
    finances (ARPA transfers distort AFR data 2020--2023). Including
    these years would conflate pandemic-driven relocation with
    tax-motivated sorting.

    \item \textbf{Minimum employment threshold: 50 total jobs.}
    Small municipalities have share\_live\_in values dominated by
    sampling noise (a single worker moving changes the ratio by
    $1/N$). The 50-job threshold balances precision against sample
    size. Sensitivity analysis (Section~\ref{sec:robustness}) confirms
    stability across thresholds from 10 to 500.

    \item \textbf{Rate outlier filters.}
    PA DCED reports the Local Services Tax (a flat dollar amount) in
    the same field as the EIT rate (a percentage) in some years.
    Dropping rates $>5$\% removes these data entry errors without
    affecting any real EIT rate (the highest standard EIT is $\sim$1\%;
    only Philadelphia exceeds 3\%).

    \item \textbf{April 1 alignment.}
    LODES records employment as of April 1. A rate change that takes
    effect mid-year could be misattributed if not aligned. We assign
    each municipality-year the rate in effect on April 1, ensuring
    the tax rate a worker faces and the OD flow recording their
    location refer to the same date.

    \item \textbf{Outcome variables.}
    The primary outcome is $\text{share\_live\_in}_{it} =
    \text{live\_and\_work}_{it} / \text{total\_jobs}_{it}$, the
    locally-resident fraction of a city's workforce. Unlike population
    counts (which mix retirees, students, and non-workers) or IRS
    migration data (which is county-level), share\_live\_in isolates
    the residential choices of active workers at municipal resolution.

    To decompose the share response into levels, we also estimate
    effects on log(resident workers), log(nonresident commuters), and
    log(total jobs) separately. Stage~2 introduces additional outcomes:
    log(commuter flow) for city-pair panels, log(resident workers) for
    the neighbor exposure model, and the within-city residence share
    at the tract level. Fiscal outcomes include log EIT revenue, log
    total tax revenue, log total revenue, log police expenditure, log
    population, and log market value of real estate from the PA DCED
    Annual Financial Reports. Pre-treatment balance tests cover eleven
    observables including crime and building permits.

    \item \textbf{Treatment definitions.}
    We use three treatment measures to test different margins.
    \textit{Binary}: 1 after a municipality's first rate increase,
    identifying the extensive-margin sorting response and enabling
    Sun-Abraham estimation.
    \textit{Continuous}: the current rate level, capturing the
    intensive margin via DCDH and TWFE.
    \textit{Cumulative dose}: current rate minus baseline, isolating
    the net rate change from the rate level for treated-only
    dose-response models.

    \item \textbf{Control groups.}
    OH offers two natural control groups: (a) municipalities that
    never adopted a tax (clean extensive-margin contrast, 104 cities)
    and (b) municipalities with a stable rate (intensive-margin
    contrast, 403 cities). PA has only (b) because EIT is universal
    (2,261 never-changed municipalities). The no-tax control in OH
    provides the cleanest identification; the stable-rate control in
    PA provides the largest donor pool for Sun-Abraham.

    \item \textbf{Fiscal controls.}
    Rate increases may coincide with other fiscal changes (police
    hiring, debt issuance) that independently affect residential
    attractiveness. We include log police expenditure, log streets
    expenditure, log total debt, and log state intergovernmental aid
    as time-varying controls from the PA DCED AFR. The main estimates
    are unchanged with and without these controls
    (Table~\ref{tab:robustness}).

    \item \textbf{Neighbor exposure.}
    To test whether workers displaced from rate-raising cities
    relocate to nearby lower-rate municipalities (Channel~2), we
    compute each municipality's average neighbor EIT rate within
    80~km. A positive coefficient on neighbor rates indicates that
    cities absorb workers when surrounding municipalities raise rates.

    \item \textbf{Commuter pair panel.}
    The municipal-level outcome cannot distinguish residential
    relocation from complete job relocation. We disaggregate LODES
    to (work city $A$, home city $B$, year) pairs within 80~km,
    enabling direct tests of how rate changes in the work municipality
    affect the flow of commuters from specific home municipalities.
    Pair fixed effects absorb time-invariant distance, amenity
    differentials, and initial rate gaps.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Empirical Strategy}
\label{sec:strategy}
%==========================================================

We organize the analysis in two stages. The first stage establishes
the baseline sorting response using municipal-level outcomes, building
from the extensive margin (tax adoption) through the intensive margin
(rate changes) to asymmetry. The second stage uses tract-level
origin-destination data to decompose the sorting response into the
three channels described in Section~\ref{subsec:channels} and to
test the predictions from Section~\ref{subsec:predictions}.

All specifications include municipality (or tract, or pair) and year
fixed effects, with standard errors clustered at the municipality
level. The municipality fixed effects absorb time-invariant differences
across jurisdictions; the year fixed effects absorb aggregate shocks
common to all municipalities (e.g., business cycles, statewide policy
changes). Identification comes from within-municipality rate variation
over time.

\subsection{Stage 1: Municipal-Level Sorting}
\label{subsec:stage1}

For binary treatments under staggered timing, we use the
\citet{sunab2021} interaction-weighted estimator:
%
\begin{equation}
    Y_{it} = \alpha_i + \gamma_t + \sum_{\ell \neq -1} \mu_\ell \cdot D_{it}^{\ell} + \mathbf{X}'_{it}\boldsymbol{\phi} + \varepsilon_{it}
    \label{eq:sunab}
\end{equation}
%
\noindent where $Y_{it}$ is share\_live\_in for municipality $i$ in
year $t$, $D_{it}^{\ell}$ indicates municipality $i$ being $\ell$
years from its first treatment, and $\mathbf{X}_{it}$ is a vector
of fiscal controls (police expenditures, streets, debt service, and
state aid; available for PA). Pre-treatment coefficients
($\ell < -1$) test parallel trends. For continuous treatments (the
actual tax rate), we use the \citet{dechaisemartin2024}
estimator, which handles both staggered timing and varying
treatment intensity.

\paragraph{Q1: Does adopting a local income tax cause residential sorting?}
Ohio municipalities can adopt a local income tax; unincorporated
townships cannot. We compare municipalities that adopted a tax
during the panel (going from 0 to a positive rate) against no-tax
townships using Equation~\ref{eq:sunab}. This is the cleanest test
of the extensive margin: a discrete switch from no tax to having one.
Only Ohio provides this variation; all Pennsylvania municipalities
levy an EIT throughout the panel.

\paragraph{Q2: Do rate increases cause additional sorting?}
Conditional on already having a tax, does raising the rate push
additional residents out? We estimate Equation~\ref{eq:sunab} with
rate increasers as treated. For Ohio, we use no-tax townships as
controls (stable-rate taxed cities produce null results, likely
because they share unobserved urban characteristics with treated
cities). For Pennsylvania, controls are the 2,209 municipalities
that never changed their EIT rate. We also estimate the DCDH
continuous treatment model to test whether rate \textit{magnitude}
matters beyond binary treatment status.

\paragraph{Q3: Are rate changes asymmetric?}
We test whether increases and decreases have symmetric effects using
a split cumulative dose specification:
%
\begin{equation}
    Y_{it} = \alpha_i + \gamma_t
    + \beta^{+} \cdot R^{+}_{it}
    + \beta^{-} \cdot R^{-}_{it}
    + \mathbf{X}'_{it}\boldsymbol{\phi} + \varepsilon_{it}
    \label{eq:split_dose}
\end{equation}
%
\noindent where $R^{+}_{it}$ and $R^{-}_{it}$ are cumulative rate
increases and decreases from baseline. If $|\beta^{+}| > |\beta^{-}|$,
residential location decisions are sticky: rate increases push
residents out, but rate decreases do not pull them back. We also
estimate Equation~\ref{eq:sunab} separately for rate decreasers
versus never-changed controls.

\paragraph{Act 32 structural break.}
Prediction 1 implies that the sorting response should differ before
and after the 2012 consolidation of tax collection. We test this by
interacting the treatment variable with pre- and post-Act 32
indicators:
%
\begin{equation}
    Y_{it} = \alpha_i + \gamma_t
    + \beta^{\text{pre}} \cdot \tau_{it} \cdot \mathbf{1}[t < 2012]
    + \beta^{\text{post}} \cdot \tau_{it} \cdot \mathbf{1}[t \geq 2012]
    + \varepsilon_{it}
    \label{eq:act32}
\end{equation}
%
Under Prediction 1, $\beta^{\text{pre}} \approx 0$ (no response when
the tax is not effectively collected) and $\beta^{\text{post}} < 0$
(sorting begins when the tax becomes binding through employer withholding).

\subsection{Stage 2: Tract-Level Decomposition}
\label{subsec:stage2}

The municipal-level outcome share\_live\_in captures the net effect
of all three adjustment channels but cannot distinguish between them.
To decompose the response, we exploit the tract-level structure of
LODES origin-destination data, which records job counts for every
$(w_{\text{tract}}, h_{\text{tract}}, \text{year})$ triplet. We
aggregate these flows to the city-pair level---$(A, B, t)$ for work
municipality $A$ and home municipality $B$---and construct three
analytic panels.

\paragraph{Q4: Do workers leave entirely, or just change where they sleep?}
\textit{(Tests Predictions 2 and 3: destination choice and complete
relocation.)}

For each municipality $B$, we construct a \textit{resident worker
panel} counting workers who both live and work in $B$. We then
measure $B$'s ``exposure'' to neighboring rate increases:
%
\begin{equation}
    \ln(\text{ResWorkers}_{Bt}) = \alpha_B + \gamma_t
    + \beta_1 \cdot \tau_{Bt}
    + \beta_2 \cdot \bar{\tau}^{\text{nbr}}_{Bt}
    + \varepsilon_{Bt}
    \label{eq:q4_relocation}
\end{equation}
%
\noindent where $\tau_{Bt}$ is $B$'s own EIT rate and
$\bar{\tau}^{\text{nbr}}_{Bt}$ is the average rate among $B$'s
neighbors (within 80~km). If $\beta_1 < 0$, higher own rates reduce
resident workers (consistent with Channels 1--3). If $\beta_2 > 0$,
cities absorb resident workers when neighbors raise rates---evidence
of Channel 2 (complete relocation toward the lower-rate city).

To test Prediction 2 (destination rates irrelevant for stayers),
we estimate a \textit{commuter pair model}:
%
\begin{equation}
    \ln(\text{Flow}_{ABt}) = \alpha_{AB} + \gamma_t
    + \beta_1 \cdot \tau^{w}_{At}
    + \beta_2 \cdot \tau^{h}_{Bt}
    + \varepsilon_{ABt}
    \label{eq:q4_pairs}
\end{equation}
%
\noindent for all commuter pairs $(A \neq B)$ within 80~km,
where $\text{Flow}_{ABt}$ is the number of workers employed in $A$
and living in $B$. Under the credit system, workers who keep their
job in $A$ pay $\max(\tau_A, \tau_B) = \tau_A$ regardless of where
they live (when $\tau_A > \tau_B$). If Prediction 2 holds,
$\beta_2 \approx 0$: the home municipality's rate does not predict
commuter flows, because it provides no tax savings.

To test Prediction 3, we add the rate gap interaction:
%
\begin{equation}
    \ln(\text{Flow}_{ABt}) = \alpha_{AB} + \gamma_t
    + \beta_1 \cdot \tau^{w}_{At}
    + \beta_2 \cdot \tau^{w}_{At} \times (\tau^{w}_{At} - \tau^{h}_{Bt})
    + \varepsilon_{ABt}
    \label{eq:q4_gap}
\end{equation}
%
If $\beta_2 > 0$, flows are larger when the rate gap between work
and home is bigger---consistent with workers choosing cheaper
destinations. If $\beta_2 \leq 0$, destination choice is
unrelated to the rate differential, ruling out tax optimization
for Channel 1 stayers.

\paragraph{Q5: Do school district rates have opposite effects?}
\textit{(Tests Prediction 4: municipal versus school district
decomposition.)}

Since each municipality in our panel has both a municipal EIT rate
($\tau^{m}_{it}$) and a school district EIT rate ($\tau^{sd}_{it}$),
and these vary independently, we estimate:
%
\begin{equation}
    Y_{it} = \alpha_i + \gamma_t
    + \beta^{m} \cdot \tau^{m}_{it}
    + \beta^{sd} \cdot \tau^{sd}_{it}
    + \varepsilon_{it}
    \label{eq:sd_decomp}
\end{equation}
%
If Prediction 4 holds, $\beta^{m} < 0$ (municipal rates push
residents out) and $\beta^{sd} > 0$ (school district rates attract
residents, reflecting the amenity value of school spending). This
decomposition explains why the \textit{total} EIT rate---municipal
plus school district---may show no net sorting effect: the two
components pull in opposite directions. We estimate
Equation~\ref{eq:sd_decomp} separately for resident workers
(people who both live and work in the municipality), nonresident
commuters (people who work in the municipality but live elsewhere),
and total employment, to identify which margin the school district
rate operates on.

We also extend the commuter pair model
(Equation~\ref{eq:q4_pairs}) to include school district rates
at both the work and home municipality:
%
\begin{equation}
    \ln(\text{Flow}_{ABt}) = \alpha_{AB} + \gamma_t
    + \beta^{m}_w \cdot \tau^{m}_{At}
    + \beta^{sd}_w \cdot \tau^{sd}_{At}
    + \beta^{m}_h \cdot \tau^{m}_{Bt}
    + \beta^{sd}_h \cdot \tau^{sd}_{Bt}
    + \varepsilon_{ABt}
    \label{eq:sd_pairs}
\end{equation}
%
If school district rates operate as a residential amenity, we expect
$\beta^{sd}_h > 0$: workers are more likely to commute \textit{from}
municipalities with higher school district rates, because people
choose to live where schools are well-funded.

\paragraph{Q6: Is sorting concentrated at municipal boundaries?}
\textit{(Tests Prediction 5: boundary gradient.)}

We build a tract-level panel within each municipality, classifying
tracts as ``boundary'' (centroid within 1~km of the nearest municipal
boundary) or ``interior.'' For each work tract, we compute the share
of workers whose home tract is in the same municipality:
%
\begin{equation}
    S^{\text{within}}_{jt} = \alpha_j + \gamma_t
    + \beta_1 \cdot \tau_{it}
    + \beta_2 \cdot \tau_{it} \times \mathbf{1}[\text{boundary}_j]
    + \varepsilon_{jt}
    \label{eq:boundary}
\end{equation}
%
\noindent where $j$ indexes work tracts within municipality $i$.
If $\beta_2 < 0$, boundary tracts are more sensitive to rate changes
than interior tracts, consistent with cross-boundary sorting where
a short residential move crosses a tax boundary. If $\beta_2
\approx 0$, the city-level sorting result is driven by a uniform
response across the municipality rather than boundary-specific
sorting. We also estimate a distance gradient, binning tracts by
distance from the nearest municipal boundary (0--0.5~km, 0.5--1~km,
1--2~km, 2--5~km, 5+~km) to trace how the sorting response decays
with distance from a tax boundary.

\paragraph{Q7: Where does the incremental revenue go, and does it matter for sorting?}
\textit{(Tests whether the salience and visibility of expenditure affect
the sorting response.)}

If residents can observe the return on their higher tax rate---more
police patrols, better roads---they may tolerate the rate increase and
stay. If the revenue funds invisible or long-horizon investments
(debt service, community development, administrative overhead), the
rate increase reduces take-home pay with no perceptible benefit,
strengthening the incentive to leave.

We test this in two steps. First, we estimate where the marginal EIT
dollar goes by regressing log expenditure in each budget category on
the municipal EIT rate:
%
\begin{equation}
    \ln(\text{Exp}^{k}_{it}) = \alpha_i + \gamma_t
    + \beta^{k} \cdot \tau_{it}
    + \varepsilon_{it}
    \label{eq:q7_decomp}
\end{equation}
%
\noindent for category $k \in \{\text{police, fire, streets, admin,
debt service, sewer, recreation, community development}\}$, with
municipality and year fixed effects. A significant $\beta^{k}$ indicates
that category $k$ absorbs a disproportionate share of the incremental
revenue from rate changes.

Second, we test whether the sorting response depends on a municipality's
pre-treatment spending allocation. For each treated municipality, we
compute the pre-treatment share of total expenditure allocated to
``visible'' services (police + fire + streets) and interact this with
the EIT rate:
%
\begin{equation}
    Y_{it} = \alpha_i + \gamma_t
    + \beta_1 \cdot \tau_{it}
    + \beta_2 \cdot \tau_{it} \times S^{\text{visible}}_{i}
    + \varepsilon_{it}
    \label{eq:q7_hetero}
\end{equation}
%
\noindent where $S^{\text{visible}}_{i}$ is municipality $i$'s
pre-treatment average share of expenditure on police, fire, and
streets. If visible spending attenuates the sorting response,
$\beta_2 > 0$ (less negative sorting in municipalities that spend
more on visible services). We also estimate split-sample models
for municipalities above and below the median visible spending share.

\paragraph{Companion outcomes.}
A decline in share\_live\_in could reflect residential sorting,
compositional change in the workforce, or differential job growth.

\subsection{Heterogeneity}
\label{subsec:heterogeneity}

A standard prediction of the tax migration literature is that
high-income workers should be more responsive to tax changes
\citep{kleven2020, young2016}. LODES provides two earnings segments
(SE01: \$1,250/month or less; SE03: \$3,333/month or more) and three
age segments (SA01: 29 or younger; SA02: 30--54; SA03: 55 or older).
We estimate the PA dose-response specification separately for each
segment.

The earnings and age segments respond nearly identically: the
dose-response coefficient is approximately $-0.009^{**}$ for both
low-earnings (SE01) and high-earnings (SE03) workers, and similar
across all three age groups. We find no evidence that higher-earning
or older workers are more responsive to local EIT rate changes.

This null heterogeneity result likely reflects the coarseness of the
LODES earnings categories. The SE03 threshold (\$40,000 per year)
captures middle-income workers, not the high earners who drive state-level
tax migration results. The local income tax rates in our sample
(0.5--3.0\%) generate modest dollar differences at these income levels:
a 1 percentage point rate increase costs a \$40,000 earner \$400
per year, unlikely to produce differential migration responses by
earnings category. True heterogeneity testing would require linked
employer-employee data with continuous earnings, which LODES does not
provide.

concentrate at municipal boundaries, suggesting it operates through
city-wide employment and residential choice rather than cross-boundary
arbitrage over short distances.

    \item \textbf{Summary:} The Act 32 structural break is consistent
    across all outcomes. Municipal EIT effects on sorting, resident

    gradient does not sharpen with enforcement, suggesting the sorting
    mechanism is city-wide relocation (Channel~2) rather than
    cross-boundary residential arbitrage (Channel~1).
\end{itemize}

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
%==========================================================

The results confirm the Tiebout hypothesis at the local level---workers
do sort across municipal boundaries in response to income tax rate
changes---but the magnitude and mechanism depart from the canonical
prediction in several ways.

\paragraph{The response is real but small.}
A 1 percentage point municipal EIT rate increase reduces the
locally-resident worker share by 0.5--0.8 points (Stage 1) and
log resident workers by 13--14\% (Stage 2). These are economically
meaningful: a typical PA municipality with 700 resident workers would
lose approximately 90--100 over 8 years. But the behavioral response
is far too small to erode the tax base. EIT revenue rises 67\% per
percentage point of rate increase, total tax revenue rises 30\%, and
population and property values are unaffected. Municipalities that
raise rates are on the correct side of the revenue hill
\citep{haughwout2004}.

\paragraph{Enforcement is the mechanism, not tax salience.}
The Act 32 structural break is the paper's strongest identification
result. Before 2012, when nonresident EIT collection was fragmented
and largely unenforced, the same statutory rate produced no detectable
sorting. After 2012, when consolidated employer withholding made the
tax binding, the effect is large and precisely estimated. This pattern
holds across all outcomes: municipal sorting, resident worker counts,
commuter pair flows, fiscal revenue, and the school district
decomposition. The implication is that the relevant margin is not
tax salience or information---workers know their tax rate---but
whether the tax is actually collected. Prior studies of local tax
competition that do not account for enforcement quality may
underestimate the behavioral response.

\paragraph{Asymmetry implies sticky residential choices.}
Rate increases push residents out; rate decreases do not bring them
back. This asymmetry is consistent across estimators, both states,
and all outcome measures. The most natural interpretation is that
residential relocation involves fixed costs (moving expenses, social
ties, school enrollment) that make the decision irreversible at the
margin. A rate increase that pushes a worker past the threshold for
moving cannot be reversed by a rate decrease of equal magnitude,
because the worker has already incurred the fixed cost and reoptimized.
This has policy implications: a municipality that raises and then
lowers its rate will not recover the residents it lost.

\paragraph{School district rates operate through a different channel.}
The opposite sign on municipal and school district EIT rates is among
the paper's most striking findings. Municipal rate increases fund
invisible community development spending (Q7a); school district rate
increases were authorized under Act 1 of 2006 specifically for
property tax relief. However, PDE fiscal data begins in 2015, after
most SD rate changes (2006--2011), so we cannot directly verify that
property taxes fell in rate-raising districts. What we can observe is
that SD rate increases are associated with lower facilities spending
($-0.73^{***}$) and lower administration ($-0.03^{**}$), consistent
with a reallocation of the budget rather than expansion. The
decomposition reveals that the SD effect operates specifically on
resident workers (people choose to \textit{live} in high-SD-rate areas)
and has no effect on nonresident commuters or total employment. Whether
the attraction reflects actual property tax relief, a school quality
signal, or unobserved amenity changes correlated with SD rate increases
remains an open question. This explains why studies using the total
EIT rate find null results: they conflate two taxes with opposite
behavioral effects.

\paragraph{Uniform response across earnings challenges selective migration.}
The tax migration literature emphasizes that high earners are more
responsive to tax differentials \citep{kleven2020, young2016}. We
find no differential response by LODES earnings category. While the
coarseness of LODES earnings segments limits the test (the highest
category captures workers earning \$40,000+, not top earners), the
uniform response is consistent with local income tax sorting operating
through a different mechanism than state-level tax migration: at the
dollar amounts involved (a few hundred dollars per year), the
relocation decision is driven by cumulative quality-of-life
considerations rather than tax optimization by high-net-worth
individuals.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
Three limitations merit acknowledgment. First, LODES employment data
are subject to noise infusion at the census block level; while
aggregation to the municipality level substantially reduces
measurement error, it introduces a mechanical tendency toward
attenuation bias. Second, our design cannot distinguish tax-motivated
sorting from sorting driven by correlated amenity changes (e.g., a
municipality raises rates and simultaneously cuts services). The
fiscal controls and pre-treatment balance tests mitigate this concern,
but we cannot rule out all confounds. Third, the LODES reference date
(April 1) captures a snapshot of employment, not the full year;
seasonal or temporary workers may introduce noise in municipalities
with volatile employment.

%==========================================================
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
%==========================================================

This paper provides causal evidence that local income tax rate changes
cause residential sorting across municipal boundaries. Using LEHD
Origin-Destination data for Ohio and Pennsylvania (2002--2019), I show
that rate increases reduce the locally-resident share of a city's
workforce, that the response is asymmetric (increases push residents
out but decreases do not bring them back), and that the sorting is
entirely concentrated after Pennsylvania's Act 32 enforcement
consolidation in 2012.

The tract-level decomposition reveals that the sorting operates through
city-wide relocation rather than cross-boundary arbitrage, that school
district EIT rates attract residents through an amenity channel that
fully offsets municipal rate repulsion, and that commuter destination
choices are unrelated to destination tax rates---exactly as the credit
system predicts. Despite the behavioral response, rate increases are
fiscally productive: revenue gains dominate the modest base erosion
from sorting.

These findings have implications for municipal fiscal policy. First,
local income tax rate increases ``work'' in the narrow fiscal sense:
they raise revenue without measurably reducing population or property
values. Second, the enforcement mechanism matters as much as the
statutory rate; municipalities considering rate increases should ensure
collection capacity. Third, the school district decomposition suggests
that not all local tax increases are alike: SD rate increases are
associated with residential attraction rather than repulsion,
though whether this reflects property tax relief, school quality
signaling, or correlated amenities cannot be fully disentangled
with the available fiscal data.

The LODES-based approach developed here---linking worker residential
choices to municipal tax variation at annual frequency---can be applied
to other local fiscal instruments (property taxes, sales taxes, fees)
and to related questions about the spatial incidence of local policy.

\section{Pre-Trend Tests on Fiscal Observables}
\label{app:pretrends}

We test the parallel trends assumption on eleven observables---six fiscal
variables from the PA DCED Annual Financial Reports, real estate market
values, total employment from LODES, Part~I index crime from the FBI
Uniform Crime Reports, and housing units permitted from the Census
Building Permits Survey---using the \citet{sunab2021} estimator.
For each outcome, we estimate Equation~\ref{eq:sunab} with municipality
and year fixed effects, clustering standard errors at the municipality
level. Table~\ref{tab:pretrends} reports the maximum absolute
$t$-statistic among the pre-treatment coefficients at relative times
$\ell \in \{-5, -4, -3, -2\}$.

Pre-trends are flat for nine of eleven observables: total revenue
(max $|t| = 1.57$), EIT revenue ($1.40$), RE tax revenue ($1.69$),
streets expenditure ($0.70$), population ($1.09$), total debt ($1.36$),
total jobs from LODES ($0.83$), index crime ($0.68$), and building
permits ($1.36$). None of the pre-treatment coefficients for these
outcomes are individually significant at the 5\% level.
The jobs, crime, and permits results are particularly important: they
confirm that treated and control municipalities were on parallel
trajectories in employment, public safety, and housing development
before the rate change, ruling out differential economic trends as
confounds.

Police expenditure is an exception. The pre-treatment coefficients at
$\ell = -5$ and $\ell = -4$ are negative and significant ($t = -2.85$ and
$t = -2.42$, respectively), indicating that treated municipalities had
relatively lower police spending 4--5 years before the rate increase.
This pattern suggests that some EIT increases may have been associated with
anticipated public-safety expansions financed by the new revenue. Importantly,
the main results on \texttt{share\_live\_in} are robust to controlling for
log police expenditure (Table~\ref{tab:robustness}), confirming that this
pre-trend does not confound the estimated sorting response.

Market value of real estate also shows a pre-trend at $\ell = -3$
($t = -2.59$), but this variable has limited coverage (pre-2012 only,
$N = 9{,}338$) and the pre-trend may reflect composition effects in the
truncated sample rather than a genuine violation of parallel trends.
