\section{Empirical Strategy}
\label{sec:strategy}
%==========================================================

Our empirical strategy proceeds in two steps. First, we estimate
difference-in-differences models comparing municipalities that changed
their tax rates to those that never changed. Because tax changes occur
at different times across municipalities, we use the Sun and Abraham
(2021) interaction-weighted estimator, which avoids the bias from
heterogeneous treatment timing that can affect standard two-way fixed
effects. We estimate rate increases and decreases separately, each
against a clean never-changed control group, and then jointly to test
for asymmetry. Second, we estimate dose-response models testing whether
the magnitude of rate changes predicts additional sorting beyond the
binary treatment effect.

\subsection{Event Study Specification}
\label{subsec:event_study}

Our baseline specification is a Sun and Abraham (2021) event study:

\begin{equation}
    Y_{it} = \alpha_i + \gamma_t + \sum_{\ell \neq -1} \mu_\ell \cdot D_{it}^{\ell} + \mathbf{X}'_{it}\boldsymbol{\phi} + \varepsilon_{it}
    \label{eq:sunab}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $Y_{it}$ is share\_live\_in for municipality $i$ in year
$t$; $\alpha_i$ and $\gamma_t$ are municipality and year fixed effects;
$D_{it}^{\ell}$ is an indicator for municipality $i$ being $\ell$ years
from its first rate change; $\mathbf{X}_{it}$ is a vector of fiscal
controls (log police expenditure, log streets expenditure, log total
debt, and log state intergovernmental aid); and $\mu_\ell$ is the
treatment effect at relative time $\ell$, estimated via the
interaction-weighted approach of Sun and Abraham (2021). We normalize
to $\ell = -1$ and cluster standard errors at the municipality level.
Pre-treatment coefficients ($\ell < -1$) test the parallel trends
assumption. Fiscal controls are available for Pennsylvania from the
DCED Annual Financial Reports; we report specifications with and
without controls.

We estimate this event study three times for each state:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Increasers vs.\ never-changed.} Treated municipalities
    are those that raised their tax rate. The control group is
    municipalities that never changed their rate. Municipalities that
    only decreased rates are excluded from the sample entirely.
    \item \textbf{Decreasers vs.\ never-changed.} The mirror test:
    treated municipalities are those that lowered their rate. Increasers
    are excluded.
    \item \textbf{Asymmetry test.} Both increase and decrease indicators
    enter the same model, with the never-changed group as the common
    control:
\end{enumerate}

\begin{equation}
    Y_{it} = \alpha_i + \gamma_t
    + \delta^{+} \cdot \text{Post}^{+}_{it}
    + \delta^{-} \cdot \text{Post}^{-}_{it}
    + \mathbf{X}'_{it}\boldsymbol{\phi} + \varepsilon_{it}
    \label{eq:asymmetry}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\text{Post}^{+}_{it} = 1$ for municipalities that
raised rates, in all years after their first increase, and analogously
$\text{Post}^{-}_{it}$ for decreases. Comparing $\delta^{+}$ and $\delta^{-}$ tests whether
sorting responses are symmetric.

\paragraph{Control groups.}
In Pennsylvania, the control group consists of 1,211 municipalities that
never changed their EIT rate during the panel. Because all PA
municipalities levy an EIT, identification comes from rate changes
among universally taxed municipalities.

In Ohio, we report results with two control groups. The first is 104
municipalities that never levied an income tax, providing a clean
extensive-margin comparison. The second is 230 municipalities that
maintained a stable tax rate (including taxed cities that never
adjusted). The no-tax control group produces cleaner pre-trends,
likely because stable-rate taxed cities share unobserved urban
characteristics with treated cities.

\subsection{Dose-Response}
\label{subsec:dose}

Among municipalities that raised rates, we test whether rate magnitude
predicts additional sorting beyond a common post-treatment shift:

\begin{equation}
    Y_{it} = \alpha_i + \gamma_t
    + \delta \cdot \text{Post}_{it}
    + \beta \cdot (\text{Rate}_{it} \times \text{Post}_{it})
    + \mathbf{X}'_{it}\boldsymbol{\phi} + \varepsilon_{it}
    \label{eq:dose}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\text{Post}_{it} = 1$ in all years after municipality
$i$'s first rate increase. The sample is restricted to municipalities
that raised rates. The coefficient $\delta$ captures common
post-treatment shifts (e.g., fiscal stress that prompted the rate
increase), while $\beta$ isolates the tax channel. If fiscal distress alone drove the result, rate
magnitude should not matter conditional on being a treated city.

To test for asymmetry in the dose-response, we decompose cumulative
rate changes from baseline on the full sample:

\begin{equation}
    Y_{it} = \alpha_i + \gamma_t
    + \beta^{+} \cdot R^{+}_{it}
    + \beta^{-} \cdot R^{-}_{it}
    + \mathbf{X}'_{it}\boldsymbol{\phi} + \varepsilon_{it}
    \label{eq:split_dose}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $R^{+}_{it} = \max(\text{rate}_{it} - \text{rate}_{i0},\; 0)$
is the cumulative rate increase from baseline (zero if rate has not risen) and
$R^{-}_{it} = |\min(\text{rate}_{it} - \text{rate}_{i0},\; 0)|$
is the absolute value of the cumulative rate decrease. If sorting is symmetric, $\beta^{+}$ and $\beta^{-}$
should be equal in magnitude with opposite signs. Asymmetry ---
specifically $|\beta^{+}| > |\beta^{-}|$ --- would indicate that
residential location decisions are sticky: tax increases push residents
out, but tax decreases do not pull them back.

\subsection{Heterogeneity-Robust Continuous Treatment Estimator}
\label{subsec:dcdh}

As an additional robustness check, we implement the de Chaisemartin and
D'Haultfoeuille (2024) estimator, which accommodates both continuous
treatments and non-absorbing treatment paths under staggered timing.
Unlike the Sun and Abraham (2021) estimator, which requires binary
absorbing treatment, the DCDH estimator uses the actual tax rate as the
treatment variable, allowing rate increases and decreases of varying
magnitudes. This directly addresses the concern that our TWFE and
Sun-Abraham specifications may suffer from heterogeneous treatment
timing contamination when the treatment is continuous.

We estimate the DCDH specification separately for rate increases and
decreases, each against never-changed controls. This mirrors the
structure of our main Sun-Abraham results
(Tables~\ref{tab:pa_binary}--\ref{tab:oh_binary_notax}) while
handling the continuous rate as treatment.

Table~\ref{tab:dcdh} reports the results. For Pennsylvania rate increases
(column 1), the DCDH average total effect is $-0.008$ (SE $0.007$),
comparable to the Sun-Abraham ATT of $-0.005$ (SE $0.003$) from
Table~\ref{tab:pa_binary}. Pre-trends are clean (joint placebo
$p = 0.90$), and the effect at $\ell = 8$ reaches $-0.011$
($p < 0.001$). The PA decrease estimate (column 2) shows a negative
average effect ($-0.008$), reinforcing the asymmetry finding: rate
decreases do not reverse sorting, consistent with sticky residential
location decisions.

Ohio continuous estimates (columns 3--4) are uninformative due to the
0--3 rate scale, which inflates standard errors. The Ohio results rely
on the binary Sun-Abraham estimator with no-tax controls
(Table~\ref{tab:oh_binary_notax}), where identification is sharper.

The consistency between the Sun-Abraham and DCDH estimates for
Pennsylvania confirms that the main results are not driven by
heterogeneous treatment timing contamination or the restriction
to binary treatment.

\section{Pre-Trend Tests on Fiscal Observables}
\label{app:pretrends}

We test the parallel trends assumption on six fiscal observables from the PA
DCED Annual Financial Reports using the Sun and Abraham (2021) estimator.
For each outcome, we estimate Equation~\ref{eq:sunab} with municipality and
year fixed effects, clustering standard errors at the municipality level.
Table~\ref{tab:pretrends} reports the maximum absolute $t$-statistic among
the pre-treatment coefficients at relative times $\ell \in \{-5, -4, -3, -2\}$.

Pre-trends are flat for five of six fiscal observables: total revenue
(max $|t| = 1.60$), EIT revenue ($1.33$), streets expenditure ($0.80$),
population ($1.09$), and total debt ($1.36$). None of the pre-treatment
coefficients for these outcomes are individually significant at the 5\% level.

Police expenditure is the exception. The pre-treatment coefficients at
$\ell = -5$ and $\ell = -4$ are negative and significant ($t = -2.85$ and
$t = -2.42$, respectively), indicating that treated municipalities had
relatively lower police spending 4--5 years before the rate increase.
This pattern suggests that some EIT increases may have been associated with
anticipated public-safety expansions financed by the new revenue. Importantly,
the main results on \texttt{share\_live\_in} are robust to controlling for
log police expenditure (Table~\ref{tab:robustness}), confirming that this
pre-trend does not confound the estimated sorting response.
