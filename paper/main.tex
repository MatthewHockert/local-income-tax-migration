\section{Empirical Strategy}
\label{sec:strategy}
%==========================================================

We organize our analysis around four sequential questions, each
building on the previous. All specifications use the Sun and Abraham
(2021) interaction-weighted estimator to handle staggered treatment
timing, with municipality and year fixed effects and standard errors
clustered at the municipality level:

\begin{equation}
    Y_{it} = \alpha_i + \gamma_t + \sum_{\ell \neq -1} \mu_\ell \cdot D_{it}^{\ell} + \mathbf{X}'_{it}\boldsymbol{\phi} + \varepsilon_{it}
    \label{eq:sunab}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $Y_{it}$ is share\_live\_in, $D_{it}^{\ell}$ indicates
municipality $i$ being $\ell$ years from its first treatment, and
$\mathbf{X}_{it}$ is a vector of fiscal controls (available for PA).
Pre-treatment coefficients ($\ell < -1$) test parallel trends.

\paragraph{Q1: Does adopting a local income tax cause sorting?}
Ohio municipalities can adopt or repeal a local income tax; unincorporated
townships have no tax. We compare the 5 municipalities that adopted a tax
during the panel to the 104 no-tax townships. This is the cleanest Tiebout
test: a binary switch from no tax to having one.

\paragraph{Q1b: Does the rate at adoption matter?}
Among adopters, we test whether higher initial rates produce more sorting
by interacting the post-adoption indicator with the rate set at adoption.

\paragraph{Q2: Does raising an existing rate cause additional sorting?}
Conditional on already having a tax, does increasing the rate push
additional residents out? For Ohio, we compare the 252 municipalities
that raised their rate to two control groups: (a) 403 stable-rate taxed
cities, and (b) 104 no-tax townships. For Pennsylvania, where all
municipalities levy an EIT, we compare the 196 rate increasers to the
2,209 municipalities that never changed.

\paragraph{Q3: Is the response symmetric?}
If rate increases push residents out, do rate decreases pull them back?
We estimate Equation~\ref{eq:sunab} with decreasers as the treated group
and never-changed as controls. We also test asymmetry jointly:

\begin{equation}
    Y_{it} = \alpha_i + \gamma_t
    + \delta^{+} \cdot \text{Post}^{+}_{it}
    + \delta^{-} \cdot \text{Post}^{-}_{it}
    + \mathbf{X}'_{it}\boldsymbol{\phi} + \varepsilon_{it}
    \label{eq:asymmetry}
\end{equation}

\noindent and with the split cumulative dose specification:

\begin{equation}
    Y_{it} = \alpha_i + \gamma_t
    + \beta^{+} \cdot R^{+}_{it}
    + \beta^{-} \cdot R^{-}_{it}
    + \mathbf{X}'_{it}\boldsymbol{\phi} + \varepsilon_{it}
    \label{eq:split_dose}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $R^{+}_{it}$ and $R^{-}_{it}$ are cumulative rate
increases and decreases from baseline. If residential location decisions
are sticky, $|\beta^{+}| > |\beta^{-}|$.

\subsection{Heterogeneity-Robust Continuous Treatment Estimator}
\label{subsec:dcdh}

As an additional robustness check, we implement the de Chaisemartin and
D'Haultfoeuille (2024) estimator, which accommodates both continuous
treatments and non-absorbing treatment paths under staggered timing.
Unlike the Sun and Abraham (2021) estimator, which requires binary
absorbing treatment, the DCDH estimator uses the actual tax rate as the
treatment variable, allowing rate increases and decreases of varying
magnitudes. This directly addresses the concern that our TWFE and
Sun-Abraham specifications may suffer from heterogeneous treatment
timing contamination when the treatment is continuous.

We estimate the DCDH specification separately for rate increases and
decreases, each against never-changed controls. This mirrors the
structure of our main Sun-Abraham results
(Tables~\ref{tab:pa_binary}--\ref{tab:oh_binary_notax}) while
handling the continuous rate as treatment.

Table~\ref{tab:dcdh} reports the results. For Pennsylvania rate increases
(column 1), the DCDH average total effect is $-0.008$ (SE $0.007$),
comparable to the Sun-Abraham ATT of $-0.005$ (SE $0.003$) from
Table~\ref{tab:pa_binary}. Pre-trends are clean (joint placebo
$p = 0.90$), and the effect at $\ell = 8$ reaches $-0.011$
($p < 0.001$). The PA decrease estimate (column 2) shows a negative
average effect ($-0.008$), reinforcing the asymmetry finding: rate
decreases do not reverse sorting, consistent with sticky residential
location decisions.

Ohio continuous estimates (columns 3--4) are uninformative due to the
0--3 rate scale, which inflates standard errors. The Ohio results rely
on the binary Sun-Abraham estimator with no-tax controls
(Table~\ref{tab:oh_binary_notax}), where identification is sharper.

The consistency between the Sun-Abraham and DCDH estimates for
Pennsylvania confirms that the main results are not driven by
heterogeneous treatment timing contamination or the restriction
to binary treatment.

\section{Pre-Trend Tests on Fiscal Observables}
\label{app:pretrends}

We test the parallel trends assumption on six fiscal observables from the PA
DCED Annual Financial Reports using the Sun and Abraham (2021) estimator.
For each outcome, we estimate Equation~\ref{eq:sunab} with municipality and
year fixed effects, clustering standard errors at the municipality level.
Table~\ref{tab:pretrends} reports the maximum absolute $t$-statistic among
the pre-treatment coefficients at relative times $\ell \in \{-5, -4, -3, -2\}$.

Pre-trends are flat for five of six fiscal observables: total revenue
(max $|t| = 1.60$), EIT revenue ($1.33$), streets expenditure ($0.80$),
population ($1.09$), and total debt ($1.36$). None of the pre-treatment
coefficients for these outcomes are individually significant at the 5\% level.

Police expenditure is the exception. The pre-treatment coefficients at
$\ell = -5$ and $\ell = -4$ are negative and significant ($t = -2.85$ and
$t = -2.42$, respectively), indicating that treated municipalities had
relatively lower police spending 4--5 years before the rate increase.
This pattern suggests that some EIT increases may have been associated with
anticipated public-safety expansions financed by the new revenue. Importantly,
the main results on \texttt{share\_live\_in} are robust to controlling for
log police expenditure (Table~\ref{tab:robustness}), confirming that this
pre-trend does not confound the estimated sorting response.
