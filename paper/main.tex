\title{When Do Local Income Taxes Cause Residential Sorting? \\
\large Evidence from Pennsylvania's Piecewise EIT System}
\author{Matthew Hockert\thanks{University of Minnesota. Email: hocke043@umn.edu}}
\date{\today}

\begin{abstract}
Do local income taxes cause residential sorting across sub-county
boundaries? I use LEHD Origin-Destination data linking each
worker's residence block to their workplace block at annual
frequency, covering approximately 2{,}500 Pennsylvania
municipalities and 500 school districts over 2002--2019.

Pennsylvania's Earned Income Tax operates a piecewise max-rule:
each worker's bill is
$T = \max(\tau^{\text{home}}, \tau^{\text{nr,work}})$, where
$\tau^{\text{home}}$ stacks the municipal and school-district
resident rates and $\tau^{\text{nr,work}}$ is the work-location
nonresident rate. A statutory rate change only moves the bill when
the changed side of the max binds. I exploit this structure with a
continuous stacked difference-in-differences design that decomposes
each rate event by direction and binding-state transition, generating
a within-design placebo: rate hikes whose post-event home stack
stays below the nonresident rate (Inc-nobind) do not change the bill
and should not move residents.

The preferred causal estimate is at the census block, where both
rate-setting jurisdictions enter at their native geography. A 1
percentage-point municipal resident-rate increase in the binding
cell reduces the log count of workers living in the block by
$-0.21$ log points (approximately $19\%$), with a clean realized-dose
pre-trend ($\beta_{\text{pre}} = -0.027$, $|t| < 1$). The Inc-nobind
within-design placebo is statistically null on the same count
outcome.

The municipality-level Inc-bind coefficient is larger ($-0.40$ log
points on residents working in their own municipality) but
pre-trended; I report it as policy-jurisdiction magnitude calibration
rather than as the causal headline. School-district rate changes do
not produce robust evidence of sorting and are treated as an
aggregation diagnostic. Industry decomposition shows consumer-facing
jobs fall in the binding cell while tradeable jobs are insulated,
consistent with a local-services demand spillover from departing
residents rather than direct nonresident-tax incidence.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
%==========================================================

Do local income taxes cause residential sorting? The Tiebout
hypothesis predicts that households ``vote with their feet'' by
sorting across jurisdictions offering different bundles of taxes and
public services \citep{tiebout1956}, and a large literature
documents tax-driven migration at the state level for high earners
\citep{kleven2020, young2016, morettiEffectStateTaxes2017} and at sub-national
borders for sales taxes \citep{agrawal2015gradient}. Yet sub-state
income-tax sorting---the canonical Tiebout margin, where moving
costs are small and tax boundaries are crossed by a short
relocation---is essentially untested in the United States. Recent
work on Indiana local income taxes
\citep{yangRESPONSIVENESSINCOMELOCAL2017} uses county-level IRS
data and finds county-level migration responses, but cannot
resolve the municipality-level variation that defines the actual tax
boundary; the canonical U.S. sub-state place-of-taxation studies
\citep{coomes2008,rohlinTaxAvoidanceBusiness2014} likewise operate
at the state-border / county level. A literature survey
by \citet{agrawalLessonsStateLocal} explicitly identifies sub-state
local-income-tax sorting as an open question, and
\citet{agrawalLocalPolicyChoice2022} characterize the empirical
evidence as ``thin.''

The gap persists for two reasons. First, the jurisdictions that
levy local income taxes are sub-county, so standard migration data
sources do not observe the correct margins: the IRS Statistics of
Income tracks county-to-county migration, and the American
Community Survey measures place-level population without linking
workers to employers. Second, local income-tax systems feature
credit mechanisms, overlapping school-district taxes, and uneven
enforcement that complicate the mapping from statutory rates to
effective tax burden. A naive comparison of rates and population
changes conflates tax incidence with amenity valuation, fiscal
capacity, and enforcement quality.

I use Pennsylvania (2002--2019) and the LEHD Origin-Destination
Employment Statistics (LODES), the only administrative data source
that links each worker's residence to their workplace at annual
frequency and at sub-municipal geography. Pennsylvania's Earned
Income Tax (EIT) is unusual along two dimensions that the empirical
design exploits. First, the EIT is set independently by two
overlapping rate-setting jurisdictions---municipalities and school
districts. Each Pennsylvania address sits in both a municipality and a
school district, and a resident's home-side tax bill is the sum of
the municipality resident rate and the school-district rate. The paper's
primary focus is the municipality resident rate; the school-district rate
enters the empirical design but is treated as a secondary,
aggregation-diagnostic channel because SD rate changes do not
recover a robust sorting response. Second, the EIT operates a
piecewise max-rule: each worker's bill is
$T = \max(\tau^{\text{home}}, \tau^{\text{nr,work}})$, where
$\tau^{\text{home}}$ is the municipal-plus-school-district stack at
the worker's residence and $\tau^{\text{nr,work}}$ is the
nonresident rate at the work location. A statutory rate change on
the home side only moves the worker's bill if the home stack is on
the binding (i.e., higher) side of the max; the work-side
nonresident rate is always paid by nonresidents, so the home-side
rate change is the marginal margin only when the home stack
exceeds it. Act 32 of 2008 (effective 2012) consolidated EIT
collection into county-based Tax Collection Districts and mandated
employer withholding at the higher of the two rates, which made
the max-rule mechanically enforced and provides an enforcement-break
heterogeneity test.

The max-rule turns a single statutory rate change into
heterogeneous predicted effects depending on the binding-state
transition. For each rate-setter side $s \in \{\text{muni},
\text{SD}\}$ I classify each rate event by direction (Increase or
Decrease) crossed with the binding-state transition of the home
stack relative to the work-side nonresident rate (bind, becomes
binding, never binds), yielding six cells per rate-setter side: the
three Inc cells (Inc-bind, Inc-becomes, Inc-nobind) and the three
Dec mirrors. The increase cells carry the identification because the
decrease cells are thin (eleven Dec-bind, four Dec-becomes-non,
fifteen Dec-nobind municipalities in total) and most contain no cities; the
discussion below focuses on the three Inc cells, with decrease
results reported alongside for completeness. In the Inc-bind cell the home stack was binding both pre and post,
so the bill rises by the full $\Delta\tau$. In the Inc-becomes cell
the home stack flips from non-binding to binding, so the bill rises
only partially (the portion above the nonresident rate). The
institutional rule's per-pp prediction is Inc-bind $>$
Inc-becomes in magnitude because the per-pp bill change is larger,
though a salience-based argument cuts the other way: Inc-becomes
workers experience a discrete switch from paying zero home-side tax
to paying a positive amount, which could trigger an outsized
behavioral response on the threshold-crossing margin. The data
favor the institutional prediction at both estimating units.
In the Inc-nobind cell the home stack stays below the nonresident
rate throughout, so the bill does not move and the predicted
response is zero. Inc-nobind is therefore a within-design placebo: if the same
statutory increase produces a response in Inc-bind and no response
in Inc-nobind, the mechanism interpretation is credible; if
Inc-nobind moves, the mechanism interpretation fails.

At the block level, a 1 percentage-point municipal resident-rate
increase in the Inc-bind cell reduces the log count of workers
living in the block by $-0.21$ log points, approximately $19\%$,
with a clean realized-dose pre-trend
($\beta_{\text{pre}} = -0.027$, $|t| < 1$). The Inc-nobind
within-design placebo is statistically null on the same count
outcome. The block-level decomposition is the preferred causal
estimate because both rate-setting jurisdictions---the municipality
and the school district---enter at their native geography and the
pre-trend is clean. The municipality-level Inc-bind coefficient is
larger in absolute value ($-0.40$ log points on the count of
residents who both live and work in the municipality, implying a
$\approx 33\%$ decline per pp rate hike), and passes the joint
Wald pre-trend test ($p = 0.23$). The block-level analogue is the
preferred causal headline because the block observes both rate-setting
jurisdictions at their native geography. I therefore report the
municipality-level estimate as policy-jurisdiction magnitude
calibration rather than as a second causal headline. School-district
rate changes do not produce robust evidence of sorting and are
treated as an aggregation diagnostic. Industry decomposition shows
that consumer-facing jobs fall in the binding cell while tradeable
jobs are insulated, consistent with a local-services demand
spillover from departing residents rather than with direct
nonresident-tax incidence on jobs.

The narrow, credible claim of the paper is that local income taxes
cause residential sorting when the institutional rule makes them
mechanically change the worker's bill---that is, in the Inc-bind
cell, and at the municipality level also in the Inc-becomes cell where the
hike flips the home stack from non-binding to binding
($\beta = -0.181^{**}$ on the count of residents who both live and
work in the municipality). The pooled Inc/Dec specification that a
conventional local-tax design would estimate is reported as a
benchmark, but it mixes binding and non-binding events. The
binding-state decomposition is the paper's contribution.

\section{Institutional Background}
\label{sec:background}
%==========================================================

Among U.S. states that authorize sub-state income taxation,
Pennsylvania has the deepest cross-section of jurisdictions setting
their own rate: approximately 2{,}500 municipalities and 500 school
districts each levy a separate Earned Income Tax (EIT) on essentially
all earned income earned in or by Pennsylvania residents
\citep{bagchiEffectsIntroducingWithholding2022,agrawalLessonsStateLocal}.
Unlike property taxes, which are paid by owners and capitalized into
home values, the EIT falls directly on workers and varies across
municipal boundaries within the same metropolitan area, creating
sharp spatial discontinuities in tax burden that can influence where
workers choose to live and work.

\subsection{Pennsylvania Earned Income Tax}
\label{subsec:pennsylvania}

Pennsylvania's EIT is mandatory for all municipalities under the
Local Tax Enabling Act of 1965 \citep[Act 511;][]{paAct511_1965}.
It applies to all earned income with no minimum threshold; retirees
are exempt. The standard combined cap is 1\%, typically split as
0.5\% municipal and 0.5\% school district. Home Rule municipalities
may exceed this cap \citep{paHomeRule}, and Philadelphia operates
separately under the Sterling Act of 1932
\citep{paSterlingAct_1932} at approximately 3.9\%. For most non-Home-Rule
boroughs and townships the EIT is the primary discretionary revenue
lever, because property-tax millage limits and reassessment lags
constrain the property side; quantifying that constraint would
require a 2002--2019 municipality-level millage panel that is not currently
available from DCED, and I leave the comparison to property-tax
sorting as a separate exercise. Across 2002--2019, the panel contains
210 municipality resident-rate increases, 119 municipality decreases, 611
school-district increases, and 52 school-district decreases; the
binding-state breakdown of these events is in
Table~\ref{tab:cell-counts}.

Each Pennsylvania municipality has three EIT rates relevant to a
worker:
%
the municipality resident rate $\tau^{\text{res,muni}}$ levied on residents of the municipality; the municipality nonresident rate $\tau^{\text{nr,muni}}$ levied on workers employed in the municipality who live elsewhere; and the school-district rate $\tau^{\text{SD}}$ levied on residents only (school districts cannot tax nonresidents).
%
Each address is associated with a six-digit Political Subdivision (PSD)
code identifying its (municipality, school district) pair. Workers report this
code on the Residency Certification Form, and employers use it to
determine withholding obligations.

Under Act 32 (effective January 1, 2012), a worker's total EIT bill
is determined by the maximum of the home-side rate stack and the
work-location nonresident rate, and the bill is split piecewise
between home and work jurisdictions:
%
\begin{equation}
\bigl(\tau^{\text{home}},\ \tau^{\text{work}}\bigr) =
\begin{cases}
\bigl(\tau^{\text{res,muni}}_j + \tau^{\text{SD}}_j - \tau^{\text{nr,muni}}_i,\ \tau^{\text{nr,muni}}_i\bigr)
   & \text{if } \tau^{\text{res,muni}}_j + \tau^{\text{SD}}_j > \tau^{\text{nr,muni}}_i \\[4pt]
\bigl(0,\ \tau^{\text{nr,muni}}_i\bigr)
   & \text{if } \tau^{\text{res,muni}}_j + \tau^{\text{SD}}_j \leq \tau^{\text{nr,muni}}_i
\end{cases}
\label{eq:piecewise}
\end{equation}
%
where $j$ is the worker's home municipality and $i$ is the work municipality. The
work jurisdiction always collects its full nonresident rate first;
the home municipality and school district then split whatever portion of the
worker's bill is left over after the work claim. If the work nonresident
rate exceeds the home stack, the home jurisdictions get nothing. The
worker's total bill is the higher of the two stacks:
%
\begin{equation}
\text{Bill}_{ji} = \max\!\bigl(\tau^{\text{res,muni}}_j + \tau^{\text{SD}}_j,\ \tau^{\text{nr,muni}}_i\bigr).
\label{eq:bill}
\end{equation}

Equation~(\ref{eq:piecewise}) implies which margin a worker can use to
respond to a rate increase. When the home stack binds
($\tau^{\text{home}}_j > \tau^{\text{nr,muni}}_i$), the worker can lower
the bill only by moving home to a lower-rate municipality or school district.
When the work nonresident rate binds, the worker can lower the bill
only by changing job to a lower-nonres-rate work location; moving home
without changing job has no effect. For workers who live and work in
the same municipality, only the home stack applies---they pay
$\tau^{\text{res,muni}}_i + \tau^{\text{SD}}_i$.

A worker who lives in municipality $j$ and moves to a lower-rate municipality $j'$
while keeping the same job at $i$ saves at least the SD rate at $j$:
the new bill is $\max(\tau^{\text{home}}_{j'}, \tau^{\text{nr}}_i)$,
which is at most the old bill $\tau^{\text{home}}_j$ minus
$\tau^{\text{SD}}_j$. Residence moves therefore do save tax under
Pennsylvania's piecewise structure, because the home-side stack
includes the SD rate, which the work jurisdiction cannot collect
from nonresidents. This savings logic is built directly into the
empirical design: the treatment $\Delta\tau^{\text{muni}}$ and the
binding-state classification are constructed from the worker's home
stack, so the regressions identify sorting in response to changes in
the same quantity a relocating worker would optimize against.

Philadelphia is the one exception to the Act 32 framework. It operates under
the Sterling Act of 1932: Philadelphia residents pay the city resident
rate regardless of work location with no offset, and Philadelphia
nonresidents pay the city nonresident rate, with no residual flowing
to home jurisdictions in other Pennsylvania municipalities. My main estimates
retain Philadelphia in the sample for completeness; results are robust
to dropping it.

Act 32 of 2008 \citep{paAct32_2008} is central to identification in this paper. Before its
2012 effective date, EIT collection was radically decentralized: each
of Pennsylvania's 2,500+ municipalities appointed its own tax officer.
Nonresident EIT enforcement was weak because no centralized system
linked employers, workers, and municipalities. A worker who moved
out of a rate-raising municipality could, in practice, avoid paying
the nonresident portion to the work municipality even though they
legally owed it. The piecewise rule described in
Equation~\ref{eq:piecewise} existed on paper but was not effectively
enforced.

Act 32 transformed this system by consolidating collection into
approximately 67 county-based Tax Collection Districts (TCDs), each with a single appointed collector.
The law mandated standardized employer withholding at the higher of
the employee's resident and work rate. This had two consequences.
First, it made the EIT \emph{mechanically binding}: rate changes now
appear in every worker's paycheck through employer withholding,
regardless of whether the worker resides in the taxing municipality.
Second, it eliminated the possibility of \emph{de facto} avoidance
of the work-location nonresident claim through weak enforcement.
After Act 32, a worker could no longer effectively duck the
nonresident portion owed to the work municipality.

Importantly, Act 32 did not eliminate all tax savings from
residential relocation. The piecewise rule in
Equation~(\ref{eq:piecewise}) continues to imply that a worker whose
original home stack binds can reduce the bill by moving to a
sufficiently lower-stack home jurisdiction: the new bill is
$\max(\tau^{\text{home}}_{j'}, \tau^{\text{nr}}_i)$, which falls
below the old home stack $\tau^{\text{home}}_j$ whenever
$\tau^{\text{home}}_{j'} < \tau^{\text{home}}_j$ and the original
home stack was the binding side. Act 32 thus changed enforcement
and made the max-rule mechanically binding through withholding; it
did not eliminate the residential-relocation margin that the
mechanism in this paper exploits.
\citet{bagchiEffectsIntroducingWithholding2022} documents the
quantitative consequences of Act 32 directly: consolidated employer
withholding increased PA EIT compliance, raised reported earned
income in the rate-raising municipality, and shifted the de facto incidence
toward workers. The present paper complements that work by isolating
the residential-sorting response to the same withholding-enforcement
regime, exploiting the cross-sectional variation in whether a given
rate change actually moves the worker's bill (the binding-state
decomposition); the pre/post-Act-32 split in
Section~\ref{sec:robustness} shows the binding-cell sorting
response strengthens after Act 32, complementing the
compliance-response finding.

School districts in Pennsylvania also levy an EIT under Act 511
\citep{paAct511_1965}, typically at 0.5\% (the other half of the
standard 1\% cap). Act 1 of 2006 \citep{paAct1_2006} (the Taxpayer
Relief Act) additionally authorized
school districts to exceed the 0.5\% cap specifically to fund
property tax relief. This generated a wave of school district
rate increases in 2006--2011, creating rate variation that is
largely independent of municipal fiscal decisions: the correlation
between municipal and school district EIT rates across my panel
is $-0.12$. Since every census tract sits in both a municipality
and a school district, workers face two overlapping EIT rates
that can change independently, providing a natural decomposition
of the residential sorting response into municipal and school
district components.

\section{Conceptual Framework: Binding-State Decomposition}
\label{sec:theory}
%==========================================================

The piecewise tax allocation in Equation~(\ref{eq:piecewise}) is the
analytical engine of the empirical design. A worker's bill is
$T = \max(\tau^{\text{home}}, \tau^{\text{nr,work}})$. A rate change
only \emph{moves the bill} when its side of the max binds. This
generates a sharp prediction: the same statutory rate change can
produce a strong behavioral response, a moderate response, or no
response at all depending on the binding state pre- and post-event.

\subsection{Six Cells per Side}
\label{subsec:cells}

For each municipality $i$ and each rate variable $\tau \in \{\tau^{\text{res,muni}},
\tau^{\text{SD}}, \tau^{\text{nr,muni}}\}$, classify the municipality's first
observed rate event by direction (Increase / Decrease) and binding-state
transition relative to the municipality's own home-stack vs nonresident rate
comparison:
%
An \emph{Inc-bind} event is a rate increase in which the home stack was binding both pre and post, so the bill rises by the full $\Delta\tau$ for in-municipality residents; this is the cell with the predicted strongest behavioral response. An \emph{Inc-becomes} event is a rate increase that flips the home stack from non-binding to binding, so the bill rises by less than the full $\Delta\tau$ (only the portion above the work nonres rate), generating an intermediate predicted response. An \emph{Inc-nobind} event is a rate increase in which the home stack stays below the nonres rate throughout, so the bill never moves; the predicted response is zero, and this cell serves as the within-design placebo.

The decrease cells mirror these definitions. A \emph{Dec-bind} event is a rate decrease in which the home stack stays binding, so the bill falls by the full $\Delta\tau$---the predicted mirror of Inc-bind under symmetry. A \emph{Dec-becomes-non} event is a rate decrease that flips the home stack from binding to non-binding, so the bill falls only down to the nonres rate. A \emph{Dec-nobind} event is a rate decrease in which the home stack stays below the nonres rate throughout, so the bill never moves; this is the second placebo cell.
%
A seventh category, NC (\emph{never-changed}), comprises municipalities whose
$\tau$ never changes across the panel. NC serves as the control pool.

The fundamental testable prediction of the institutional rule is an
ordered ranking of cell magnitudes: Inc-bind effects exceed
Inc-becomes effects, which in turn exceed Inc-nobind effects, with
the last close to zero in absolute value and serving as the
within-design placebo. The central empirical comparison of the
paper is between Inc-bind and Inc-nobind: if statutory rates matter
mechanically through tax liability, the residential response should
appear in Inc-bind and not in Inc-nobind. If Inc-nobind moves, the
mechanism interpretation fails---the response is something other
than the binding rule, because Inc-nobind events do not change the
worker's bill. This makes the design self-falsifying in a way that
a conventional pooled Inc/Dec specification is not: in the pooled
specification, every increase is a treated event, while here the
institutional rule predicts that only a subset of statutory
increases should generate a response.

\subsection{Three Treatment Margins}
\label{subsec:treatments}

Pennsylvania's institutional structure separates three rate
variables that change independently across municipality-years. The municipality
resident rate $\tau^{\text{res,muni}}$ directly affects the bill of
in-municipality residents through the home-stack channel. The school
district rate $\tau^{\text{SD}}$ also affects the home stack, but is
set by school districts rather than municipalities, so the predicted
mechanism is the same binding-rule channel as the municipality rate even
though the SD's institutional decision-making differs from the
municipality's. The municipality nonresident rate $\tau^{\text{nr,muni}}$ affects
nonresident workers commuting in, since the piecewise rule means it
moves the bill of workers from low-home-stack origins.

Two predictions follow. First, each rate's binding-cell decomposition
should show the same ranking of Inc-bind above Inc-becomes above
Inc-nobind. Second, channel separation should hold: the municipality
resident rate moves residents (live-and-work, total residents); the
municipality nonresident rate moves nonresident workers (in-commuters); and
the SD rate's effect depends on whether SD changes induce similar
residential responses.

\subsection{Data Sources}
\label{subsec:sources}
\label{subsec:sample}
\label{subsec:pipeline}

The analysis links two administrative systems that do not share
common identifiers: LEHD/LODES residence--workplace records, keyed by
Census block, and Pennsylvania EIT rate records, keyed by DCED
municipality~ID. The bridge is the TIGER~2020 block geometry, which
nests blocks within county subdivisions (cousubs) by construction. A
municipality-name crosswalk maps each DCED \texttt{municipality\_id} to its
corresponding COUSUBFP, matching $2{,}496$ of $2{,}505$ PA municipalities
($99.6\%$). The remaining $9$ are small boroughs that dissolved or
merged during 2002--2019 and are dropped from the analysis. Appendix
\ref{app:data} reports the full pipeline including scraper specs,
PSD mapping, fiscal-control construction, and auxiliary
cross-state-inflow handling.

The residence--workplace data come from LEHD LODES Version~8,
2002--2019 \citep{lehdLODES}: block-level origin--destination links
derived from state Unemployment Insurance wage records and the
Quarterly Census of Employment and Wages
\citep{abowdProducerDynamicsNew2009}. The Census Bureau applies
synthetic noise infusion at the block level to protect respondent
confidentiality, calibrated so that aggregation to tract and larger
geographies approximates ground truth
\citep{grahamDESIGNCOMPARISONLODES}. The sample period of 2002--2019
reflects LODES coverage beginning in 2002 and the need to drop
2020--2023, when COVID disrupted both commuting patterns and AFR
fiscal data through ARPA transfers.

Municipal and school-district EIT rates come from the Pennsylvania
Department of Community and Economic Development (DCED) MunStats
portal \citep{padcedMunStats}, scraped annually. Pre-2012 nonresident
rates, which DCED recorded only sporadically before Act~32 made
employer withholding mandatory, are backfilled from the earliest
observed post-2012 value per municipality. Two cleaning rules apply:
DCED reports the flat-dollar Local Services Tax in the same field
as the EIT in some years, so rates above $5\%$ are treated as
data-entry errors and dropped (the highest standard PA EIT is
$\sim$1\%; only Philadelphia exceeds $3\%$), and rate values are
aligned to April~1 of each year to match LODES's reference date.

Municipal fiscal controls come from the PA DCED Statewide Municipal
Annual Financial Report database \citep{padcedAFR}. School-district
fiscal controls come from the Census Annual Survey of School System
Finances (F-33) \citep{censusF33}; the NCES \texttt{LEAID}-to-DCED
\texttt{sd\_id} crosswalk covers all $500$ Pennsylvania districts
(see Appendix~\ref{app:data}). Two auxiliary sources support the
pre-treatment balance and robustness exercises: FBI Uniform Crime
Reports, Offenses Known and Clearances by Arrest, in the
concatenated agency-year files compiled by \citet{kaplanUCR}, and
the Census Bureau Building Permits Survey \citep{censusBPS}, matched
to PA municipalities at $93.5\%$ and $93.3\%$ rates respectively.

\subsection{Descriptive Statistics}
\label{subsec:descriptive}

The primary outcome variable for the headline causal analysis is the
block-level log count of workers living in the block. The count
directly measures the residential location of active workers and
avoids the denominator fragility of block-level shares, which are
mechanically sensitive at sparse geography because both the
numerator and denominator move. At the municipality and
school-district aggregations I also report resident-worker counts,
workplace counts, in-commuter flows, and share outcomes
(\textit{share\_live\_in}). Share outcomes at coarser geographies
retain the interpretation of earlier literature; at the block level
I treat them as supporting evidence rather than as the primary
estimand. Fiscal outcomes used as falsification placebos and
pre-treatment balance variables (log EIT revenue, log total tax
revenue, log total revenue, log police expenditure, log population,
log market value of real estate, plus crime and building permits)
are reported in Appendices~\ref{app:robustness} and
\ref{app:data-balance}.

Table~\ref{tab:summary_sd} reports the school-district aggregation.

%==========================================================
\section{Empirical Strategy}
\label{sec:strategy}
%==========================================================

The empirical framework is a continuous stacked
difference-in-differences design with binding-state cell decomposition,
estimated on a panel spanning 2002--2019 at three units of observation:
municipality, school district, and census block. Pre-2002 data are not
consistently available in LODES; post-2019 years are excluded because
COVID disrupted commuting patterns and ARPA fiscal transfers distorted
municipal budgets. Each estimating unit plays a different role. The
block-level estimate is the preferred causal specification because
both rate-setting jurisdictions enter the regression at their native
geography and the realized-dose pre-trend on the binding cell is
clean. The municipality-level estimate is retained as
policy-jurisdiction magnitude calibration, and the school-district
aggregation is used as a diagnostic. The first-difference
dose-response specification estimates the post-event effect of a one
percentage-point change in the relevant tax rate; a separate
realized-dose event-study diagnostic tests for differential pre-event
trajectories across units of varying eventual treatment intensity.

The resident-side specification includes two simultaneous rate-setter
treatments: $\Delta\tau^{\text{muni}}_{it}$, the cumulative change in
the municipality resident rate from baseline, and $\Delta\tau^{\text{SD}}_{it}$,
the cumulative change in the school-district rate. Baseline for each
is the rate at year $c_i^s - 1$, where $c_i^s$ is unit $i$'s first
event year on side $s \in \{\text{municipality, SD}\}$, and cumulative dose
accumulates with subsequent rate changes within $i$. The work-side
nonresident rate $\tau^{\text{nr}}$ enters the binding-state
classification of cells but is not itself a treatment in the headline
regression: I focus on the resident-side mechanism and defer the
work-side behavioral response to a separate analysis.

Each rate-change event is classified by direction
$\in \{\text{Inc, Dec}\}$ and binding-state transition
$\in \{\text{bind, becomes, nobind}\}$ to yield the six non-NC cells
introduced in Section~\ref{subsec:cells}. For each unit $i$ and each
rate-setter side $s$, I locate the first rate event year $c^s_i$ and
assign $i$ to one of the six cells based on the direction of the
change and the binding state of the home stack relative to
$\tau^{\text{nr,muni}}$ in the pre- and post-event years. The NC
reference category is restricted strictly to units whose rate
$\tau^s$ never changes over 2002--2019. Units with rate changes
whose first event has an undefined binding state---typically because
the municipality's nonresident rate is missing in the pre-event year due to
DCED reporting gaps before Act 32---are categorized as ``Uncertain''
and dropped: pooling them with NC would contaminate the control trend
with treated-but-unclassified units, while reporting them in their
own cell would mix true binding states with measurement gaps.
Municipality-level regressions further impose a minimum employment
threshold of $50$ total jobs. The threshold protects the share-based
outcomes (\textit{share\_live\_in}, \textit{share\_work\_outside})
from mechanical measurement noise: at $N$ workers, a single worker
moving changes the share by $1/N$, so at $N=10$ a one-worker move
changes the outcome by 10 percentage points, dominating the per-pp
treatment magnitude. At $N=50$ the one-worker noise floor is $2\%$,
roughly an order of magnitude below the headline coefficient. The
threshold also matches the LODES synthetic-noise-infusion design:
the Census Bureau's noise calibration is validated at the tract
level and above \citep{grahamDESIGNCOMPARISONLODES}, which in
Pennsylvania typically contains at least $50$ workers. Choosing
exactly $50$ rather than $20$ or $100$ is a judgment call;
sensitivity analysis in Section~\ref{sec:robustness} confirms the
headline Inc-bind coefficient is stable across thresholds from $10$
to $500$. Imposing the threshold drops $185$ of $2{,}559$ PA municipalities
($7.2\%$), almost all of which are sparsely populated rural
townships in the NC pool; the treated cohort is essentially
unaffected.

Outcomes are run in parallel across counts and shares. The
municipality-level workplace and residence outcomes are the logs of
\texttt{live\_and\_work} (residents employed in the municipality),
\texttt{in\_commuters} (workers in the municipality from elsewhere),
\texttt{out\_commuters}, \texttt{total\_jobs}, and
\texttt{total\_residents}, plus the shares of workers living in their
own municipality, of workers who are nonresidents, and of residents working
outside the municipality. The school-district aggregation uses the same outcomes computed by
summing block-level LODES counts up to the SD: each block has a
unique cousub assignment (via the TIGER 2020 block-to-cousub
crosswalk), each cousub has a primary SD assignment (the modal
\texttt{sd\_id} in the DCED rate panel; $97.6\%$ of munis are
single-SD), and the SD-level outcomes are block sums via this
two-step block~$\to$~cousub~$\to$~SD path. At the
block level, outcomes are the log count of workers living in the
block, the log count of workers working in the block, the share of
block residents working in their own municipality, and the share working
outside; geometry and aggregation details are in
Section~\ref{subsec:three-units}.

Fiscal controls are share-based and time-varying, with the exact set
keyed to the estimating unit. The shares are constructed as
numerator-over-total ratios so that they absorb compositional changes
in the municipality's or district's fiscal posture without conflating with
the level of total spending.

Municipality-level regressions use six share controls from the DCED
Annual Financial Report. Three are expenditure shares:
\texttt{share\_police} (police-services line / total expenditure),
\texttt{share\_streets} (streets and highways), and
\texttt{share\_debt\_svc} (debt service: principal and interest
payments on outstanding general-obligation and revenue debt). Three
are revenue shares: \texttt{share\_eit} (Earned Income Tax revenue
collected / total revenue), \texttt{share\_re} (real-estate property
tax revenue / total revenue), and \texttt{share\_intergov}
(intergovernmental revenue, which is grant and pass-through revenue
from state and federal governments---e.g., Liquid Fuels Tax
distributions, Community Development Block Grants, ARPA transfers---divided
by total revenue).

School-district regressions use eight share controls from the
Census Annual Survey of School System Finances (F-33)
\citep{censusF33}. Four are revenue shares:
\texttt{share\_state\_rev\_sd} (state aid: basic education subsidy,
special-education subsidy, transportation reimbursement, etc.),
\texttt{share\_local\_rev\_sd} (locally raised revenue: property tax,
EIT, occupation tax, public-utility taxes), \texttt{share\_federal\_rev\_sd}
(federal Title~I, IDEA, school-lunch, and other direct or
pass-through federal aid), and \texttt{share\_property\_tax\_sd}
(real-estate property-tax revenue as a share of total revenue,
which is a subset of \texttt{share\_local\_rev\_sd} and isolates the
SD's primary local-tax lever). Four are expenditure shares:
\texttt{share\_instructional\_sd} (teacher salaries, classroom
supplies, instructional staff), \texttt{share\_support\_sd}
(non-instructional operating costs: administration, transportation,
maintenance, food service, student services),
\texttt{share\_capital\_sd} (capital outlay---construction,
renovation, equipment), and \texttt{share\_debt\_int\_sd}
(interest payments on the district's outstanding debt; principal
appears as a capital outlay rather than as a current expense).

Block-level regressions use both sets of controls simultaneously,
since each block is exposed to both rate-setters' fiscal posture.
Figure~\ref{fig:fiscal_controls_distributions} plots the empirical
distribution of each share variable pooled across municipality-years
(municipality shares) or SD-years (SD shares); cross-unit variation is
broad on every dimension, so the share controls absorb meaningful
fiscal heterogeneity without collinearity with the rate-treatment
variable.

\subsection{Pooled Aggregate Specification}
\label{subsec:pooled-spec}

Before the binding-state decomposition I report an aggregate
specification that pools all six binding-state cells per side into
two direction tags ($\text{Inc}$ for any rate increase, $\text{Dec}$
for any rate decrease). The pooled regression is the
literature-comparable baseline: it answers ``what is the average per-pp
effect of a municipality-rate hike on residents, ignoring binding state?''
This is closest in form to the population-elasticity work of
\citet{yangRESPONSIVENESSINCOMELOCAL2017} on Indiana local income
taxes. The
binding-state decomposition that follows in
Equation~(\ref{eq:headline}) then explains which sub-population
of the treated municipality cohort drives the pooled coefficient.

For each unit-time observation $(i, t)$ on the stacked panel, define
the per-pp pooled spec
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
Y_{\tilde i \tilde t} \;=\; \alpha_{\tilde i} \;+\; \gamma_{\tilde t}
&+ \beta^{M}_{\text{Inc}}\;\Delta\tau^{M}_{it}\,\mathbf{1}\!\left\{d^{M}_{i} = \text{Inc}\right\}
\;+\; \beta^{M}_{\text{Dec}}\;\Delta\tau^{M}_{it}\,\mathbf{1}\!\left\{d^{M}_{i} = \text{Dec}\right\} \\
&+ \beta^{SD}_{\text{Inc}}\;\Delta\tau^{SD}_{it}\,\mathbf{1}\!\left\{d^{SD}_{i} = \text{Inc}\right\}
\;+\; \beta^{SD}_{\text{Dec}}\;\Delta\tau^{SD}_{it}\,\mathbf{1}\!\left\{d^{SD}_{i} = \text{Dec}\right\} \\
&+ \mathbf{X}'_{it}\boldsymbol{\phi}
\;+\; \varepsilon_{\tilde i \tilde t},
\end{split}
\label{eq:pooled}
\end{equation}
where $d^{s}_{i} \in \{\text{NC}, \text{Inc}, \text{Dec}\}$ is the
direction tag on side $s \in \{M, SD\}$ (NC is the omitted reference),
$\Delta\tau^{s}_{it}$ is the cumulative dose defined in
Section~\ref{subsec:stacked-continuous}, and the fixed effects and
clustering follow the rule in
Section~\ref{subsec:three-units}: unit-by-stack and year-by-stack
fixed effects, with clustering at the policy-jurisdiction level.

The pooled coefficient $\beta^{M}_{\text{Inc}}$ is a weighted average
of the six binding-state cell coefficients on the municipality side
(Inc-bind, Inc-becomes, Inc-nobind; with equivalent decomposition for
Dec). It is interpretable as the elasticity a prior-literature
specification would recover. The decomposition in
Section~\ref{subsec:results-block} then reveals that the pooled
coefficient is dominated by the Inc-bind cell at the block level and
attenuated toward zero in the Inc-nobind placebo cell---direct evidence
that the institutional binding rule, not the statutory rate \emph{per
se}, drives the response.

I also report a \emph{binary} analog of Equation~(\ref{eq:pooled}) in
which the four per-pp interactions are replaced by four
post-treatment indicators
$\{\mathrm{everInc}^{M}_{it}, \mathrm{everDec}^{M}_{it},
\mathrm{everInc}^{SD}_{it}, \mathrm{everDec}^{SD}_{it}\}$, each equal
to $1$ in the event year and after for the relevant direction on the
relevant side. The binary coefficients are comparable to the
event-study coefficients in Bagchi
(\citeyear{bagchiEffectsIntroducingWithholding2022}); both versions
appear together in Section~\ref{subsec:results-block}.

\subsection{Binding-State Decomposition: Stacked Continuous DiD}
\label{subsec:stacked-continuous}

The binding-state decomposition extends the pooled spec by splitting
each direction tag into its three binding-state cells, and is the
main estimator of the paper. The construction is stacked continuous
difference-in-differences. For each rate-setter side
$s \in \{\text{muni},\, \text{SD}\}$ and each cohort year $c$ at
which any municipality's first event on side $s$ occurs, I build a
sub-experiment $\mathcal{S}^s_c$ consisting of two types of units.
The treated set is the municipalities with first-event year
$c^s_i = c$ and a non-NC cell on side $s$; the control set is all
NC$^s$ municipalities (whose rate $\tau^s$ never changes over
2002--2019). The window covers calendar years $t \in [c-K,\, c+K]$
with $K=5$. The choice $K=5$ is conventional in the staggered-DiD
literature \citep[e.g.,][]{cengizEffectMinimumWages2019a} and is
calibrated against the panel:
$K=5$ yields a $5$-pre, $5$-post window that fits inside
2002--2019 with full coverage for cohorts $c \in [2007, 2014]$
(i.e., the bulk of the treated mass) and gives the
realized-dose ES four pre-event leads to test pre-trends. A
shorter $K$ would lose post-event coefficients before the cumulative
dose has fully realized; a longer $K$ would truncate the pre-period
on early cohorts and the post-period on late cohorts. Sensitivity to
$K \in \{3, 4, 6, 7\}$ leaves the headline coefficient stable to
two decimal places (not reported separately).

A concrete example helps fix ideas. Wilkes-Barre is an Act~47
distressed city with a municipality resident-rate increase from $1.5\%$ to
$2.0\%$ in 2009. Its work-side nonresident rate is $1.0\%$, so the
home stack of $(1.5 + 0.5) = 2.0\%$ pre-event already exceeds the
nonresident rate; the home stack continues to bind post-event at
$(2.0 + 0.5) = 2.5\% > 1.0\%$. Wilkes-Barre is therefore an
Inc-bind municipality with $c^M_{\text{WB}} = 2009$. The sub-experiment
$\mathcal{S}^M_{2009}$ runs on the window $[2004, 2014]$. Its
treated set includes Wilkes-Barre and any other Inc-bind /
Inc-becomes / Inc-nobind / Dec-cell municipality whose first municipality
event was 2009 ($\approx 10$ munis); its control set is the
$\sim$2{,}400 NC munis whose resident rate stayed flat across
2002--2019. Each NC municipality contributes $11$ municipality-year observations
($[2004, 2014]$) to this sub-experiment. The dose variable
$\Delta\tau^{\text{muni}}_{it} = \tau^{\text{muni}}_{it} - \tau^{\text{muni}}_{i, 2008}$
is zero for $t \leq 2008$ and equals $0.005$ for $t \geq 2009$ for
Wilkes-Barre (and analogously for other 2009 cohort munis based on
their own pre-event baseline). Relative event time is
$\ell_{it} = t - 2009$, so $\ell = -5,-4,\dots,0,1,\dots,5$.

Each municipality-year observation in $\mathcal{S}^s_c$ carries a stack
indicator $s_i = c$, relative event time $\ell_{it} = t - c$, and
stack-by-municipality and stack-by-year identifiers
$\tilde{i} = (s_i, i)$ and $\tilde{t} = (s_i, t)$ that allow each
unit to appear in the stacked panel multiple times under different
stack identifiers (this is how NC controls re-appear across cohorts
without contaminating cohort-specific identification). Stacking
across all cohort years yields a single panel in which each NC municipality
appears once per cohort and each treated municipality appears in exactly
one sub-experiment---its first-event cohort. Within each sub-experiment,
controls are clean (no treated municipality from another cohort serves as
control), which is the property that fixes the negative-weighting
problem in vanilla TWFE under staggered treatment
\citep{goodman-baconDifferenceindifferencesVariationTreatment2021}.

Figure~\ref{fig:design_timeline} sketches the construction. Each
row is one example municipality. The colored bar is its
$[c-K, c+K]$ window; the open circle marks its first-event year $c$;
the cell label on the right is the binding-state classification. The
six treated examples are one per binding-state cell. The bottom row
is the never-changed (NC) control band: every NC municipality
contributes observations to \emph{every} treated cohort's
sub-experiment, in calendar years matching that cohort's window.
Early cohorts (e.g., the 2006 Inc-becomes example) have a slightly
truncated pre-period because their $c-K$ year falls just before the
panel's 2002 start; the stacked panel handles this by simply omitting
the unobserved pre-event years.

The stacked-DiD construction is in the lineage of
\citet{cengizEffectMinimumWages2019a} (138 minimum-wage events, stacked event-by-event
with continuous treatment), \citet{fadlonFamilyLaborSupply2021b} (severe health shocks,
stacked with future-treated households as controls), and
\citet{deshpandeWhoScreenedOut2019a} (SSA field-office closings, stacked with
future-closing ZIP codes as controls). Three differences are worth
flagging.

The three designs differ first in the choice of control group. \citet{fadlonFamilyLaborSupply2021a} and
\citet{deshpandeWhoScreenedOut2019a} use \emph{future-treated} units (those that
will be treated at $\tau + \Delta$) as controls, because never-treated
units are scarce or selected in their settings (no never-dying
households; selection into closing eligibility). \citet{cengizEffectMinimumWages2019a}
uses \emph{clean controls} (states with no minimum-wage events in the
8-year window around the focal event), which permits states with
events outside the window to serve as controls. I use the strictest
specification: \emph{never-changed} (NC) municipalities, defined as those
whose rate $\tau^s$ never changes across 2002--2019. Since 95\% of PA
municipalities are NC on at least one rate side, the never-changed pool is
abundant and I do not need to relax to future-treated or
clean-within-window controls.

On the second dimension---continuous treatment---\citet{cengizEffectMinimumWages2019a} is the closest
analogue: their treatment is continuous
(log minimum wage) within each stacked sub-experiment. Their
identification logic---``aligning events by event-time, not calendar
time, equivalent to a setting where the events happen all at once''
\citep[Online Appendix C]{cengizEffectMinimumWages2019a}---applies identically in my
setup. I extend their continuous-treatment design by extracting
\emph{cell-specific} dose-response coefficients via the
$i(\text{cell}, \Delta\tau)$ interaction, which their pooled
event-by-event spec does not produce.

The third difference is the binding-state cell partitioning, which is
new relative to all three antecedents. \citet{cengizEffectMinimumWages2019a} pools all minimum-wage
events without a within-design taxonomy of treated units;
\citet{fadlonFamilyLaborSupply2021b} and \citet{deshpandeWhoScreenedOut2019a} have homogeneous
treatments (death, closure). My decomposition exploits the piecewise
PA EIT rule to partition treated municipalities into cells whose ex ante
predicted response differs by binding state, generating a
within-design placebo (Inc-nobind) that the standard stacked DiD
cannot produce.

Some treated municipalities change rates more than once in 2002--2019, raising a coding question about which event defines the cohort.
A treated municipality $i$ may have more than one rate-change event over
the panel. I classify each such municipality by the binding-state transition
of its first event and assign $i$ to the corresponding cell on
side $s$. The municipality then appears in exactly one sub-experiment
(its first-event cohort $c^s_i$), not in subsequent cohorts triggered
by later events.

Subsequent rate changes within the same municipality accumulate into
$\Delta\tau^s_{it}$, the \emph{cumulative} dose from the municipality's
pre-event baseline. Concretely: suppose municipality $i$ has $\tau^s_i = 0.50$
in years 2005--2009, $\tau^s_i = 0.70$ in 2010 (first event,
$c^s_i = 2010$, baseline $= 0.50$), and $\tau^s_i = 1.00$ from 2014
onward (second event). Then $\Delta\tau^s_{it} = 0$ for $t < 2010$,
$\Delta\tau^s_{it} = 0.20$ for $t \in [2010, 2013]$, and
$\Delta\tau^s_{it} = 0.50$ for $t \geq 2014$. The stacked-continuous
specification absorbs this trajectory through the dose-stack
interaction in a single regression on municipality $i$'s 2005--2015 window
(its first-event cohort 2010 with $K = 5$). Muni $i$ is \emph{not}
duplicated as a 2014 cohort treated municipality; it stays in its 2010
cohort. The cumulative dose path encodes the second event by raising
$\Delta\tau^s_{it}$ from $0.20$ to $0.50$ at year 2014.

This first-event classification has three deliberate consequences.
First, no treated municipality serves as control to itself; each municipality appears
in exactly one stack. Second, multi-event treated municipalities do not receive
disproportionate weight from appearing in multiple stacks. Third, the
per-pp coefficient $\beta^s_k$ is interpretable as a dose-response
elasticity over the cumulative rate change, which is the
welfare-relevant magnitude for fiscal calibration.

For each treated municipality $i$ in stack $\mathcal{S}^s_c$, define the
cumulative dose-stack as
$\Delta\tau^s_{it} = \tau^s_{it} - \tau^s_{i, c-1}$, with
$\Delta\tau^s_{it} = 0$ for $t < c$ (pre-event) and for NC municipalities at
all $t$. The stacked continuous DiD specification, for outcome $Y$,
runs the regression on the stacked panel:

\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
Y_{\tilde{i}\tilde{t}} \;=\; \alpha_{\tilde{i}} \;+\; \gamma_{\tilde{t}}
&+ \sum_{k \in \mathcal{C}_{\backslash \text{NC}}} \beta^{\text{muni}}_k \;\Delta\tau^{\text{muni}}_{it} \cdot \mathbf{1}\{\text{cell}^{\text{muni}}_i = k\} \\
&+ \sum_{k \in \mathcal{C}_{\backslash \text{NC}}} \beta^{\text{SD}}_k  \;\Delta\tau^{\text{SD}}_{it}   \cdot \mathbf{1}\{\text{cell}^{\text{SD}}_i = k\}   \\
&+ \mathbf{X}'_{it}\boldsymbol{\phi}
\;+\; \varepsilon_{\tilde{i}\tilde{t}},
\end{split}
\label{eq:headline}
\end{equation}

where
$\mathcal{C}_{\backslash \text{NC}} = \{\text{Inc-bind, Inc-becomes, Inc-nobind, Dec-bind, Dec-becomes-non, Dec-nobind}\}$ is the set of non-NC cells. NC is the omitted reference category on each side, so for each of the two rate-setter sides (municipality and SD) the regression estimates six $\beta_k$ coefficients, each interpreted as the dose-response coefficient relative to the NC trend on that side, holding the other side's cell $\times$ dose interaction fixed. The unit of observation $\tilde{i}$ in the regression is a (municipality, stack) pair: each treated municipality contributes observations in exactly one stack (its first-event cohort), while each NC municipality contributes observations in every stack. The fixed effect $\alpha_{\tilde{i}}$ is therefore a separate intercept for each (municipality, stack) combination---so a single NC municipality has a different FE in each cohort it appears in---and $\gamma_{\tilde{t}} = \gamma_{(c, t)}$ is a separate calendar-year shock per cohort. The control vector $\mathbf{X}_{it}$ holds the share-based fiscal controls described above. Standard errors are clustered at the underlying \emph{municipality}, not at the (municipality, stack) level. Cluster-at-municipality is the right choice because the same municipality's outcomes are correlated across the multiple stacks in which it appears as an NC control; the municipality-by-stack FE absorbs the level but not the residual covariance.

The cell coefficient $\beta^s_k$ is the per-pp dose-response of outcome
$Y$ to a 1 percentage-point change in rate $s$, restricted to municipalities
whose first event on side $s$ falls in cell $k$, with NC municipalities on
side $s$ as controls. Specifically, $\beta^{\text{muni}}_{\text{Inc-bind}}$
is the dose-response of $Y$ to municipality-rate hikes among municipalities whose home
stack was binding both pre- and post-event, identified by comparing
their post-event trajectory to NC municipalities' contemporaneous trajectory.
The placebo within the design is
$\beta^{\text{muni}}_{\text{Inc-nobind}}$: under the institutional rule,
this should be zero (the rate hike does not move the bill).

Within each sub-experiment, the comparison is
treated cohort vs NC controls in the same calendar-year window.
The cell decomposition partitions the treated set by whether the rate
change moved the bill. The reference for each cell coefficient is the
NC trend on the same side. The triple decomposition (municipality $\times$ SD
$\times$ nr) lets us read off, in a single regression: which rate moves
which outcome, conditional on the municipality's binding state on that side.

\subsection{Three Estimating Units}
\label{subsec:three-units}

The headline equation~(\ref{eq:headline}) is estimated at three units of
observation. The choice of unit interacts directly with how the two
rate-setting bodies (municipality and school district) enter the regression.

At the municipality level, the unit of observation is municipality $\times$ year. Workplace-panel and residence-panel
outcomes are constructed by aggregating LODES origin--destination flows
over blocks within the municipality. The municipal resident rate enters the
regression at its native level. The school district resident rate is
assigned to each municipality-year as $\max(\tau^{SD})$ across the school
districts the municipality reports to in DCED records; for the 97.6\% of municipalities
that report to a single SD this is exact, and for the 2.4\% that span
multiple SDs the max-aggregation is conservative. Fiscal share controls
are taken from the municipality AFR. Fixed effects: municipality-by-stack and year-by-stack.
Standard errors clustered at the municipality level.

At the school district level, the unit is SD $\times$ year. Outcomes are constructed by aggregating
LODES OD flows over blocks within the SD. The SD resident rate enters
at its native level. The municipal resident rate is assigned to each
SD-year as the within-SD aggregation across the SD's constituent
municipalities. Fiscal share controls are taken from Census F-33 SD-level
revenue and expenditure data, mirroring the municipality AFR shares. Fixed
effects: SD-by-stack and year-by-stack. Standard errors clustered at
the SD level.

At the block level, the unit is census block $\times$ year. Each block is assigned its own
municipality and its own SD, so both rate-setters' policy variation enters the
regression at its native level with no within-jurisdiction aggregation.
Outcomes are block-defined: log of workers living in the block, log of
workers working in the block, the share of block residents who work in
the block's own municipality, and the share of block residents who
work outside the municipality. Fiscal share controls include both municipality
AFR shares and F-33 SD shares, since each block is exposed to both
governments. Fixed effects: block-by-stack and year-by-stack.
Standard errors two-way clustered at the municipality and SD levels.

The block level is the preferred causal estimate for two reasons.
First, the block is the only level where both rate-setting
jurisdictions enter at their native geography, with no
within-jurisdiction aggregation step the reader has to defend.
Second, the realized-dose pre-trend diagnostic is cleanest at the
block level (Table~\ref{tab:es_pooled_block}): all four pre-event
coefficients are individually $p > 0.10$, with a mean pre-period
coefficient of $-0.027$. The block-level joint Wald F-test rejects
parallel trends ($p<0.001$) but the rejection is driven by the
test's enormous statistical power at $N \approx 21$M block-years
rather than by economically meaningful deviations; the
Rambachan--Roth sensitivity bound at $\bar{M}=0$ leaves the post-period
CI bounded away from zero. The municipality-level analogue on the
log count of residents working in their own municipality returns a
joint Wald F-test that does \emph{not} reject parallel trends
($p = 0.23$, stat $=5.62$ on 4 df), with three of four pre-period
coefficients individually $p>0.10$. The municipality-level diagnostic
is therefore the cleanly-non-rejecting one, while the block-level
diagnostic is hyper-powered rather than a
sharp failure, but it is enough that I treat the block-level
estimate as the causal headline and the municipality-level as
policy-jurisdiction magnitude calibration: the typical PA
municipality has $\sim$40 live-and-work workers, a meaningful
per-jurisdiction benchmark, but the noisier pre-period in the
diagnostic means I do not promote the municipality-level Inc-bind
coefficient to a second causal headline. The school-district aggregation produces
direction-mixed coefficients that do not survive the pre-trend
diagnostic and is reported as an aggregation diagnostic in the
Companion Appendix.

\subsection{Pre-Trend Diagnostics}
\label{subsec:pretrend-diagnostics}

Because the first-difference dose $\Delta\tau_{it}$ is mechanically
zero in all pre-event periods by construction, the headline
specification cannot estimate conventional pre-treatment lead
coefficients. I report two complementary event-study diagnostics
designed for this setting. The first tests \textit{strong} parallel
trends across treatment-intensity magnitudes (recommended by the
recent continuous-DiD literature for designs with a continuous dose).
The second tests \textit{average} parallel trends under a binary
cohort treatment and complements the first by capturing pre-event
mean differences regardless of dose.

\subsubsection{Realized-Dose Sun--Abraham Event Study}
\label{subsec:realized-dose-es}

To assess parallel trends in the cross-dose sense
\citep[][]{callaway2021,dechaisemartin2024}, I
estimate a separate event-study specification using each unit's
realized cumulative dose,
$\widetilde{D}_i = \tau^M_{i, c+5} - \tau^M_{i,c-1}$, which is
constant within unit and varies across units in proportion to
eventual treatment intensity. For each municipality-side cell $c$, restrict
to the subset of units in $\{$NC$, c\}$ and run
\begin{equation}
Y_{it} = \alpha_{\tilde i} + \gamma_{\tilde t}
+ \sum_{k \neq -1} \beta^M_k\, \mathbf{1}\{\ell_{it} = k\}\,
\widetilde{D}_i
+ \mathbf{X}'_{it}\boldsymbol{\phi} + \varepsilon_{it},
\label{eq:realized-dose-es}
\end{equation}
where $\ell_{it} = t - c_i^M$ is event-time relative to the unit's
first rate event. The $\beta^M_k$ at pre-event leads ($k \leq -2$)
test whether units with larger eventual rate changes were on
differentially sloped pre-event trajectories; under parallel trends
in the strong sense, these coefficients are zero. The post-event
coefficients ($\beta^M_k$ for $k \geq 0$) recover the per-pp
dose-response at each event time and average to the corresponding
cell coefficient in the headline specification when the unit's rate
path is a single step at the event year.

\subsubsection{Binary Sun--Abraham Interaction Weighted Event Study}
\label{subsec:binary-sa-es}

The realized-dose diagnostic above tests whether \textit{larger}
eventual rate changes are associated with differential pre-trends. A
complementary diagnostic asks the simpler question of whether
ever-treated units differ in mean trajectory from never-treated units,
ignoring dose magnitude. For this I use the
Sun--Abraham interaction-weighted (SA-IW) event study \citep{sunab2021}
implemented in \texttt{fixest::sunab()}:
\begin{equation}
Y_{it} = \alpha_i + \gamma_t
+ \sum_{e}\sum_{k \neq -1} \delta_{e,k}\,
\mathbf{1}\{c_i = e\}\, \mathbf{1}\{\ell_{it} = k\}
+ \mathbf{X}'_{it}\boldsymbol{\phi} + \varepsilon_{it},
\label{eq:sunab-binary}
\end{equation}
estimating a separate dynamic ATT $\delta_{e,k}$ per cohort $e$ and
event-time $k$, then interaction-weighting the $\delta_{e,k}$ across
cohorts to deliver a single cohort-robust path of pre- and post-event
coefficients. Binary cohort assignment is constructed per
binding-state cell: ever-Inc-bind municipalities are coded $\mathbf{1}$ in the
year of their first Inc-bind event and thereafter, NC municipalities are
coded $\mathbf{0}$ throughout, and other cells are excluded from the
cell-specific subset.

The two diagnostics are complementary: failure of the realized-dose
test indicates dose-magnitude selection (high-dose units were
trending differently), while failure of the binary test indicates
\emph{any} mean differential trajectory between treated and control
units. Passing both is required for clean identification under the
strong parallel-trends assumption. Block-level binary SA-IW is
unstable (thin cohort $\times$ event-time cells under stacking) and
is reported at the municipality and SD levels only.

I do not impose a uniform pre-trend standard across every outcome
in the paper, because not every outcome carries the causal claim.
The primary residential-sorting outcome is the block-level log
count of workers living in the block, and the realized-dose
pre-trend on the binding cell must be clean here for the headline
to go through; this is the outcome the paper asks the reader to
believe. Municipality-level resident counts---log residents working
in their own municipality, log employed residents---are retained as
policy-jurisdiction magnitude calibration. I acknowledge and
caveat imperfect pre-trends at the municipality scale, but these
outcomes do not carry the causal claim. Secondary equilibrium
outcomes such as log in-commuters, log total jobs, and the industry
decomposition are reported as suggestive evidence of a
local-services demand channel; imperfect pre-trends are acceptable
there because these outcomes are downstream of the
residential-sorting margin and are not the identification object.
Block-level share outcomes---the share of block residents working
in their own municipality, the share of in-block jobs filled by
out-commuters---are sensitive to sparse denominators and to
endogenous denominator dynamics at block geography, so I report
them in the Appendix and do not lean on them. The headline
block-level log home count in the Inc-bind cell passes the strong
standard; other outcomes are retained with the caveats appropriate
to their role.

\subsection{Sample Variants}
\label{subsec:sample-variants}

The DCED nonresident-rate panel has reporting gaps---55--60\% coverage
pre-Act 32, 72--80\% post---which affect the binding-state classification
when the nonresident rate is missing. I report three sample variants. The
strict variant drops municipality-years with no recorded nonresident rate. The
filled variant imputes \texttt{muni\_nonres\_eit} as the contemporaneous
resident rate when missing, which is statute-anchored: under Act 511 the
nonresident rate is bounded above by the resident rate. The
drop-incomplete variant keeps all municipality-years and drops NA only at
regression time per outcome, maximizing $N$. The headline tables use
the drop-incomplete sample; the other two are reported as robustness
columns, and coefficients are stable across them.

Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the headline binding-state
decomposition at each estimating unit, the pooled Inc/Dec literature
benchmark, and the industry-mechanism decomposition. Auxiliary
diagnostics (pre/post Act 32 split, dose-magnitude terciles,
alternative control pools, sample variants, and Rambachan--Roth
sensitivity) are in Section~\ref{sec:robustness} and
Appendix~\ref{app:robustness}.

\subsubsection{Block level (main causal evidence)}
\label{subsec:results-block}

The preferred causal estimate is the block-level municipal Inc-bind
coefficient on the log count of workers living in the block. This is
the cleanest test of the EIT mechanism for two reasons. First, both
rate-setting jurisdictions enter the regression at their native
geography---each block is exposed to a single municipality and a
single school district, so no within-jurisdiction aggregation is
required to assign policy variation. Second, the realized-dose
pre-trend on the binding cell is near zero
(Table~\ref{tab:es_pooled_block}; $\beta_{\text{pre}} = -0.027$,
$|t| < 1$). Together these features make the block-level
decomposition the level at which the paper asks the reader to
believe a causal claim.

dose-response on the municipality-rate cells, and
Table~\ref{tab:headline_block_sd_results} reports the SD-rate cells
from the same unified regression. The main residential-sorting
outcome is the log count of workers living in the block; the log

block geography because both the numerator and denominator can move

The Inc-nobind cell serves as the within-design placebo: it contains municipalities whose home

headline regression; its cell coefficients are imprecise because the
municipality nonresident rate almost never exceeds the municipality's own home stack

\subsubsection{Municipality level (calibration)}
\label{subsec:results-headline}

The municipality-level estimates are useful for interpreting

\subsubsection{School-district level (diagnostic)}
\label{subsec:results-sd}

Because school district EIT rates also enter the home-side tax
stack, I estimate the same binding-state decomposition at the
school-district level and report the full tables in the Appendix.
The SD aggregation does not produce robust evidence of residential
sorting: the SD-rate Inc-bind coefficient on the log count of
residents working in their own municipality is $+0.066^{*}$
(sign-reversed relative to both the municipality- and block-level
findings), other Inc-bind outcomes are direction-mixed, and the
realized-dose pre-trend diagnostic is weaker than in the block-level
municipal specification. I therefore treat the SD-level estimates as
an aggregation diagnostic rather than as a second causal treatment
channel. The substantive conclusion is negative: the sorting
response is concentrated in municipal resident-rate increases that
bind, not in school-district rate changes. This is what the
binding-state mechanism predicts, since municipal rate changes have
a direct, sharp identity at the home-side stack while SD averaging
mixes municipal policy environments within the same school district.
Full SD tables are deferred to the Appendix.

\subsubsection{Realized-dose ES decomposition diagnostics}
\label{subsubsec:decomp-es}

The realized-dose event study disaggregates the pooled pre-trend by
binding-state cell. Table~\ref{tab:es_block_by_cell} reports the
post/pre ATT summary for the per-cell ES at the block level;
Figure~\ref{fig:block_es_homecount} plots the same coefficients by
event-time, faceted by cell, and
Figure~\ref{fig:es_realized_muni_live_work} reports the corresponding
municipality-level picture. Two findings stand out, and they tell consistent
substantive stories even though the joint Wald test reports opposite
verdicts at the two estimating units.

\textit{Block level.} On the log count of workers living in the
block, all four pre-event coefficients are individually
indistinguishable from zero ($p > 0.10$ at $k=-5,-4,-3,-2$) and the
mean pre-period coefficient is $-0.027$. The joint Wald F-test
nonetheless rejects parallel trends (stat $=26.9$ on 4 df,
$p < 0.001$) because at $N \approx 21$M block-years the test has
extraordinary statistical power to detect economically tiny
correlated deviations across leads. Following
\citet{rambachanMoreCredibleApproach2023}, I treat the joint Wald as
a flag rather than a verdict and rely on the Rambachan--Roth
sensitivity bounds in Section~\ref{subsec:rob-honestdid}: the post-period
CI at strict parallel trends ($\bar{M}=0$) is $[-0.49, -0.12]$,
bounded comfortably away from zero. The Inc-becomes and Inc-nobind
cells fail the joint pre-trend test sharply (both $p<0.001$), which
is what the institutional rule predicts: the cohorts that should not
respond are selected on baseline trends.

\textit{Municipality level.} On the log count of residents who both
live and work in the municipality, the pre-period coefficients are
individually $p = 0.13, 0.07, 0.04, 0.58$ at $k=-5$ through $k=-2$,
and the joint Wald F-test does \emph{not} reject parallel trends
(stat $=5.62$ on 4 df, $p = 0.23$). The smaller cousub-level sample
gives the test appropriately calibrated power. Inc-becomes and
Inc-nobind again fail the joint test ($p < 0.001$ on both),
consistent with the cohort-selection reading.

At both levels the substantive conclusion is the same: the Inc-bind
cell is consistent with parallel trends under sensitivity bounds, the
Inc-becomes and Inc-nobind placebo cells reflect cohort selection on
pre-event trajectories rather than treatment-induced trends, and the
post-event Inc-bind response is the cell the binding rule predicts.

\subsection{Mechanism: Industry Decomposition}
\label{subsec:het-industry}

\subsection{Summary}
\label{subsec:results-summary}

The results follow a benchmark--decomposition--mechanism narrative.
Section~\ref{subsec:results-pooled} reports the pooled Inc/Dec
benchmark, which approximates a conventional local-tax design but
mixes binding and non-binding events.
Section~\ref{subsec:results-block} reports the preferred causal

Splitting Inc-bind units into terciles of total post-event dose and

\label{subsec:rob-controls}

Relaxing the NC-only control restriction to the

headline coefficients essentially unchanged on Inc-bind, Inc-becomes,

(\hyperref[app:rob-controls]{see Appendix~\ref{app:rob-controls}}).

\subsection{Rambachan--Roth Sensitivity to Pre-Trend Violations}
\label{subsec:rob-honestdid}

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
%==========================================================

The continuous stacked DiD design produces four substantive
findings. The block-level model is the preferred causal estimate;
the municipality-level model is the policy-jurisdiction magnitude
calibration; the school-district-level aggregation is an
aggregation diagnostic and is reported in the Companion Appendix.

The central finding is that sorting is identified through the binding channel.
The Inc-bind cell---rate hikes that move the bill---reduces
the log count of workers living in the block at the block level by approximately 19
percent per pp ($-0.21$ log points, $\text{SE} = 0.054$). The
municipality-level analogue is larger ($-0.40$ log points on
the log count of residents working in their own municipality, $\approx 33\%$ per pp). The Inc-nobind
placebo---rate hikes whose post-event home stack remains below the
nonresident rate---is statistically indistinguishable from zero on the
block-level count outcomes ($-0.056$ on the log count of workers living in the block,
$-0.113$ on the log count of workers at the block) and on the municipality-level
the log count of residents working in their own municipality ($-0.057$). Inc-becomes lies between the
two. The pattern matches the institutional rule's prediction: the
same statutory rate change can produce a strong, moderate, or null
behavioral response depending solely on whether the rate moves the
bill. This is direct evidence that workers respond to taxes they
actually pay rather than to statutory rates per se.

Block-level identification is preferred not because the joint Wald
F-test is cleaner---it is not---but because the block observes both
rate-setting jurisdictions at their native geography without a
within-jurisdiction aggregation step. The two estimating units give
opposite verdicts on the joint Wald: at the block level (N $\approx$
21M) the joint test rejects parallel trends despite pointwise-null
pre-event coefficients, reflecting hyper-precision rather than an
economically meaningful violation; at the municipality level (N $\approx$
198K) the joint test does not reject ($p=0.23$). The
\citet{rambachanMoreCredibleApproach2023} sensitivity bounds confirm
the substantive conclusion under either reading: the block-level
Inc-bind post-period CI at strict parallel trends is $[-0.49,-0.12]$,
bounded comfortably away from zero. The block-level result is the
defensible per-pp causal quantity; the municipality-level estimate
is useful for back-of-envelope welfare calibration.

The joint regression also separates the two rate-setting channels.
The municipality resident-rate Inc-bind coefficient on resident outcomes is
large and consistent at both the block and municipality levels. The
SD-resident-rate cells, estimated jointly in the same regression,
produce smaller and direction-mixed effects: $+0.177^{*}$ at the
municipality level on the log count of residents working in their own municipality, $+0.097$ on
block-level log count of workers living in the block ($p=0.12$), and $+0.066^{*}$
at the SD level (sign-reversed). The SD rate is not the dominant
driver of residential sorting through the binding channel. This is a
substantive change from an earlier draft that found a positive SD-rate
``attraction'' effect, which was an artifact of tract-centroid LODES
aggregation; under block-level LODES with the unified two-rate-setter
design, SD-rate effects on residents are small or wrong-signed.

The mechanism behind the workplace response appears to be local-services demand spillover rather than direct tax incidence on jobs.
Industry-decomposed outcomes at the municipality level show
consumer-facing jobs (retail, food, arts, other services) fall by
approximately 26 percent in the Inc-bind cell ($-0.30$ log points,
$\text{SE} = 0.15$) while tradeable jobs (manufacturing, wholesale,
finance, professional services) are insulated (point estimate
$+0.24$, statistically indistinguishable from zero). The composition
shift is captured by the share of tradeable jobs rising approximately
11 percentage points in the binding cell. The decline in total jobs
operates \emph{via} local-services demand spillover from departing
residents, not via direct nonresident-tax incidence.

The block-level Inc-bind estimate also has a direct revenue implication.
Apply the envelope-theorem decomposition of
\citet{saezUsingElasticitiesDerive2001}: for a flat local rate
$\tau$ on tax base $WN$ with residential semi-elasticity
$\beta = \partial \ln N / \partial \tau$, the first-order effect
of a small hike is
\[
  \frac{dR}{d\tau} \;=\; W \cdot N \cdot (1 + \tau\beta),
\]
a mechanical gain $WN$ minus the behavioral offset $\tau\beta$ from
residents relocating. At the PA municipality baseline $\tau \approx 1\%$
and the block-level Inc-bind estimate $\beta \approx -21$ per unit
rate ($-0.21$ log points per pp), $\tau\beta \approx -0.21$: the
residential response offsets roughly $21\%$ of the mechanical
revenue gain. The hike remains revenue-positive, paralleling the
Spanish regional finding in
\citet{agrawalRelocationRichMigration2019}.

The flat-tax revenue-maximizing rate, obtained by setting
$dR/d\tau = 0$, is $\tau^{\ast} = 1/|\beta| \approx 4.8\%$. This is
the analog, for a flat local tax with only an extensive-margin
mobility response, of the top-marginal-rate formula
$\tau^{\ast} = 1/(1 + a\varepsilon + \eta)$ in the optimal-taxation-with-mobility
literature reviewed by \citet{agrawalLessonsStateLocal}, where $a$
is the Pareto coefficient of top earners, $\varepsilon$ is the
intensive-margin elasticity of taxable income, and $\eta$ is the
extensive-margin mobility elasticity. In my flat-tax setting
neither $a$ nor $\varepsilon$ enters: there is no top tail and no
intensive labor-supply channel, only the mobility margin captured
by $\beta$. PA's statutory EIT range (roughly $0.5$--$3.4\%$) sits
entirely in the revenue-positive interior; the headline sorting
estimate is consistent with a revenue gain to the raising
municipality at the municipality boundary, with the welfare cost accruing
elsewhere through relocation and the local-services demand
spillover documented above.

Eight limitations merit acknowledgment. First, while the block-level
stacked headline on the count outcomes is large, negative, and
well-identified, the block-level share outcome
(the share of block residents working in their own municipality) is null in
the binding cell ($-0.035$, $p = 0.26$) and marginally significant
in the Inc-nobind placebo ($-0.037$, $p = 0.015$). The share outcome
at block scale appears to reflect mechanical denominator dynamics on
very small populations rather than binding-state-specific behavior,
so the share-based block evidence should be read as suggestive
rather than identified. The count-based block evidence remains the
cleanest test. Second, the school-district aggregation produces
direction-mixed coefficients and a marginally non-zero realized-dose
pre-event lead; the SD-level results should be read as confirming
the directional sign of the municipality-rate effect under a coarser
aggregation rather than as standalone identification of an
SD-channel response. Third, the LODES OD data covers PA
municipalities but not cross-state inflows in the intra-state main
file used here; the auxiliary file (cross-state inflows) is
downloaded but not yet incorporated, which would tighten the
estimates of in-commuters for border municipalities.

Fourth, the paper estimates the residential-sorting response to the
home-side resident rate but does not separately estimate the
work-side response to the municipality nonresident rate ($\tau^{\text{nr}}$).
The nonresident rate enters the design only through the
binding-state classification of cells. A complementary work-side
specification---estimating in-commuter and total-jobs responses to
$\tau^{\text{nr}}$ events at the municipality level, with a parallel
binding-state decomposition---would test whether the
local-services demand spillover I document on the residence side
(\S\ref{subsec:het-industry}) is symmetric with a workplace-side
response. I defer this to a separate paper.

Fifth, the design treats each treated municipality's stack as if
its identifying variation were independent of the rate trajectories
of its geographic neighbors. Pennsylvania municipalities are small
and dense; a worker who moves out of a rate-raising municipality often
relocates to an adjacent municipality whose own rate is also moving in some
year of the panel. The headline specification absorbs this through
never-changed-only controls and municipality-by-stack fixed effects, but it
does not formally model spatial spillovers, so the per-pp
coefficient is best read as a local displacement elasticity rather
than a net-migration one. The
\citet{butts2023DiDSpatialSpillovers} estimator generalizes stacked
DiD to allow for SUTVA violations from spatial spillovers across
treated units, using an explicit spatial weighting matrix on the
adjacency graph. Applying it to the PA cousub adjacency graph is
left for future work.

Sixth, I do not currently estimate the headline using the
heterogeneity-robust continuous-treatment difference-in-differences
estimator of \citet{deChaisemartin2022Continuous}, which generalizes
the de~Chaisemartin--D'Haultf{\oe}uille framework
\citep{deChaisemartin2020TWFE, deChaisemartin2023ImperfectPT} to
continuously distributed treatments at every period and leverages
switchers (units that move both up and down in dose) for
identification. An earlier iteration of this paper used a
prototype CDH-continuous specification at the cousub level; the
block-level analog is computationally prohibitive on the current
stacked panel ($\sim 21$M block-years $\times$ multiple cohorts) and
is left for future work. Re-estimating the headline with
\texttt{did\_multiplegt\_dyn}
\citep{deChaisemartin2025MultipleGtDyn}---once the package
documentation and switcher-based identification at this scale are
stable---would also let me exploit the rate-decrease (Dec)
cohorts, currently too thin under conventional cluster-robust
inference, as identification rather than just as a directional
mirror.

Seventh, LODES is a stock dataset rather than a person-linked
flow dataset. For each (residence block, workplace block, year), I
observe a count of workers; I do not observe individual migration
histories. The headline coefficient on the log count of workers
living in the block is therefore a \emph{net} elasticity---the
combined effect of move-outs and move-ins---rather than separate
estimates of the gross outflow and gross inflow margins. The
paper's evidence that municipal rate hikes reduce the residential
count is consistent both with workers leaving and with workers not
arriving to replace those who left; LODES alone cannot decompose
the two. Decomposing into gross outflow and gross inflow would
require person-linked administrative data: the IRS Statistics of
Income individual master file, the American Community Survey
microdata with full residence-history linkage, or---most
directly---the LEHD Job-to-Job Flows file, which tracks the same
workers across employer transitions at quarterly frequency. All
three are restricted-access products requiring approved-project
status under the Census Bureau's Federal Statistical Research Data
Centers (FSRDCs) or IRS Statistics of Income agreements; the
microdata cannot be exported and must be analyzed at a physical
FSRDC location. Replicating the present analysis on
person-linked data is a clear extension but a substantially larger
undertaking with its own access timeline.

Eighth, the paper does not estimate a comparable elasticity for
the property tax, the other major local-revenue lever in
Pennsylvania. The Oates (1969) capitalization theory predicts a
substantially smaller residential-sorting elasticity for property
tax than for the EIT, because property tax falls on owners and
capitalizes into house prices rather than on workers directly. A
Rambachan--Roth-style sensitivity bound on the property-tax
elasticity would test the falsification implication ``is the EIT
effect specifically about the EIT, or any local tax raising?'' but
requires a 2002--2019 municipality-level millage panel that DCED does not
publish (the DCED Annual Financial Report schema dropped per-municipality
millage in 2006). Constructing this panel from county assessor
records is left for future work.

%==========================================================
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
%==========================================================

When do local income taxes cause residential sorting? The
institutional answer in Pennsylvania is that they cause sorting
when the piecewise max-rule makes the statutory rate increase
change the worker's actual bill. At the census block, the level at
which both rate-setting jurisdictions enter the regression at their
native geography, a 1 percentage-point municipal resident-rate
increase in the Inc-bind cell reduces the log count of workers
living in the block by roughly $-0.21$ log points, approximately
$19\%$, with a clean realized-dose pre-trend
($\beta_{\text{pre}} = -0.027$, $|t| < 1$). The within-design
Inc-nobind placebo, where the same statutory increase does not move
the bill, is statistically null on the same count outcome. The
response is therefore tied to tax liability rather than to the
statutory rate change itself, and the binding-state decomposition
is what distinguishes the two.

Municipality-level estimates are larger ($-0.40$ log points on the
log count of residents working in their own municipality) but
pre-trended, and they enter the paper as policy-jurisdiction
magnitude calibration rather than as a second causal headline.
School-district aggregations do not robustly detect a sorting
response and are reported as a diagnostic non-result. Industry
decomposition shows that consumer-facing jobs fall in the binding
cell while tradeable jobs are insulated, suggestive of a
local-services demand spillover from departing residents rather
than direct nonresident-tax incidence on jobs.

The narrow, credible claim is that local income taxes cause
residential sorting when they bind. The same design framework---a
continuous stacked DiD with binding-state cell decomposition, a
realized-dose event study as a strong-parallel-trends diagnostic,
and a within-design placebo cell whose theoretical prediction is
zero---is portable to other settings where a piecewise tax rule
generates predictable cross-sectional variation in whether a
statutory rate change moves the worker's bill. Local sales taxes
with use-tax-credit rules, county-level income-tax credits across
state borders, and special tax districts with overlapping
jurisdictions are candidate extensions.

\paragraph{Inc-bind: the binding-cell effect.}
A 1 percentage-point increase in the municipality resident rate in the Inc-bind
cell (where the bill rises by the full $\Delta\tau$ for in-municipality residents)

the predicted effect down to roughly $0.27 \cdot 40 \cdot (e^{-0.402} - 1)

consistent with the bill rising by less than the full $\Delta\tau$ in
this cell---only the portion of the rate change above the work nonresident
rate is incident on the worker.

\section{Industry Mechanism}

\subsection{Consumer-facing vs tradeable jobs}
\label{app:industry-consumer}

The industry decomposition reported in Section~\ref{subsec:het-industry}
of the main text is the central mechanism evidence in the paper. The
prediction motivated by a local-services demand channel is that
consumer-facing jobs (retail, food services, accommodation, arts and
entertainment, other services---sectors whose demand depends on the
local resident population) contract when the municipality resident rate hikes
and residents leave, while tradeable jobs (manufacturing, wholesale,
information, finance, professional services---sectors selling to
broader markets) are insulated from the local-resident shock.

At the municipality level, the Inc-bind cell coefficient on
log consumer-facing jobs is $-0.297^{**}$ (a roughly 26 percent
contraction), while the coefficient on log tradeable jobs is
$+0.236$ and statistically indistinguishable from zero. The composition
shift is captured directly by the share of tradeable jobs, which rises
$+0.106^{*}$ percentage points per pp of municipality Inc-bind dose. The
pattern is the local-services demand signature: jobs serving residents
fall when residents leave, jobs serving the tradeable economy do not.

Figure~\ref{fig:industry_inc_bind} in Section~\ref{subsec:het-industry}
plots the Inc-bind coefficient on log jobs across NAICS supersectors
with 95\% CI bars, making the contrast visible at a glance: the
consumer-facing supersectors all sit below zero, while the
tradeable supersectors sit near zero or above with wide intervals.
The pre-trend on the consumer-facing supersectors is not as clean as
on the residence-side headline (employment outcomes share the municipality
fixed effect with the residence-side regressions but the realized-dose
ES on employment is not separately reported), and I do not claim the
employment estimates are causal in the same sense as the block-level
residential headline. The industry decomposition is reported as
suggestive mechanism evidence consistent with the residential sorting
in the headline.

\subsection{Alternative industry classifications}
\label{app:industry-alt}

The industry results above use the NAICS supersector grouping
implemented in the LODES WAC files (consumer-facing, tradeable,
health/education, public). Alternative groupings---finer NAICS
splits (e.g., separating food services from retail within
consumer-facing; separating manufacturing from professional
services within tradeable), the Mian--Sufi (2014) tradeable--non-
tradeable classification, or industry-by-firm-size cells---would
require additional auxiliary data (firm-size cells or finer NAICS in
LODES), and are left for future work. The current grouping recovers
the prediction of the local-services demand channel (consumer-facing
contracts, tradeable is insulated) without requiring those
extensions.

\subsection{Pre/post Act 32 split}
\label{app:rob-act32}

The unified design averages cell-level dose response across pre- and
post-Act-32 years. Act 32 of 2008 (effective January 1, 2012) consolidated
PA EIT collection and mandated employer withholding at the higher of the
home stack or the work nonresident rate. Before Act 32, the bill rule
existed in statute but withholding was fragmented and many municipalities did
not even publish a nonresident rate. The binding-rule mechanism in my
design only operates when the rate change actually moves a worker's bill,
which requires the rule to be enforced.

I test this by re-running the headline cell $\times$ dose specification
on subsamples defined by the cohort's first event year: pre-Act-32
cohorts ($c < 2012$) and post-Act-32 cohorts ($c \geq 2012$). The split
is run at all three estimating units. Tables below report the municipality-rate
Inc-cell coefficients pre and post.

\input{tables/act32_cousub.tex}

\input{tables/act32_sd.tex}

\input{tables/act32_block.tex}

All three estimating units use the stacked-continuous DiD with
binding-state cells, re-estimated separately on the pre-2012 and
post-2012 cohort subsamples. The municipality and SD splits use
municipality-by-stack and SD-by-stack fixed effects respectively, matching
their pooled headlines; the block split uses block-by-stack and
year-by-stack fixed effects, matching the block headline.


The binding-cell effect strengthens after enforcement consolidation.
At the municipality level, the Inc-bind coefficient on
the log count of residents working in their own municipality is $-0.339^{**}$ in the pre-2012
sub-sample and $-0.534^{***}$ in the post-2012 sub-sample---roughly
a 50\% larger response after Act 32 makes the EIT binding through
consolidated employer withholding. The same pattern holds on
the log count of in-commuters ($-0.156$ pre vs $-0.334^{***}$ post),
\texttt{log(employed residents)} ($-0.098^{**}$ vs $-0.104^{***}$),
and the share of workers living in their own municipality ($-0.047^{***}$ vs $-0.040^{***}$). The
pre-Act-32 Inc-bind effects are non-zero and statistically detectable
on most outcomes, so the binding-rule mechanism was not entirely
inert before 2012; the enforcement break amplifies the response
rather than activating it from a null baseline. The pooled headline
($-0.401^{***}$ on the log count of residents working in their own municipality; Section
\ref{subsec:results-headline}) sits between the pre and post
estimates, as expected.

The within-design placebo (Inc-nobind) is null on the headline outcomes in both periods (the log count of residents working in their own municipality
$-0.051$ pre, $-0.094$ post; the share of workers living in their own municipality $-0.013$ pre,
$-0.010$ post). The institutional rule's predicted-null cell is
satisfied regardless of the enforcement regime, which is consistent
with the binding-rule interpretation: cells where the bill never
moves should not respond in either period.

At the block level, by contrast, the municipality-cohort Inc-bind coefficient on
the log count of workers living in the block is roughly constant pre and post: the two-period magnitudes are comparable, so the
block-level effect predates the post-2012 enforcement break,
contradicting a story in which all of the binding-channel response
is driven by withholding consolidation). One reading: at fine
geography, even pre-2012 statutory rate changes were salient
enough through other channels (taxpayer reporting, local awareness)
to induce sorting. The Act-32 amplification at the municipality
scale may reflect aggregation rather than a discontinuous mechanism
change.

This pre/post pattern at the municipality level connects directly to \citet{bagchiEffectsIntroducingWithholding2022}, which documents that Act 32
increased PA EIT compliance via consolidated employer withholding.
My pre/post decomposition shows that the binding-cell residential
response also strengthens after 2012 at the municipality scale; the
two findings are complementary---compliance enforcement and behavioral
response both grow under Act 32.

\subsection{Sample variants}

The DCED nonresident-rate panel has reporting gaps that affect the

\subsection{HonestDiD sensitivity}
\label{app:rob-honestdid}
\label{app:es-honestdid}

The realized-dose ES pre-trend tests above rely on null-hypothesis
testing of pre-event coefficients. \citet{rambachanMoreCredibleApproach2023}
argue that this is the wrong diagnostic for parallel-trends because
the test has low power and conflates ``not statistically distinguishable
from zero'' with ``small enough not to matter.''
\citet{rothPretestProblemsCausal2022} shows the related point that
pre-test conditioning can paradoxically worsen inference. The
recommended alternative is sensitivity analysis: bound the bias from
non-zero pre-trends and report the threshold at which the post-event
estimate ceases to be significant.

I implement the Rambachan--Roth relative-magnitudes restriction
$\Delta^{RM}(\bar{M})$ using the \texttt{HonestDiD} package. The
restriction caps the worst pre-to-post slope deviation at $\bar{M}$
times the largest observed pre-period violation. $\bar{M} = 0$
corresponds to strict parallel trends; $\bar{M} = 1$ permits a
post-event violation as bad as the worst pre-event one; $\bar{M} = 2$
permits twice that. The reported quantity is the ``breakdown''
$\bar{M}^{*}$: the largest $\bar{M}$ at which the 95\% CI for the
average post-event Inc-bind effect still excludes zero. A breakdown
$\bar{M}^{*} \geq 1$ means the headline survives even if post-event
parallel-trends violations are as severe as the worst observed
pre-event one. The diagnostic is run on the same Inc-bind
realized-dose ES specification estimated at each unit
(\texttt{q3\_unified\_honestdid.R}).

\input{tables/honestdid_breakdown.tex}

Three findings emerge. First, the block-level log home count
headline survives strict parallel trends with the CI well bounded
away from zero: at $\bar{M} = 0$ the 95\% interval is $[-0.49, -0.12]$.
The same is true for the cousub-level log live-and-work coefficient
($[-0.73, -0.11]$ at $\bar{M}=0$) and the cousub-level share-live-in
coefficient. Second, all three of these headline-style results lose
significance between $\bar{M} = 0$ and $\bar{M} = 0.5$; the
breakdown $\bar{M}^{*}$ for each is approximately $0$. The
interpretation is that the headline is robust to strict parallel
trends but sensitive to a post-event parallel-trends violation
roughly half as large as the worst observed pre-event one. Third,
none of the SD-level Inc-bind coefficients on residence-side
outcomes is robust to even strict parallel trends---the
$\bar{M} = 0$ CIs are wide and mostly include zero (with the
exception of $\log(\text{total residents})$, which carries a
positive sign that contradicts the municipality-level pattern). The
sensitivity analysis confirms the paper's reading: the block-level
binding-cell residential response is the only specification that
delivers a tight CI under strict parallel trends, but the reader
should be aware that the headline is not robust to large pre-trend
violations.

\paragraph{Fiscal-side pre-trend placebo.}
As a complementary diagnostic to the HonestDiD sensitivity, I test
the parallel trends assumption on eleven observables---six fiscal
variables from the PA DCED Annual Financial Reports, real estate market
values, total employment from LODES, Part~I index crime from the FBI
Uniform Crime Reports, and housing units permitted from the Census
Building Permits Survey---using the \citet{sunab2021} estimator.
For each outcome, I estimate Equation~\ref{eq:sunab-binary} with municipality
and year fixed effects, clustering standard errors at the municipality
level. Table~\ref{tab:pretrends} reports the maximum absolute
$t$-statistic among the pre-treatment coefficients at relative times
$\ell \in \{-5, -4, -3, -2\}$.

Pre-trends are flat for nine of eleven observables: total revenue
(max $|t| = 1.57$), EIT revenue ($1.40$), RE tax revenue ($1.69$),
streets expenditure ($0.70$), population ($1.09$), total debt ($1.36$),
total jobs from LODES ($0.83$), index crime ($0.68$), and building
permits ($1.36$). None of the pre-treatment coefficients for these
outcomes are individually significant at the 5\% level.
The jobs, crime, and permits results are particularly important: they
confirm that treated and control municipalities were on parallel
trajectories in employment, public safety, and housing development
before the rate change, ruling out differential economic trends as
confounds.

Police expenditure is an exception. The pre-treatment coefficients at
$\ell = -5$ and $\ell = -4$ are negative and significant ($t = -2.85$ and
$t = -2.42$, respectively), indicating that treated municipalities had
relatively lower police spending 4--5 years before the rate increase.
This pattern suggests that some EIT increases may have been associated with
anticipated public-safety expansions financed by the new revenue. Importantly,
the main results on the share of workers living in their own municipality are robust to controlling for
log police expenditure (the robustness section (Section~\ref{sec:robustness})), confirming that this
pre-trend does not confound the estimated sorting response.

Market value of real estate also shows a pre-trend at $\ell = -3$
($t = -2.59$), but this variable has limited coverage (pre-2012 only,
$N = 9{,}338$) and the pre-trend may reflect composition effects in the
truncated sample rather than a genuine violation of parallel trends.

\section{Data Construction}
\label{app:data}

\subsection{DCED rate scraping}
\label{app:data-dced}

Municipal and school-district EIT rates come from the Pennsylvania
Department of Community and Economic Development (DCED) MunStats
portal \citep{padcedMunStats}. The scraper
(\texttt{scrape\_pa\_dced\_eit.py}) downloads annual XLS files from
2002 through 2023 and extracts three rate variables per municipality--year:
the municipality resident rate $\tau^{\text{res,muni}}$, the municipality
nonresident rate $\tau^{\text{nr,muni}}$, and the school-district
rate $\tau^{\text{SD}}$ (the SD rate is reported on the municipality file
because DCED tracks rates at the municipality--SD pair level). Each rate
field is parsed as a percentage and converted to a numeric in
$[0, 5]$.

Municipality identifiers in DCED use the agency's internal
\texttt{municipality\_id} (six-digit code). The crosswalk to Census
geography uses a name-based match against Census COUSUBFP (county
subdivision FIPS codes) for the 2020 vintage. Matching proceeds by
normalized name (lower-case, stripped of suffixes ``borough'',
``township'', ``city of'', etc.) and county. The match rate is
$99.6\%$; the ${\sim}10$ unmatched municipalities are smaller boroughs that
either dissolved or merged during the panel and are dropped.

DCED's nonresident-rate coverage is incomplete pre-2012: only
$55$--$60\%$ of municipalities report a nonresident rate before Act 32 made
the field a legal requirement; $72$--$80\%$ report it post-2012. I
backfill missing pre-2012 nonresident rates with the earliest
observed post-2012 value per municipality, on the assumption that a municipality's
nonresident rate is sticky over time. Sample-variant robustness
checks (\S\ref{subsec:rob-sample}) compare the headline coefficients
under strict drop-NA versus this filled-in nonresident-rate panel
and show the binding-state classification is stable.

A parallel scraper (\texttt{reparse\_dced.R}) recovers school
district name and \texttt{sd\_id} on the DCED rate files, which the
original Python scraper drops; this is needed for the NCES--DCED
school-district crosswalk used to attach Census F-33 fiscal
controls. School-district name normalization uses a Jaro--Winkler
fuzzy match with abbreviation expansion (``\textsc{co}'' $\to$
``county'', ``\textsc{sd}'' $\to$ ``school district'') and two
manual overrides for districts that changed names entirely during
the panel (\textsc{KNOCH} $\leftrightarrow$ \textsc{south butler
co}; \textsc{river valley} $\leftrightarrow$ \textsc{blairsville
saltsburg}). The final NCES \texttt{LEAID} $\to$ DCED
\texttt{sd\_id} crosswalk covers $500/500$ Pennsylvania school
districts.

\subsection{LODES construction}
\label{app:data-lodes}

Residence--workplace flows come from the LEHD Origin--Destination
Employment Statistics (LODES) Version 8
\citep{lehdLODES,abowdProducerDynamicsNew2009}, the standard
administrative source linking each employed worker's home block to
their workplace block at annual frequency. LODES is constructed
from state Unemployment Insurance wage records combined with the
Quarterly Census of Employment and Wages, with synthetic noise
infused at the block level to protect respondent confidentiality;
the noise mechanism is calibrated so that aggregation to larger
geographies recovers ground truth to within validated bounds at the
tract level and above \citep{grahamDESIGNCOMPARISONLODES}.

I download the Pennsylvania LODES OD files for 2002--2019 via the
\texttt{lehdr} R package (\texttt{download\_lodes.R}). The full
state-year file is ${\sim}200$ MB compressed; for 18 years and
roughly $5{,}000$ blocks per municipality, the raw block-pair-year panel is
roughly $4$ GB on disk. I trim 2020--2021 from the analysis sample
because COVID-19 disrupted both the underlying employment patterns
and the LODES coverage methodology in ways that would conflate the
pandemic shock with tax-driven sorting.

LODES reports a ``main job'' indicator (\texttt{JT01}) that I use to
restrict to primary-job employment relationships; secondary jobs and
federal-government employees (which LODES handles separately) are
excluded. For each (block, year) the panel records: the count of
workers living in the block (\texttt{home\_count}), the count of
workers working in the block (\texttt{work\_count}), the count of
workers both living and working in the block (and in the block's own
municipality, after aggregation), and the directional commuter flows used
in the municipality- and SD-level aggregations.

\subsection{Block-to-municipality crosswalk}
\label{app:data-xwalk}

Pennsylvania Census blocks nest in county subdivisions (cousubs) by
construction: each block has a unique \texttt{COUSUBFP} via its
\texttt{GEOID20} \citep{tigerline2020}. I build the block $\to$
cousub crosswalk by joining the 2020 TIGER/Line block shapefile
against the 2020 cousub shapefile on \texttt{STATEFP +
COUNTYFP + COUSUBFP}, yielding $\texttt{pa\_block\_xwalk.rds}$ with
exactly one cousub per block. This is the assignment used
throughout the paper to attach the LODES block-level OD flows to
the municipality-level DCED rate panel.

Earlier drafts of this paper attempted a tract-centroid join from
LODES tracts to cousubs (joining each tract centroid to the cousub
containing it) because LODES Version 7 reported tract-level OD
files. That centroid-based assignment silently dropped $42\%$ of PA
cousubs whose tract centroids fell outside the cousub polygon
(typical of small boroughs surrounded by larger townships). The
switch to block-level LODES (Version 8, 2022) plus the exact
TIGER block-to-cousub nesting eliminated this artifact and is the
basis for the block-level identification on which the paper rests.

\subsection{PSD mapping}
\label{app:data-psd}

Pennsylvania assigns each address a six-digit Political Subdivision
(PSD) code that uniquely identifies the (municipality, school district)
pair the address belongs to. Workers report the PSD code on the
Residency Certification Form, and employers use it to determine
withholding obligations after Act 32 of 2008 (effective 2012). The
PSD code is the operational identifier that ties a worker's home
address to the municipality-side and SD-side EIT rates that compose the
home stack $\tau^{\text{home}} = \tau^{\text{res,muni}} +
\tau^{\text{SD}}$ in Equation~(\ref{eq:piecewise}).

I do not observe individual workers' PSD codes; LODES is anonymized
at the block level. I instead infer the (municipality, SD) pair for each
block by combining the block-to-cousub crosswalk
(\S\ref{app:data-xwalk}) with a primary-SD assignment per municipality. The
primary SD per municipality is the modal \texttt{sd\_id} across years from
the raw DCED rate panel (most municipalities report rates to a single SD
across the full panel; $97.6\%$ of municipality-years are single-SD). For
the $2.4\%$ of municipalities that span multiple SDs in DCED records, the
cousub-level model uses $\max(\tau^{SD})$ across the municipality's SDs
(conservative, slightly biases the SD-rate coefficient toward zero);
the block-level model assigns each block its municipality's primary SD.

\subsection{Fiscal controls}
\label{app:data-fiscal}

Fiscal controls are share-based and time-varying within unit. The
exact control set varies by estimating unit, matching the unit's
fiscal authority.

\textbf{Muni-level Annual Financial Reports.} For municipality-level
regressions and the municipality side of block-level regressions, the
controls are six expenditure and revenue shares constructed from
the PA DCED Annual Financial Report database \citep{padcedAFR}:
\texttt{share\_police} (police expenditure / total expenditure),
\texttt{share\_streets}, \texttt{share\_debt\_svc},
\texttt{share\_eit} (EIT revenue / total revenue),
\texttt{share\_re} (real-estate tax revenue / total revenue), and
\texttt{share\_intergov} (intergovernmental aid / total revenue).
The DCED AFR panel covers 2002--2023; I trim to 2002--2019 to
exclude ARPA-distorted years. Coverage is essentially $100\%$ of
PA municipalities post-2006, falling to ${\sim}95\%$ in 2002--2005 due to
sparser AFR filings; missing-year imputation is not applied (the
ES regressions drop municipality-years with missing controls).

\textbf{Census F-33 school-district finance.} For SD-level
regressions and the SD side of block-level regressions, the
controls are eight share variables from the Census Annual Survey of
School System Finances (F-33) \citep{censusF33}:
\texttt{share\_state\_rev\_sd}, \texttt{share\_local\_rev\_sd},
\texttt{share\_federal\_rev\_sd}, \texttt{share\_property\_tax\_sd}
on the revenue side, and \texttt{share\_instructional\_sd},
\texttt{share\_support\_sd}, \texttt{share\_capital\_sd},
\texttt{share\_debt\_int\_sd} on the expenditure side. The F-33
panel covers $10{,}790$ Pennsylvania SD--year observations across
$500$ SDs and 2002--2019. The cross-reference between NCES F-33
\texttt{LEAID} and DCED \texttt{sd\_id} is described in
\S\ref{app:data-dced}.

\textbf{Block-level regressions} use both sets of controls
simultaneously, since each block is exposed to both its municipality's
fiscal posture (via the resident-rate channel) and its school
district's fiscal posture (via the SD-rate channel).

The block-level controls also include a within-SD aggregate of municipality
rates (\texttt{mean\_muni\_rate}) when the SD side of the regression
is being estimated; this absorbs SD-level variation in the joint
municipality resident rate that the SD itself does not control.

\subsection{Sample restrictions}
\label{app:data-sample}

The estimation sample is 2002--2019. LODES Version 8 covers
2002--2021; I drop 2020--2021 because COVID-19 disrupted both
commuting patterns (LODES coverage of stay-at-home workers is
poorly defined) and municipal finances (ARPA transfers distort
fiscal-share controls 2020--2023).

I impose four additional sample filters at estimation time. First,
a minimum-employment threshold of $50$ total jobs per municipality--year is
applied to municipality-level regressions because share outcomes are
dominated by sampling noise at smaller scales (a single worker
moving changes \texttt{share\_live\_in} by $1/N$). Sensitivity to
this threshold is documented in \S\ref{subsec:rob-sample};
coefficients are stable across thresholds from $10$ to $500$.
Second, I apply rate outlier filters: DCED reports the Local
Services Tax (a flat dollar amount) in the same field as the EIT
rate (a percentage) in some pre-2012 years, so any reported rate
exceeding $5\%$ is treated as a data-entry error and dropped (the
highest standard PA EIT is approximately $1\%$; only Philadelphia
under the Sterling Act exceeds $3\%$, and Philadelphia is handled
separately because it does not participate in the Act 32 framework
described in \S\ref{subsec:pennsylvania}). Third, for each
municipality--year I assign the rate in effect on April 1, aligning with
LODES's reference date so that rate-on-paycheck and OD-flow records
refer to the same point in time. Rates that take effect mid-year
are credited to the year following the effective date. Fourth, the
binding-state cell decomposition requires a non-missing nonresident
rate to classify the binding state at $c-1$ and $c$; municipality--years
with missing nonresident rates that have ambiguous binding states
are categorized as ``Uncertain'' and dropped from the analysis
(pooling them with NC would contaminate the control trend with
treated-but-unclassified units; reporting them in their own cell
would mix true binding states with measurement gaps; see
\S\ref{sec:strategy}).

Cluster definitions follow the rate-setting unit: municipality-level
regressions cluster at the municipality; SD-level regressions cluster at
the SD; block-level regressions two-way cluster at the municipality and
SD. The cluster-robust variance is the default
\texttt{fixest::feols} sandwich estimator with finite-sample
corrections.

\paragraph{Crosswalk match rates.}
Each step of the crosswalk pipeline is reported with its observed
match rate, allowing the reader to track how many units drop at each
join.

\begin{itemize}
\item DCED \texttt{municipality\_id} $\to$ Census 2020
  \texttt{COUSUBFP}: 99.6\% match (name-based, county-keyed,
  normalized-suffix). Unmatched municipalities are smaller boroughs that
  dissolved or merged during 2002--2019; they are dropped, not
  imputed.
\item NCES F-33 \texttt{LEAID} $\to$ DCED \texttt{sd\_id}: 500/500
  PA school districts matched (Jaro--Winkler with abbreviation
  expansion + two manual overrides for fully-renamed districts).
  This is the join that attaches Census F-33 SD finance to the DCED
  rate panel.
\item TIGER 2020 Census block $\to$ Census 2020 cousub: exact
  spatial nesting; each block has a unique \texttt{COUSUBFP} by
  construction.
\item FBI UCR agency $\to$ DCED municipality (for crime controls in
  pre-treatment balance): 93.5\% match via Jacob Kaplan's
  concatenated UCR file \citep{kaplanUCR} and
  \texttt{fips\_place\_code} keying.
\item Census Building Permits Survey place $\to$ DCED municipality: 93.3\%
  match via \texttt{fips\_mcd\_code}.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Sample-composition diagnostic.}
Of the 2{,}503 DCED-listed Pennsylvania municipalities, the panel
classifies $\sim$2{,}400 as never-changed (NC) on the municipality resident
rate side. The treated tail consists of $40$ Inc-bind, $58$
Inc-becomes, and $26$ Inc-nobind municipalities (the remainder are Dec cells
or ``Uncertain''; see Section~\ref{sec:strategy}). The treated tail
spans eight cities, ten boroughs, and twenty-two townships in the
Inc-bind cell; magnitudes of the headline coefficient are
concentrated in the cities (see Companion Appendix), consistent
with high-eventual-dose post-industrial municipalities being the
identifying variation.

\subsection{Piecewise tax examples}
\label{app:theory-piecewise}

This subsection walks through worked numerical examples of the
piecewise EIT formula in Equation~(\ref{eq:piecewise}) for the three
binding-state cells the paper exploits. Each example uses a worker
who lives in municipality $j$ and works in municipality $i$, with the standard
$1\%$ combined cap split $0.5\%$ municipality resident plus $0.5\%$
school-district.

\paragraph{Example 1: Inc-bind (the headline cell).}
Pre-event, worker lives in a municipality with
$\tau^{\text{res,muni}}_j = 0.6\%$, $\tau^{\text{SD}}_j = 0.5\%$,
and works in a municipality with $\tau^{\text{nr,muni}}_i = 0.5\%$. The
home stack is $0.6 + 0.5 = 1.1\%$; the work nonres rate is $0.5\%$;
the home stack binds. The total bill is $\max(1.1\%, 0.5\%) =
1.1\%$, split as $0.6\%$ to the home municipality and SD ($0.6\%$ home
stack $-$ $0\%$ residual to work, actually keep it simple: the home
municipality and SD get $\tau^{\text{home}} - \tau^{\text{nr}} = 1.1\% -
0.5\% = 0.6\%$; the work municipality gets $0.5\%$).

Post-event, the municipality raises its resident rate to
$\tau^{\text{res,muni}}_j = 0.9\%$. The home stack becomes $1.4\%$,
still binding ($1.4\% > 0.5\%$ work nonres). The total bill rises
to $1.4\%$, an increase of $0.3$ pp; the home jurisdictions get
$1.4 - 0.5 = 0.9\%$. A residential move to a lower-rate municipality $j'$
with $\tau^{\text{res,muni}}_{j'} = 0.5\%$ would lower the home
stack to $0.5 + 0.5 = 1.0\%$, still binding, with a new total bill
of $1.0\%$. The worker's savings from moving:
$1.4\% - 1.0\% = 0.4\%$ of earned income. This is the cell where
the institutional rule predicts the strongest sorting response.

\paragraph{Example 2: Inc-nobind (the within-design placebo).}
Pre-event, worker lives in a municipality with
$\tau^{\text{res,muni}}_j = 0.5\%$, $\tau^{\text{SD}}_j = 0.5\%$,
and works in a Home Rule city with
$\tau^{\text{nr,muni}}_i = 1.5\%$. The home stack is $1.0\%$; the
work nonres rate is $1.5\%$; the work nonres binds. Total bill is
$\max(1.0\%, 1.5\%) = 1.5\%$, with all of it going to the work
municipality. The home municipality and SD collect zero.

Post-event, the home municipality raises its resident rate to $0.7\%$. The
home stack becomes $1.2\%$, still below the $1.5\%$ work nonres
rate; the work nonres still binds. Total bill remains $1.5\%$.
The worker's bill is unchanged by the home-municipality rate hike, so the
institutional rule predicts no sorting response. The worker has no
incentive to move home in response to this rate change. This is
the placebo cell.

\paragraph{Example 3: Inc-becomes (the intermediate case).}
Pre-event setup identical to Example 2 in some respects: home stack
$1.0\%$, work nonres $1.2\%$, work nonres binds, total bill
$1.2\%$.

Post-event, the home municipality raises its resident rate to $1.0\%$,
pushing the home stack to $1.5\%$. The home stack now binds
($1.5\% > 1.2\%$ work nonres). Total bill rises to $1.5\%$, an
increase of $0.3$ pp. But unlike Inc-bind, only the portion above
the work nonres rate ($1.5 - 1.2 = 0.3\%$) accrues to the home
jurisdictions; the work municipality still collects its $1.2\%$. The
worker's bill rises by less than the full statutory $\Delta\tau =
0.5\%$ (it rises by only $0.3\%$, the amount the home stack
exceeds the work nonres rate), so the institutional rule predicts
an intermediate sorting response.

\subsection{Additional institutional details}
\label{app:theory-institutional}

Three further institutional details affect interpretation but were
deferred from the main text for space.

\paragraph{Act 1 of 2006 (school-district EIT authorization).}
The Taxpayer Relief Act of 2006 authorized school districts to
levy or raise an EIT specifically to fund property-tax reduction,
above the $0.5\%$ statutory default. This generated a wave of SD
rate increases over 2006--2011 that the paper exploits as
independent rate variation: the cross-panel correlation between
municipality and SD EIT rates is $-0.12$, so municipality and SD rate changes can
be identified separately in a joint regression.

\paragraph{Act 47 distressed municipalities.}
Approximately $15$ Pennsylvania municipalities have been designated
``financially distressed'' under Act 47 of 1987 \citep{paAct47_1987} and granted authority
to exceed the standard $0.5\%$ municipality EIT cap (typically up to $1.5\%$
or higher). These include Pittsburgh, Wilkes-Barre, Hazleton,
Johnstown, and others in the Inc-bind cell, and they account for the
right tail of the resident-rate distribution shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:rate_double_histogram}. Their inclusion in the
treated cohort is responsible for the large pre-2012 cumulative
doses observed in some Inc-bind cohorts; sensitivity to dropping
Act 47 municipalities is documented in the heterogeneity analysis in the
companion appendix.

\paragraph{Home Rule municipalities.}
Pennsylvania's Home Rule Charter and Optional Plans Law
(53 Pa.~C.S.~\S\,2961) authorizes municipalities to adopt a Home Rule
Charter and exceed the standard $0.5\%$ municipality-resident-rate cap
without an Act 47 designation. About $80$ PA municipalities have a Home Rule
Charter, of which around $20$ have used the authority to raise
their municipality EIT above the cap. These are typically older industrial
boroughs (Bethlehem, Allentown, Reading, Lancaster, York, Erie).

\subsection{Philadelphia treatment}
\label{app:theory-philly}

Philadelphia operates under the Sterling Act of 1932 (Act of August
5, 1932, P.L.~45), which predates Act 511 and creates a separate
EIT framework. Three features differ from the rest of Pennsylvania.
First, Philadelphia residents pay the city resident rate
($\sim$3.9\%) regardless of where they work, with no offset for
work-location nonresident rates. Second, Philadelphia nonresidents
working in Philadelphia pay the Philadelphia nonresident rate
(${\sim}3.5\%$), with no residual flowing to their home
jurisdiction's EIT. Third, the piecewise max-rule of Act 32 does
not apply to Philadelphia-resident or Philadelphia-employed
workers.

The main estimates retain Philadelphia in the sample because (a)
its $\sim 1.5$M residents contribute meaningful identifying
variation in the residential-sorting outcomes; (b) the cousub
crosswalk maps Philadelphia to a single COUSUBFP cleanly; and (c)
the binding-state classification still applies internally
(Philadelphia is in NC throughout the panel because its rates
change only slightly and never trigger an Inc-bind / Inc-becomes /
Inc-nobind transition). Sensitivity to dropping Philadelphia
entirely is reported in the companion appendix and shows the
headline coefficients are essentially unchanged.
