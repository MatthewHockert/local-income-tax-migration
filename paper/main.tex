\section{Institutional Background}
\label{sec:background}
%==========================================================

Local income taxes are one of the few fiscal instruments available to
municipalities in Ohio and Pennsylvania. Unlike property taxes, which
are paid by property owners and capitalized into home values, local
income taxes fall directly on workers and vary across municipal
boundaries within the same metropolitan area. This creates sharp
spatial discontinuities in tax burden that can influence where people
choose to live and work.

\subsection{Ohio Municipal Income Tax}
\label{subsec:ohio}

Ohio authorizes municipalities (cities and villages) to levy a municipal
income tax on earned income under Ohio Revised Code Chapter 718.
Unincorporated townships cannot levy the tax, creating a clean
extensive-margin contrast between taxed and untaxed jurisdictions
within the same county. As of 2019, approximately 685 municipalities
levy the tax at rates ranging from 0.5\% to 3.0\%, with 1.0\% and
1.5\% being the most common. Our panel (2002--2019) contains 302 rate
increases and 45 rate decreases.

Ohio operates a credit system: residents who work in a municipality
with a higher tax rate than their residence receive a credit against
their home municipality's tax for taxes paid to the work municipality.
However, this credit system has historically been administered at the
municipal level without centralized enforcement, and the credit is
often partial (capped at the home municipality's rate or a fraction
thereof). This means that for Ohio workers, the interaction between
home and work municipality rates determines the effective tax burden,
but imperfect crediting can leave workers paying more than the
statutory maximum of either rate alone.

\subsection{Pennsylvania Earned Income Tax}
\label{subsec:pennsylvania}

Pennsylvania's Earned Income Tax (EIT) is mandatory for all 2,500+
municipalities under the Local Tax Enabling Act (Act 511 of 1965).
The EIT applies to all earned income with no minimum threshold;
retirees are exempt. The standard cap is 1\%, typically split
0.5\% municipal and 0.5\% school district. Home Rule municipalities
may exceed this cap, with Philadelphia operating under a separate
Sterling Act authority at approximately 3.8\%. The EIT is the primary
revenue lever for most Pennsylvania municipalities: property tax
millage caps are binding in many jurisdictions, and EIT rate changes
are uncorrelated with property tax changes ($r = -0.04$). Our panel
contains 291 rate increases and 162 rate decreases.

\paragraph{The Credit System.}
A critical institutional feature is the credit mechanism governing
how EIT is allocated between a worker's municipality of residence
and municipality of employment. A worker's total EIT liability equals
the \textit{higher} of the resident rate and the work (nonresident) rate:
%
\begin{equation}
    \tau^{\text{total}}_w = \max\!\left(\tau^{\text{res}}_w,\; \tau^{\text{work}}_w\right)
    \label{eq:credit}
\end{equation}
%
If a worker lives and works in the same municipality, they simply
pay that municipality's rate. If they live in a low-rate municipality
but work in a high-rate one, the employer withholds the work rate;
the home municipality receives nothing beyond its own rate (or the
residual after the work municipality's claim). Crucially, if a worker
moves from a high-rate municipality to a lower-rate one while keeping
the same job, their \textit{total tax liability does not change}---they
continue to pay the work municipality's rate through employer
withholding, with the credit system ensuring the work municipality
collects the difference.

This has a direct implication for interpreting residential sorting.
Under the credit system, a worker who changes residence but not
workplace gains no tax savings from the move. The only way to reduce
one's EIT burden is to change both residence and workplace to a
lower-rate jurisdiction, or to move one's residence to a municipality
whose rate equals or exceeds the work rate (so the residence rate
governs and the work municipality receives nothing).

\paragraph{Act 32: Tax Collection Consolidation.}
Before 2012, EIT collection was radically decentralized: each of
Pennsylvania's 2,500+ municipalities appointed its own tax officer.
Nonresident EIT enforcement was weak because no centralized system
linked employers, workers, and municipalities. A worker who moved
out of a rate-raising municipality could, in practice, avoid paying
the nonresident portion to the work municipality even though they
legally owed it. The credit system described in Equation~\ref{eq:credit}
existed on paper but was not effectively enforced.

Act 32 of 2008 (effective January 1, 2012) transformed this system
by consolidating collection into approximately 67 county-based Tax
Collection Districts (TCDs), each with a single appointed collector.
The law mandated standardized employer withholding at the higher of
the employee's resident and work rate. This had two consequences.
First, it made the EIT \textit{binding}: rate changes now mechanically
appear in every worker's paycheck through employer withholding,
regardless of whether they reside in the taxing municipality.
Second, it eliminated the possibility of de facto tax avoidance
through residential relocation. Before Act 32, moving away from a
rate-raising city could effectively reduce a worker's tax burden
because the work municipality lacked the enforcement capacity to
collect from nonresidents. After Act 32, the credit system is
fully enforced, and moving away while keeping the same job provides
no tax relief.

\paragraph{School District EIT.}
School districts in Pennsylvania also levy an EIT under Act 511,
typically at 0.5\% (the other half of the standard 1\% cap).
Act 1 of 2006 (the Taxpayer Relief Act) additionally authorized
school districts to exceed the 0.5\% cap specifically to fund
property tax relief. This generated a wave of school district
rate increases in 2006--2011, creating rate variation that is
largely independent of municipal fiscal decisions: the correlation
between municipal and school district EIT rates across our panel
is $-0.12$. Since every census tract sits in both a municipality
and a school district, workers face two overlapping EIT rates
that can change independently, providing a natural decomposition
of the residential sorting response into municipal and school
district components.

\section{Conceptual Framework}
\label{sec:theory}
%==========================================================

We develop a framework for how local income tax changes affect the
spatial distribution of workers across municipalities. The key
institutional features described above---the credit system, Act 32
enforcement, and overlapping municipal and school district taxes---generate
distinct predictions about \textit{who} moves, \textit{where} they
go, and \textit{why}.

\subsection{Three Channels of Adjustment}
\label{subsec:channels}

Consider a municipality $A$ that raises its EIT rate. Workers
currently employed in $A$ can respond through three channels, each
with different implications for their tax burden:

\paragraph{Channel 1: Residential relocation without job change.}
A worker moves their residence from $A$ to another municipality $B$
but continues working in $A$. Under the credit system
(Equation~\ref{eq:credit}), this worker's total EIT liability is
$\max(\tau_B, \tau_A)$. If $\tau_A > \tau_B$---the typical case
when $A$ just raised its rate---the worker still pays $\tau_A$.
\textit{Moving provides zero tax savings.} This channel reduces
$A$'s share\_live\_in (our primary outcome) but does not reduce the
worker's tax burden.

\paragraph{Channel 2: Complete relocation (residence and job).}
A worker moves both residence and job from $A$ to municipality $B$,
where $\tau_B < \tau_A$. Their new liability is $\tau_B$, yielding
genuine tax savings. This is the canonical Tiebout sorting response.
This channel reduces both $A$'s resident worker count and total
employment. In our data, it appears as a decline in the
$(w\!=\!A, h\!=\!A)$ cell of the origin-destination matrix and an
increase in the $(w\!=\!B, h\!=\!B)$ cell for nearby municipalities $B$.

\paragraph{Channel 3: Compositional change in new hires.}
When $A$ creates new positions or replaces departing workers, the
new hires may choose not to live in $A$ given the higher rate. They
take the job in $A$ but live in $B$. Under the credit system, they
pay $\max(\tau_B, \tau_A) = \tau_A$, so this choice reflects
non-tax considerations (housing costs, school quality, neighborhood
preferences). This channel reduces $A$'s share\_live\_in even though
no existing resident moved.

\subsection{Predictions}
\label{subsec:predictions}

These channels generate testable predictions that differ from a
naive Tiebout model:

\paragraph{Prediction 1: Enforcement matters.}
If the sorting response is driven by workers optimizing their tax
burden (Channel 2), the effect should depend on whether the tax is
actually collected. Before Act 32, when nonresident enforcement was
weak, a rate increase was partly a paper change. After Act 32, it
mechanically reduces take-home pay. We therefore expect the sorting
response to be \textit{stronger} after 2012, not because moving
became more attractive (it became less so under full credit
enforcement), but because the rate increase itself became binding.
Workers only respond to taxes they actually pay.

\paragraph{Prediction 2: Destination rates should not matter for stayers.}
Workers who change residence but keep the same job (Channel 1) gain
no tax advantage from choosing a lower-rate destination. If the
rate gap between work and home municipality predicts commuter flows,
it reveals a non-tax channel: housing cost capitalization, amenity
perception, or fiscal quality signaling. If destination rates are
irrelevant to commuter flows, the credit system is operating as
designed and workers are not confused about their tax liability.

\paragraph{Prediction 3: Complete relocators should choose lower-rate destinations.}
Workers who change both residence and job (Channel 2) \textit{can}
reduce their tax burden by choosing a lower-rate municipality.
If cities near a rate-raising municipality experience gains in
resident workers, and those gains are larger for lower-rate
destinations, this confirms tax-motivated complete relocation.

\paragraph{Prediction 4: School district taxes should have opposite effects.}
Municipal EIT rate increases are typically associated with fiscal
stress---the municipality needs revenue. School district EIT rate
increases, particularly those authorized under Act 1 of 2006,
fund property tax relief and school spending. Higher school district
EIT rates may therefore signal a \textit{valued amenity} (better
schools, lower property taxes) rather than fiscal extraction.
If so, school district rate increases should \textit{attract}
residents, offsetting the repulsive effect of municipal rate
increases. This decomposition is possible because municipal and
school district rates vary independently ($r = -0.12$) and every
tract sits in both jurisdictions simultaneously.

\paragraph{Prediction 5: Sorting should occur at municipal boundaries.}
If EIT rate changes cause cross-boundary residential sorting,
the effect should be concentrated at the edges of municipalities
where moving a short distance crosses a tax boundary. Interior
tracts, far from any municipal boundary, should show weaker
responses. The absence of a boundary gradient would suggest that
the ``sorting'' is actually a city-wide population decline
unrelated to spatial tax arbitrage.

\section{Empirical Strategy}
\label{sec:strategy}
%==========================================================

We organize the analysis in two stages. The first stage establishes
the baseline sorting response using municipal-level outcomes, building
from the extensive margin (tax adoption) through the intensive margin
(rate changes) to asymmetry. The second stage uses tract-level
origin-destination data to decompose the sorting response into the
three channels described in Section~\ref{subsec:channels} and to
test the predictions from Section~\ref{subsec:predictions}.

All specifications include municipality (or tract, or pair) and year
fixed effects, with standard errors clustered at the municipality
level. The municipality fixed effects absorb time-invariant differences
across jurisdictions; the year fixed effects absorb aggregate shocks
common to all municipalities (e.g., business cycles, statewide policy
changes). Identification comes from within-municipality rate variation
over time.

\subsection{Stage 1: Municipal-Level Sorting}
\label{subsec:stage1}

For binary treatments under staggered timing, we use the
Sun and Abraham (2021) interaction-weighted estimator:
%
\begin{equation}
    Y_{it} = \alpha_i + \gamma_t + \sum_{\ell \neq -1} \mu_\ell \cdot D_{it}^{\ell} + \mathbf{X}'_{it}\boldsymbol{\phi} + \varepsilon_{it}
    \label{eq:sunab}
\end{equation}
%
\noindent where $Y_{it}$ is share\_live\_in for municipality $i$ in
year $t$, $D_{it}^{\ell}$ indicates municipality $i$ being $\ell$
years from its first treatment, and $\mathbf{X}_{it}$ is a vector
of fiscal controls (police expenditures, streets, debt service, and
state aid; available for PA). Pre-treatment coefficients
($\ell < -1$) test parallel trends. For continuous treatments (the
actual tax rate), we use the de Chaisemartin and D'Haultfoeuille
(2024) estimator, which handles both staggered timing and varying
treatment intensity.

\paragraph{Q1: Does adopting a local income tax cause residential sorting?}
Ohio municipalities can adopt a local income tax; unincorporated
townships cannot. We compare municipalities that adopted a tax
during the panel (going from 0 to a positive rate) against no-tax
townships using Equation~\ref{eq:sunab}. This is the cleanest test
of the extensive margin: a discrete switch from no tax to having one.
Only Ohio provides this variation; all Pennsylvania municipalities
levy an EIT throughout the panel.

\paragraph{Q2: Do rate increases cause additional sorting?}
Conditional on already having a tax, does raising the rate push
additional residents out? We estimate Equation~\ref{eq:sunab} with
rate increasers as treated. For Ohio, we use no-tax townships as
controls (stable-rate taxed cities produce null results, likely
because they share unobserved urban characteristics with treated
cities). For Pennsylvania, controls are the 2,209 municipalities
that never changed their EIT rate. We also estimate the DCDH
continuous treatment model to test whether rate \textit{magnitude}
matters beyond binary treatment status.

\paragraph{Q3: Are rate changes asymmetric?}
We test whether increases and decreases have symmetric effects using
a split cumulative dose specification:
%
\begin{equation}
    Y_{it} = \alpha_i + \gamma_t
    + \beta^{+} \cdot R^{+}_{it}
    + \beta^{-} \cdot R^{-}_{it}
    + \mathbf{X}'_{it}\boldsymbol{\phi} + \varepsilon_{it}
    \label{eq:split_dose}
\end{equation}
%
\noindent where $R^{+}_{it}$ and $R^{-}_{it}$ are cumulative rate
increases and decreases from baseline. If $|\beta^{+}| > |\beta^{-}|$,
residential location decisions are sticky: rate increases push
residents out, but rate decreases do not pull them back. We also
estimate Equation~\ref{eq:sunab} separately for rate decreasers
versus never-changed controls.

\paragraph{Act 32 structural break.}
Prediction 1 implies that the sorting response should differ before
and after the 2012 consolidation of tax collection. We test this by
interacting the treatment variable with pre- and post-Act 32
indicators:
%
\begin{equation}
    Y_{it} = \alpha_i + \gamma_t
    + \beta^{\text{pre}} \cdot \tau_{it} \cdot \mathbf{1}[t < 2012]
    + \beta^{\text{post}} \cdot \tau_{it} \cdot \mathbf{1}[t \geq 2012]
    + \varepsilon_{it}
    \label{eq:act32}
\end{equation}
%
Under Prediction 1, $\beta^{\text{pre}} \approx 0$ (no response when
the tax is not effectively collected) and $\beta^{\text{post}} < 0$
(sorting begins when the tax becomes binding through employer withholding).

\subsection{Stage 2: Tract-Level Decomposition}
\label{subsec:stage2}

The municipal-level outcome share\_live\_in captures the net effect
of all three adjustment channels but cannot distinguish between them.
To decompose the response, we exploit the tract-level structure of
LODES origin-destination data, which records job counts for every
$(w_{\text{tract}}, h_{\text{tract}}, \text{year})$ triplet. We
aggregate these flows to the city-pair level---$(A, B, t)$ for work
municipality $A$ and home municipality $B$---and construct three
analytic panels.

\paragraph{Q4: Do workers leave entirely, or just change where they sleep?}
\textit{(Tests Predictions 2 and 3: destination choice and complete
relocation.)}

For each municipality $B$, we construct a \textit{resident worker
panel} counting workers who both live and work in $B$. We then
measure $B$'s ``exposure'' to neighboring rate increases:
%
\begin{equation}
    \ln(\text{ResWorkers}_{Bt}) = \alpha_B + \gamma_t
    + \beta_1 \cdot \tau_{Bt}
    + \beta_2 \cdot \bar{\tau}^{\text{nbr}}_{Bt}
    + \varepsilon_{Bt}
    \label{eq:q4_relocation}
\end{equation}
%
\noindent where $\tau_{Bt}$ is $B$'s own EIT rate and
$\bar{\tau}^{\text{nbr}}_{Bt}$ is the average rate among $B$'s
neighbors (within 80~km). If $\beta_1 < 0$, higher own rates reduce
resident workers (consistent with Channels 1--3). If $\beta_2 > 0$,
cities absorb resident workers when neighbors raise rates---evidence
of Channel 2 (complete relocation toward the lower-rate city).

To test Prediction 2 (destination rates irrelevant for stayers),
we estimate a \textit{commuter pair model}:
%
\begin{equation}
    \ln(\text{Flow}_{ABt}) = \alpha_{AB} + \gamma_t
    + \beta_1 \cdot \tau^{w}_{At}
    + \beta_2 \cdot \tau^{h}_{Bt}
    + \varepsilon_{ABt}
    \label{eq:q4_pairs}
\end{equation}
%
\noindent for all commuter pairs $(A \neq B)$ within 80~km,
where $\text{Flow}_{ABt}$ is the number of workers employed in $A$
and living in $B$. Under the credit system, workers who keep their
job in $A$ pay $\max(\tau_A, \tau_B) = \tau_A$ regardless of where
they live (when $\tau_A > \tau_B$). If Prediction 2 holds,
$\beta_2 \approx 0$: the home municipality's rate does not predict
commuter flows, because it provides no tax savings.

To test Prediction 3, we add the rate gap interaction:
%
\begin{equation}
    \ln(\text{Flow}_{ABt}) = \alpha_{AB} + \gamma_t
    + \beta_1 \cdot \tau^{w}_{At}
    + \beta_2 \cdot \tau^{w}_{At} \times (\tau^{w}_{At} - \tau^{h}_{Bt})
    + \varepsilon_{ABt}
    \label{eq:q4_gap}
\end{equation}
%
If $\beta_2 > 0$, flows are larger when the rate gap between work
and home is bigger---consistent with workers choosing cheaper
destinations. If $\beta_2 \leq 0$, destination choice is
unrelated to the rate differential, ruling out tax optimization
for Channel 1 stayers.

\paragraph{Q5: Do school district rates have opposite effects?}
\textit{(Tests Prediction 4: municipal versus school district
decomposition.)}

Since each municipality in our panel has both a municipal EIT rate
($\tau^{m}_{it}$) and a school district EIT rate ($\tau^{sd}_{it}$),
and these vary independently, we estimate:
%
\begin{equation}
    Y_{it} = \alpha_i + \gamma_t
    + \beta^{m} \cdot \tau^{m}_{it}
    + \beta^{sd} \cdot \tau^{sd}_{it}
    + \varepsilon_{it}
    \label{eq:sd_decomp}
\end{equation}
%
If Prediction 4 holds, $\beta^{m} < 0$ (municipal rates push
residents out) and $\beta^{sd} > 0$ (school district rates attract
residents, reflecting the amenity value of school spending). This
decomposition explains why the \textit{total} EIT rate---municipal
plus school district---may show no net sorting effect: the two
components pull in opposite directions. We estimate
Equation~\ref{eq:sd_decomp} separately for resident workers
(people who both live and work in the municipality), nonresident
commuters (people who work in the municipality but live elsewhere),
and total employment, to identify which margin the school district
rate operates on.

We also extend the commuter pair model
(Equation~\ref{eq:q4_pairs}) to include school district rates
at both the work and home municipality:
%
\begin{equation}
    \ln(\text{Flow}_{ABt}) = \alpha_{AB} + \gamma_t
    + \beta^{m}_w \cdot \tau^{m}_{At}
    + \beta^{sd}_w \cdot \tau^{sd}_{At}
    + \beta^{m}_h \cdot \tau^{m}_{Bt}
    + \beta^{sd}_h \cdot \tau^{sd}_{Bt}
    + \varepsilon_{ABt}
    \label{eq:sd_pairs}
\end{equation}
%
If school district rates operate as a residential amenity, we expect
$\beta^{sd}_h > 0$: workers are more likely to commute \textit{from}
municipalities with higher school district rates, because people
choose to live where schools are well-funded.

\paragraph{Q6: Is sorting concentrated at municipal boundaries?}
\textit{(Tests Prediction 5: boundary gradient.)}

We build a tract-level panel within each municipality, classifying
tracts as ``boundary'' (centroid within 1~km of the nearest municipal
boundary) or ``interior.'' For each work tract, we compute the share
of workers whose home tract is in the same municipality:
%
\begin{equation}
    S^{\text{within}}_{jt} = \alpha_j + \gamma_t
    + \beta_1 \cdot \tau_{it}
    + \beta_2 \cdot \tau_{it} \times \mathbf{1}[\text{boundary}_j]
    + \varepsilon_{jt}
    \label{eq:boundary}
\end{equation}
%
\noindent where $j$ indexes work tracts within municipality $i$.
If $\beta_2 < 0$, boundary tracts are more sensitive to rate changes
than interior tracts, consistent with cross-boundary sorting where
a short residential move crosses a tax boundary. If $\beta_2
\approx 0$, the city-level sorting result is driven by a uniform
response across the municipality rather than boundary-specific
sorting. We also estimate a distance gradient, binning tracts by
distance from the nearest municipal boundary (0--0.5~km, 0.5--1~km,
1--2~km, 2--5~km, 5+~km) to trace how the sorting response decays
with distance from a tax boundary.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}
%==========================================================

We present results in two stages, following the empirical strategy.
Stage 1 (Sections~\ref{subsec:q1}--\ref{subsec:q3_results})
establishes the municipal-level sorting response and its asymmetry.
Stage 2 (Sections~\ref{subsec:q4_results}--\ref{subsec:q6_results})
decomposes the response into adjustment channels using tract-level
origin-destination data.

\subsection{Stage 1: Municipal-Level Sorting}

\subsubsection{Q1: Does Adopting a Tax Cause Sorting?}
\label{subsec:q1}

We pool municipalities from both states that adopted a local income tax
during the panel (going from a zero rate to a positive rate) and compare
them to municipalities that never levied a tax. Only 8 municipalities
adopted a tax and appear in the LODES data with sufficient employment:
1 in Ohio and 7 in Pennsylvania. The control group consists of 109
never-taxed municipalities (104 Ohio townships, 5 Pennsylvania
municipalities). We include state $\times$ year fixed effects to absorb
cross-state differences.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Prediction 4 confirmed:} Municipal and school
    district EIT rates have \textit{opposite} effects on resident
    workers. A 1~pp municipal rate increase reduces log resident
    workers by $-0.136^{*}$ (SE 0.066), while a 1~pp school district
    rate increase \textit{raises} log resident workers by
    $+0.143^{**}$ (SE 0.045).
    \item The SD effect operates specifically on resident workers
    ($+0.161^{**}$), not nonresident commuters ($+0.080$, null) or
    total jobs ($+0.080$, null). Higher SD EIT rates attract people
    to \textit{live} in a municipality---consistent with a school
    quality amenity channel---without affecting where they work.
    \item Total EIT (municipal + school district combined) has
    \textit{zero} net effect ($+0.001$, SE 0.034) on resident
    workers. The two components cancel almost exactly. This explains
    why aggregate EIT rate studies may find null results: they
    conflate two taxes with opposite behavioral effects.
    \item The municipal rate coefficient is stable when controlling
    for SD rate ($-0.144$ vs $-0.136$), confirming that the two
    sources of variation are empirically independent.
\end{itemize}

\section{Pre-Trend Tests on Fiscal Observables}
\label{app:pretrends}

We test the parallel trends assumption on six fiscal observables from the PA
DCED Annual Financial Reports using the Sun and Abraham (2021) estimator.
For each outcome, we estimate Equation~\ref{eq:sunab} with municipality and
year fixed effects, clustering standard errors at the municipality level.
Table~\ref{tab:pretrends} reports the maximum absolute $t$-statistic among
the pre-treatment coefficients at relative times $\ell \in \{-5, -4, -3, -2\}$.

Pre-trends are flat for five of six fiscal observables: total revenue
(max $|t| = 1.60$), EIT revenue ($1.33$), streets expenditure ($0.80$),
population ($1.09$), and total debt ($1.36$). None of the pre-treatment
coefficients for these outcomes are individually significant at the 5\% level.

Police expenditure is the exception. The pre-treatment coefficients at
$\ell = -5$ and $\ell = -4$ are negative and significant ($t = -2.85$ and
$t = -2.42$, respectively), indicating that treated municipalities had
relatively lower police spending 4--5 years before the rate increase.
This pattern suggests that some EIT increases may have been associated with
anticipated public-safety expansions financed by the new revenue. Importantly,
the main results on \texttt{share\_live\_in} are robust to controlling for
log police expenditure (Table~\ref{tab:robustness}), confirming that this
pre-trend does not confound the estimated sorting response.
