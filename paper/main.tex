\title{Local Income Taxes and Residential Sorting: \\
\large Evidence from Pennsylvania LEHD Origin-Destination Data}
\author{Matthew Hockert\thanks{University of Minnesota. Email: hocke043@umn.edu}}
\date{\today}

\begin{document}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Do local income tax rate changes cause residential sorting across
municipal boundaries? Using LEHD Origin-Destination data linking
each worker's residence block to their workplace block at annual
frequency, I estimate the effect of Pennsylvania municipal Earned
Income Tax (EIT) rate changes on residential and workplace outcomes
for $\sim$2{,}500 municipalities over 2002--2019. The PA EIT bill
is piecewise:
$T_{ji} = \max(\tau^{\text{res,muni}}_j + \tau^{\text{SD}}_j,\
\tau^{\text{nr,muni}}_i)$, where $j$ is the worker's home muni and
$i$ is the work muni. A rate hike only \emph{moves the bill} when
the relevant side of the max binds. I exploit this structure with a
continuous stacked difference-in-differences design that decomposes
each rate event by direction (Inc/Dec) and binding-state transition
(already-binding, becomes-binding, non-binding-throughout), and
that estimates the per-pp dose-response coefficient at the census
block level---where both rate-setters' policy variation enters the
regression at its native level---with municipality- and
school-district-level aggregations reported as cross-checks. The
within-design placebo is the Inc-nobind cell: rate hikes whose
post-event home stack remains below the work-side nonresident rate,
so the bill never moves and theory predicts no behavioral response.
The headline finding is that a 1 percentage-point muni resident-rate
increase in the binding cell reduces the block-level count of workers living in the block (in logs) by approximately 19 percent ($-0.21$ log
points, $\text{SE}=0.054$) with a clean realized-dose parallel-trends
test ($\beta_{\text{pre}} = -0.027$, $|t| < 1$). The municipality-level analogue reduces the count of residents working in their own municipality (in logs) by approximately
33 percent ($-0.40$ log points) and raises the share of residents
commuting out by 4.1 percentage points, though the muni-level pooled
Inc-bind pre-trend fails parallel trends ($\beta_{\text{pre}} = +0.149$,
$|t| = 2.66$), consistent with selection of post-industrial cities
into rate hikes. The Inc-nobind placebo is null on the headline count
outcomes at both levels. School district rate changes do not produce
sorting at any binding state, even when SD changes flip the home-stack
binding state. Industry-decomposed outcomes show consumer-facing jobs
(retail, food, arts, other services) fall in the binding cell while
tradeable jobs (manufacturing, wholesale, finance, professional
services) are insulated, consistent with a local-services demand
spillover from residents leaving rather than direct nonresident-tax
incidence.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
%==========================================================

The Tiebout hypothesis---that households ``vote with their feet'' by
sorting across jurisdictions offering different bundles of taxes and
public services \citep{tiebout1956}---remains a central organizing
principle in local public finance. A large empirical literature
documents tax-driven migration at the state level for high earners
\citep{kleven2020, young2016, moretti2017} and at sub-national borders
for sales taxes \citep{agrawal2015gradient}. Yet sub-state income-tax
sorting---the canonical Tiebout margin, where moving costs are small
and tax boundaries are crossed by a short relocation---is essentially
untested in the United States. Two recent studies of Indiana and
Maryland local income taxes \citep{yang2025, heim2017} use
county-level IRS data and find county-level migration responses but
cannot resolve the muni-level variation that defines the actual tax
boundary. A literature survey by \citet{agrawal2025} explicitly
identifies sub-state local-income-tax sorting as an open question, and
\citet{agrawal2022} characterize the empirical evidence as ``thin.''

The gap persists for two reasons. First, the jurisdictions that levy
local income taxes are sub-county, so standard migration data sources
do not see them. The IRS Statistics of Income tracks county-to-county
migration; the American Community Survey measures place-level
population but does not link workers to employers. Second, local
income-tax systems feature credit mechanisms, overlapping
school-district taxes, and uneven enforcement that complicate the
mapping from statutory rates to effective tax burden. A naive
comparison of rates and population changes conflates tax incidence
with amenity valuation, fiscal capacity, and enforcement quality.

This paper addresses both challenges using LEHD Origin-Destination
Employment Statistics (LODES), the only administrative data source
that links each worker's residence tract to their workplace tract at
annual frequency. The setting is Pennsylvania, 2002--2019. Pennsylvania
operates a universal Earned Income Tax under Act 511 of 1965 with
continuous rate variation across approximately 2{,}500 municipalities
and 500 school districts, and a quasi-experimental enforcement break:
Act 32 of 2008 (effective January 1, 2012) consolidated EIT collection
into 69 Tax Collection Districts and mandated employer withholding at
the higher of the home resident rate or the work nonresident rate.
Before Act 32, withholding was fragmented and many municipalities did
not even publish a nonresident rate. The structural break in
enforcement---same statutory rates, very different effective tax
burden---anchors causal identification.

This paper makes three contributions. First, it identifies the binding
structure of the PA EIT as the central margin for empirical
identification. The bill is piecewise in
$\max(\tau^{\text{home}}, \tau^{\text{nr,work}})$, so a rate change
only moves the bill when its side binds. I classify each muni-rate
event by direction (Inc/Dec) and binding-state transition relative to
the muni's nonresident rate, yielding seven cells (six event cells +
NC controls). The within-design placebo is the Inc-nobind cell: rate
hikes whose post-event home stack stays below the nonresident rate, so
the bill never moves and theory predicts no behavioral response.

Second, the empirical design is a unified continuous stacked DiD with
two simultaneous rate-setter treatments---muni resident rate and SD
resident rate, each separately decomposed by binding cells---estimated
at three units of observation (municipality, school district, census
block). Triangulation across estimating units replaces the
cross-estimator triangulation of an earlier draft: each level aligns
the regression with a different rate-setter's jurisdiction and exposes
different pre-trend behavior. Pre-trend tests are reported via two
complementary diagnostics: a realized-dose Sun-Abraham continuous
event study \citep{callawaygoodmanbaconsantanna2024,dechaisemartin2024}
and a binary Sun-Abraham IW event study \citep{sunab2021}.
Controls are share-based throughout: police, streets, and debt service
as shares of total expenditures; EIT, real estate, and intergovernmental
revenue as shares of total revenues. The never-changed control pool is
strictly defined as munis whose rate never changed across the panel,
not the broader set of munis whose first event could be cell-classified.

Third, the headline finding is that the muni resident rate moves
residents through the binding channel only. The block-level estimate
(Inc-bind on the log count of workers living in the block) is $-0.21$ log points per
pp---approximately a 19 percent decline in resident workers per
percentage-point of $\tau^{\text{res,muni}}$, with a clean realized-
dose parallel-trends diagnostic. The municipality-level analogue is
larger ($-0.40$ log points on the log count of residents working in their own municipality,
$\approx 33\%$ per pp; $-0.27$ on log total jobs,
$\approx 24\%$; $-0.10$ on log employed residents,
$\approx 10\%$; and the share of residents commuting out rises
4.1 percentage points) but its pre-trend diagnostic fails parallel
trends, consistent with selection into rate hikes by declining
post-industrial cities. The Inc-nobind placebo cell---rate hikes
whose post-event home stack stays below the nonresident rate---is
statistically null on the headline count outcomes at both levels.
School district rate changes do not produce sorting in any cell,
even when SD changes flip the binding state, consistent with workers
responding to the bill rather than to the rate's identity. Industry
decomposition using LODES WAC at the municipality level shows
consumer-facing jobs (retail, food, arts, other services) fall in
the binding cell while tradeable jobs (manufacturing, wholesale,
finance, professional services) are insulated, consistent with a
local-services demand spillover from residents leaving rather than
direct nonresident-tax incidence.

The municipality-level magnitude is concentrated in 8 post-industrial
PA cities (Wilkes-Barre, Hazleton, Johnstown, Jeannette, Coatesville,
Warren, Carbondale, Farrell); the 32 borough and township Inc-bind
munis show direction-consistent but individually insignificant
effects. The pre-trend failure at the municipality level is
attributable to this composition: high-eventual-dose cities are on
declining trajectories before their rate changes. The block-level
specification absorbs the gentrification-correlated pre-trend at
finer geography, which is why I report the block-level estimate as
the headline causal quantity and the municipality-level estimate as
the typical-jurisdiction magnitude calibration.

\section{Institutional Background}
\label{sec:background}
%==========================================================

Pennsylvania's Earned Income Tax (EIT) is the largest source of
local-income-tax variation in the United States. Approximately 2{,}500
municipalities and 500 school districts each set their own rate,
covering essentially all earned income earned in or by Pennsylvania
residents. Unlike property taxes, which are paid by owners and
capitalized into home values, the EIT falls directly on workers and
varies across municipal boundaries within the same metropolitan area.
This creates sharp spatial discontinuities in tax burden that can
influence where workers choose to live and work.

\subsection{Pennsylvania Earned Income Tax}
\label{subsec:pennsylvania}

Pennsylvania's EIT is mandatory for all municipalities under the
Local Tax Enabling Act (Act 511 of 1965). It applies to all earned
income with no minimum threshold; retirees are exempt. The standard
combined cap is 1\%, typically split as 0.5\% municipal and 0.5\%
school district. Home Rule municipalities may exceed this cap, and
Philadelphia operates separately under the Sterling Act of 1932 at
approximately 3.9\%. The EIT is the primary revenue lever for most
Pennsylvania municipalities: property tax millage caps are binding in
many jurisdictions. Our panel (2002--2019) contains 210 muni rate
increases, 119 muni rate decreases, 611 school-district rate
increases, and 52 school-district rate decreases.

\paragraph{Three rates per municipality.}
Each Pennsylvania municipality has three EIT rates relevant to a
worker:
%
\begin{itemize}
\item $\tau^{\text{res,muni}}$, the muni resident rate, levied on residents of the muni;
\item $\tau^{\text{nr,muni}}$, the muni nonresident rate, levied on workers employed in the muni who live elsewhere;
\item $\tau^{\text{SD}}$, the school-district rate, levied on residents only (school districts cannot tax nonresidents).
\end{itemize}
%
Each address is associated with a six-digit Political Subdivision (PSD)
code identifying its (muni, school district) pair. Workers report this
code on the Residency Certification Form, and employers use it to
determine withholding obligations.

\paragraph{The piecewise tax allocation.}
Under Act 32 (effective January 1, 2012), a worker's total EIT bill
is determined by the maximum of the home-side rate stack and the
work-location nonresident rate, and the bill is split piecewise
between home and work jurisdictions:
%
\begin{equation}
\bigl(\tau^{\text{home}},\ \tau^{\text{work}}\bigr) =
\begin{cases}
\bigl(\tau^{\text{res,muni}}_j + \tau^{\text{SD}}_j - \tau^{\text{nr,muni}}_i,\ \tau^{\text{nr,muni}}_i\bigr)
   & \text{if } \tau^{\text{res,muni}}_j + \tau^{\text{SD}}_j > \tau^{\text{nr,muni}}_i \\[4pt]
\bigl(0,\ \tau^{\text{nr,muni}}_i\bigr)
   & \text{if } \tau^{\text{res,muni}}_j + \tau^{\text{SD}}_j \leq \tau^{\text{nr,muni}}_i
\end{cases}
\label{eq:piecewise}
\end{equation}
%
where $j$ is the worker's home muni and $i$ is the work muni. The
work jurisdiction always collects its full nonresident rate first;
the home muni and school district then split whatever portion of the
worker's bill is left over after the work claim. If the work nonresident
rate exceeds the home stack, the home jurisdictions get nothing. The
worker's total bill is the higher of the two stacks:
%
\begin{equation}
\text{Bill}_{ji} = \max\!\bigl(\tau^{\text{res,muni}}_j + \tau^{\text{SD}}_j,\ \tau^{\text{nr,muni}}_i\bigr).
\label{eq:bill}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Implications for sorting.}
Equation~(\ref{eq:piecewise}) implies which margin a worker can use to
respond to a rate increase. When the home stack binds
($\tau^{\text{home}}_j > \tau^{\text{nr,muni}}_i$), the worker can lower
the bill only by moving home to a lower-rate muni or school district.
When the work nonresident rate binds, the worker can lower the bill
only by changing job to a lower-nonres-rate work location; moving home
without changing job has no effect. For workers who live and work in
the same muni, only the home stack applies---they pay
$\tau^{\text{res,muni}}_i + \tau^{\text{SD}}_i$.

A worker who lives in muni $j$ and moves to a lower-rate muni $j'$
while keeping the same job at $i$ saves at least the SD rate at $j$:
the new bill is $\max(\tau^{\text{home}}_{j'}, \tau^{\text{nr}}_i)$,
which is at most the old bill $\tau^{\text{home}}_j$ minus
$\tau^{\text{SD}}_j$. The often-stated claim that ``moving residence
under a credit system yields zero tax savings'' is wrong under
Pennsylvania's piecewise structure---residence moves do save tax,
because the home-side stack includes the SD rate that does not
reappear at the work side.

\paragraph{Philadelphia (excluded from the Act 32 framework).}
Philadelphia operates under the Sterling Act of 1932 rather than
Act 32. Philadelphia residents pay the city resident rate regardless
of work location with no offset; Philadelphia nonresidents pay the
city nonresident rate, with no residual flowing to home jurisdictions
in other Pennsylvania munis. Our main estimates retain Philadelphia in
the sample for completeness; results are robust to dropping it.

\paragraph{Act 32: Tax Collection Consolidation.}
Before 2012, EIT collection was radically decentralized: each of
Pennsylvania's 2,500+ municipalities appointed its own tax officer.
Nonresident EIT enforcement was weak because no centralized system
linked employers, workers, and municipalities. A worker who moved
out of a rate-raising municipality could, in practice, avoid paying
the nonresident portion to the work municipality even though they
legally owed it. The piecewise rule described in
Equation~\ref{eq:piecewise} existed on paper but was not effectively
enforced.

Act 32 of 2008 (effective January 1, 2012) transformed this system
by consolidating collection into approximately 67 county-based Tax
Collection Districts (TCDs), each with a single appointed collector.
The law mandated standardized employer withholding at the higher of
the employee's resident and work rate. This had two consequences.
First, it made the EIT \textit{binding}: rate changes now mechanically
appear in every worker's paycheck through employer withholding,
regardless of whether they reside in the taxing municipality.
Second, it eliminated the possibility of de facto tax avoidance
through residential relocation. Before Act 32, moving away from a
rate-raising city could effectively reduce a worker's tax burden
because the work municipality lacked the enforcement capacity to
collect from nonresidents. After Act 32, the credit system is
fully enforced, and moving away while keeping the same job provides
no tax relief.

\paragraph{School District EIT.}
School districts in Pennsylvania also levy an EIT under Act 511,
typically at 0.5\% (the other half of the standard 1\% cap).
Act 1 of 2006 (the Taxpayer Relief Act) additionally authorized
school districts to exceed the 0.5\% cap specifically to fund
property tax relief. This generated a wave of school district
rate increases in 2006--2011, creating rate variation that is
largely independent of municipal fiscal decisions: the correlation
between municipal and school district EIT rates across our panel
is $-0.12$. Since every census tract sits in both a municipality
and a school district, workers face two overlapping EIT rates
that can change independently, providing a natural decomposition
of the residential sorting response into municipal and school
district components.

\section{Conceptual Framework: Binding-State Decomposition}
\label{sec:theory}
%==========================================================

The piecewise tax allocation in Equation~(\ref{eq:piecewise}) is the
analytical engine of the empirical design. A worker's bill is
$T = \max(\tau^{\text{home}}, \tau^{\text{nr,work}})$. A rate change
only \emph{moves the bill} when its side of the max binds. This
generates a sharp prediction: the same statutory rate change can
produce a strong behavioral response, a moderate response, or no
response at all depending on the binding state pre- and post-event.

\subsection{Six Cells per Side}
\label{subsec:cells}

For each muni $i$ and each rate variable $\tau \in \{\tau^{\text{res,muni}},
\tau^{\text{SD}}, \tau^{\text{nr,muni}}\}$, classify the muni's first
observed rate event by direction (Increase / Decrease) and binding-state
transition relative to the muni's own home-stack vs nonresident rate
comparison:
%
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Inc-bind}: rate up; home stack was binding pre and post.
  Bill rises by full $\Delta\tau$ for in-muni residents. \emph{Predicted:
  strongest behavioral response.}
\item \textbf{Inc-becomes}: rate up; home stack flips from non-binding
  to binding. Bill rises by less than full $\Delta\tau$ (only the portion
  above the work nonres rate). \emph{Predicted: intermediate response.}
\item \textbf{Inc-nobind}: rate up; home stack stays below nonres rate
  throughout. \emph{Bill never moves. Predicted: no response. This is
  the within-design placebo.}
\item \textbf{Dec-bind}: rate down; home stack stays binding. Bill falls
  by full $\Delta\tau$. Predicted mirror of Inc-bind under symmetry.
\item \textbf{Dec-becomes-non}: rate down; home stack flips from binding
  to non-binding. Bill falls only down to nonres rate.
\item \textbf{Dec-nobind}: rate down; home stack stays below nonres
  throughout. Bill never moves. Second placebo cell.
\end{itemize}
%
A seventh category, NC (\emph{never-changed}), comprises munis whose
$\tau$ never changes across the panel. NC serves as the control pool.

The fundamental testable prediction of the institutional rule is that
\textbf{Inc-bind effects $>$ Inc-becomes effects $>$ Inc-nobind effects
$\approx 0$ in absolute value}, with Inc-nobind serving as the
within-design placebo. If the Inc-nobind cell shows behavioral
response, the design's mechanism interpretation fails---the response
is something other than the binding rule.

\subsection{Three Treatment Margins}
\label{subsec:treatments}

PA's institutional structure separates three rate variables that
change independently across muni-years:
%
\begin{itemize}
\item $\tau^{\text{res,muni}}$ (muni resident rate) directly affects
  the bill of in-muni residents through the home-stack channel.
\item $\tau^{\text{SD}}$ (school district rate) also affects the home
  stack but is set by school districts, not municipalities. Predicted:
  same binding-rule mechanism as muni rate, but the SD's institutional
  decision-making is different from the muni's.
\item $\tau^{\text{nr,muni}}$ (muni nonresident rate) affects nonresident
  workers commuting in. The piecewise rule means it moves the bill of
  workers from low-home-stack origins.
\end{itemize}
%
The two predictions across treatments:
%
\begin{enumerate}
\item Each rate's binding-cell decomposition shows the same Inc-bind $>$
  Inc-becomes $>$ Inc-nobind pattern.
\item Channel separation: muni resident rate moves residents (live-and-work,
  total residents); muni nonresident rate moves nonresident workers
  (in-commuters); SD rate's effect depends on whether SD changes induce
  similar residential responses.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Data}
\label{sec:data}
%==========================================================

\subsection{Data Construction Pipeline}
\label{subsec:pipeline}

The analysis links two administrative systems that do not share
common identifiers: LEHD/LODES employment records (Census block
geography) and Pennsylvania EIT rates (DCED municipality IDs). The
build sequence scrapes the DCED MunStats portal for the rate panel,
scrapes the DCED Annual Financial Reports for muni fiscal controls,
downloads block-level LODES files for 2002--2019 (a $\sim$5 GB
raw OD database), crosswalks PA municipalities to TIGER COUSUBFP
codes by normalized name and county (99.6\% match), builds a
block-to-municipality crosswalk by point-in-polygon assignment of
block centroids to 2020 cousub geometries (Census blocks nest in
cousubs by construction), and aggregates block-pair-year LODES
flows to the municipality, school-district, and block levels of
observation. Step-by-step pipeline detail (scraper specs,
crosswalks, imputation rules, and the auxiliary LODES file for
cross-state inflows) is in the Companion Appendix
(\texttt{appendix.tex}).

\subsection{Data Sources}
\label{subsec:sources}

\paragraph{LODES Origin-Destination Data.}
LEHD LODES Version 8, 2002--2019. Block-level residence--workplace
links derived from state Unemployment Insurance wage records and the
Quarterly Census of Employment and Wages \citep{abowd2009lehd}. We
trim 2020--2021 due to COVID-related disruptions in both employment
patterns and LODES coverage. The Census Bureau applies synthetic
noise infusion at the block level to protect respondent
confidentiality; the mechanism is calibrated so that aggregation to
larger geographies produces estimates that approximate ground truth
and have been validated against external sources at the tract level
and above \citep{machanavajjhala2008, graham2014lodes,
lodes_techdoc_v8}. We aggregate blocks to county subdivisions
(municipalities) using the TIGER 2020 block-to-municipality crosswalk described
in Section~\ref{subsec:pipeline}, item~3.

\paragraph{Municipal Tax Rate Panel.}
Pennsylvania: DCED MunStats, scraped annually. Resident and
nonresident municipal EIT rates, school district EIT rates for all
$\sim$2{,}500 municipalities. Name-based crosswalk to Census COUSUBFP
(99.6\% match rate). Pre-2012 nonresident rates backfilled from
earliest observed post-2012 value per muni (the registry was
incomplete pre-Act 32).

\paragraph{Municipal Financial Reports.}
PA DCED Annual Financial Reports, 2002--2019 (trimmed from 2002--2023
to exclude COVID/ARPA fiscal distortion). 71 columns per
municipality-year including EIT revenue, real estate tax revenue,
police expenditure, streets expenditure, total debt, state
intergovernmental aid, population, and assessed market value of real
estate.

\paragraph{Crime.}
FBI Uniform Crime Reports, Offenses Known and Clearances by Arrest
(Jacob Kaplan concatenated files), 2002--2019. Monthly agency-level
counts of Part I index crimes (murder, robbery, aggravated assault,
burglary, larceny-theft, motor vehicle theft). Aggregated to annual
totals per agency. Matched to PA municipalities via \texttt{fips\_place\_code}
(93.5\% agency match rate).

\paragraph{Building Permits.}
Census Bureau Building Permits Survey (BPS), place-level annual files,
2002--2019. Total housing units permitted by structure type (1-unit,
2-unit, 3--4 unit, 5+ unit). PA municipalities matched via
\texttt{fips\_mcd\_code} = COUSUBFP (93.3\% MCD match rate).

\subsection{Sample Restrictions and Transformations}
\label{subsec:sample}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Sample period: 2002--2019.}
    LODES coverage begins in 2002. We trim at 2019 because COVID
    disrupted both commuting patterns (LODES 2020--2021) and municipal
    finances (ARPA transfers distort AFR data 2020--2023). Including
    these years would conflate pandemic-driven relocation with
    tax-motivated sorting.

    \item \textbf{Minimum employment threshold: 50 total jobs.}
    Small municipalities have share\_live\_in values dominated by
    sampling noise (a single worker moving changes the ratio by
    $1/N$). The 50-job threshold balances precision against sample
    size. Sensitivity analysis (Section~\ref{sec:robustness}) confirms
    stability across thresholds from 10 to 500.

    \item \textbf{Rate outlier filters.}
    PA DCED reports the Local Services Tax (a flat dollar amount) in
    the same field as the EIT rate (a percentage) in some years.
    Dropping rates $>5$\% removes these data entry errors without
    affecting any real EIT rate (the highest standard EIT is $\sim$1\%;
    only Philadelphia exceeds 3\%).

    \item \textbf{April 1 alignment.}
    LODES records employment as of April 1. A rate change that takes
    effect mid-year could be misattributed if not aligned. We assign
    each municipality-year the rate in effect on April 1, ensuring
    the tax rate a worker faces and the OD flow recording their
    location refer to the same date.

    \item \textbf{Outcome variables.}
    The primary outcome is $\textit{share\_live\_in}_{it} =
    \text{live\_and\_work}_{it} / \text{total\_jobs}_{it}$, the
    locally-resident fraction of a city's workforce. Unlike population
    counts (which mix retirees, students, and non-workers) or IRS
    migration data (which is county-level), share\_live\_in isolates
    the residential choices of active workers at municipal resolution.

    To decompose the share response into levels, we also estimate
    effects on log(resident workers) and log(total jobs) separately.
    Fiscal outcomes include log EIT revenue, log total tax revenue,
    log total revenue, log police expenditure, log population, and
    log market value of real estate from the PA DCED Annual Financial
    Reports. Pre-treatment balance tests cover eleven observables
    including crime and building permits.

    \item \textbf{Treatment definitions.}
    We use both a continuous and a binary treatment definition.
    \textit{Continuous}: the current rate level (resident municipal,
    school district, and the home stack as defined in
    Equation~\ref{eq:piecewise}), capturing the intensive margin via
    TWFE and DCDH. \textit{Binary cohorts}: 1 after a municipality's
    first rate increase (or first decrease), enabling Sun-Abraham
    estimation of staggered-DiD-robust dynamic effects.

    \item \textbf{Control group.}
    Pennsylvania's EIT is universal, so the relevant control group
    is municipalities with a stable rate over 2002--2019
    (1{,}264 never-changed municipalities). For the Q3 clean-groups
    specification we further partition treated municipalities into
    increase-only (97) and decrease-only (24), dropping the
    flip-floppers that change in both directions.

    \item \textbf{Fiscal controls.}
    Rate increases may coincide with other fiscal changes (police
    hiring, debt issuance) that independently affect residential
    attractiveness. We include log police expenditure, log streets
    expenditure, log total debt, and log state intergovernmental aid
    as time-varying controls, plus revenue shares (EIT,
    real-estate, intergovernmental) from the PA DCED AFR. The main
    estimates are unchanged with and without these controls
    (the robustness section (Section~\ref{sec:robustness})).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Empirical Strategy}
\label{sec:strategy}
%==========================================================

The empirical framework is a continuous stacked
difference-in-differences design with binding-state cell decomposition,
estimated at three units of observation: municipality, school district,
and census block. Each estimating unit aligns the regression with a
different level of treatment aggregation, producing a triangulation across
units rather than across estimators. The first-difference dose-response
specification estimates the post-event effect of a one percentage-point
change in the relevant tax rate; a separate realized-dose event-study
diagnostic tests for differential pre-event trajectories across units of
varying eventual treatment intensity.

\subsection{Setup}
\label{subsec:setup}

This subsection describes the elements of the design that are common
across the three estimating units (municipality, school district, census
block); unit-specific choices for treatment construction, fiscal
controls, fixed effects, and clustering are described in
Section~\ref{subsec:three-units}.

\textbf{Sample period.} 2002--2019. Pre-2002 data are not consistently
available in LODES; post-2019 years are excluded because COVID-19
disrupted commuting patterns and ARPA fiscal transfers distorted
muni budgets.

\textbf{Outcomes.} The continuous-stacked-DiD specification is run in
parallel across a set of municipality-level workplace and residence
outcomes: log of the count of \texttt{live\_and\_work} (residents
employed in the muni), \texttt{in\_commuters} (workers in the muni
from elsewhere), \texttt{out\_commuters} (residents employed
elsewhere), \texttt{total\_jobs}, and \texttt{total\_residents};
plus the shares $the share of workers living in their own muni$, $the share of workers who are nonresidents$,
and $the share of residents working outside the muni$. SD-level regressions use the
same outcomes aggregated to the SD via the muni--SD assignment. At
the block level, outcomes are redefined as block-level analogues
(the log count of workers living in the block, the log count of workers at the block,
the share of block residents working in their own muni, the share of residents working outside the muni); see
Section~\ref{subsec:three-units}.

\textbf{Two rate treatments.} The unified resident-side specification
includes two simultaneous rate-setter treatments:
$\Delta\tau^{\text{muni}}_{it}$ (cumulative change in the muni
resident rate from baseline) and $\Delta\tau^{\text{SD}}_{it}$
(cumulative change in the school district rate). Baseline for each
is the rate at year $c_i^s - 1$ where $c_i^s$ is the unit's first
event year on side $s \in \{\text{muni, SD}\}$. Cumulative dose
accumulates with subsequent rate changes within unit $i$. The work-side
nonresident rate $\tau^{\text{nr}}$ enters the binding-state
classification of cells (Section~\ref{subsec:cell-class}) but is not
itself a treatment in the headline regression; we focus on the
resident-side mechanism in this paper and defer the work-side
behavioral response to a separate analysis.

\textbf{Cell decomposition.} Each rate-change event is classified by
direction $\in \{\text{Inc, Dec}\}$ and binding-state transition $\in
\{\text{bind, becomes, nobind}\}$, yielding six non-NC cells per
side. The classification rule is given in
Section~\ref{subsec:cell-class}. Units whose rate $\tau^s$ never
changes across the panel are assigned to the NC reference category
on side $s$.

\textbf{Fiscal controls.} Share-based, all time-varying. The exact
control set differs by estimating unit and is described in
Section~\ref{subsec:three-units}. Municipality-level regressions use muni
AFR shares (police, streets, debt service / total expenditures; EIT,
real estate, intergovernmental / total revenues). SD-level
regressions use Census F-33 SD shares (state, local, federal,
property-tax revenue shares; instructional, support, capital, debt-
interest expenditure shares). Block-level regressions use both,
since each block is exposed to both rate-setters' fiscal posture.

\subsection{Cell Classification (per side)}
\label{subsec:cell-class}

The six binding-state cells are defined in Section~\ref{subsec:cells}.
For each unit $i$ and each rate-setter side $s \in \{\text{muni, SD}\}$,
I identify the first rate event year $c^s_i$ and assign the unit to
one of the six non-NC cells based on the direction of the change and
the binding state of the home stack relative to
$\tau^{\text{nr,muni}}$ in the pre- and post-event years. Units
whose rate $\tau^s$ never changes across 2002--2019 are assigned to
the NC reference category on side $s$.

\textbf{NC purity.} The NC category is restricted strictly to units
whose rate is constant over the full panel. Units with rate changes
whose first event has an undefined binding state---typically because
the muni's nonresident rate is missing in the pre-event year due to
DCED reporting gaps before Act 32---are categorized as ``Uncertain''
and dropped from the analysis. Pooling them with NC would contaminate
the control trend with treated-but-unclassified units; reporting them
in their own cell would mix true binding states with measurement
gaps.

\subsection{Headline Estimator: Stacked Continuous DiD}
\label{subsec:stacked-continuous}

The headline estimator is stacked continuous difference-in-differences.
For each side $s$ and each cohort year $c$ at which any muni's first
event on side $s$ occurs, build a sub-experiment $\mathcal{S}^s_c$:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Treated set:} munis $i$ with $c^s_i = c$ and cell$^s_i
\neq$ NC.
\item \textbf{Control set:} all NC$^s$ munis (never-changed on side $s$).
\item \textbf{Window:} years $t \in [c-K, c+K]$ where $K = 5$.
\item \textbf{Stack indicator:} $s_i = c$ for treated muni $i$;
$s_i = c$ for each NC muni replicated in this stack.
\item \textbf{Relative-event time:} $\ell_{it} = t - c$.
\item \textbf{Stack-muni and stack-year identifiers:}
$\tilde{i} = (s_i, i)$, $\tilde{t} = (s_i, t)$.
\end{itemize}

Stacking all sub-experiments yields a single panel where each NC muni
appears once per cohort and each treated muni appears in exactly one
sub-experiment. Within each sub-experiment, controls are clean (no
treated muni from another cohort serves as control), avoiding the
negative-weighting problem in vanilla TWFE under staggered treatment
\citep{goodmanbacon2021}.

The stacked-DiD construction is in the lineage of
\citet{cengiz2019} (138 minimum-wage events, stacked event-by-event
with continuous treatment), \citet{fadlon2021} (severe health shocks,
stacked with future-treated households as controls), and
\citet{deshpande2019} (SSA field-office closings, stacked with
future-closing ZIP codes as controls). Three differences are worth
flagging.

\textbf{Control-group choice.} \citet{fadlon2021} and
\citet{deshpande2019} use \emph{future-treated} units (those that
will be treated at $\tau + \Delta$) as controls, because never-treated
units are scarce or selected in their settings (no never-dying
households; selection into closing eligibility). \citet{cengiz2019}
uses \emph{clean controls} (states with no minimum-wage events in the
8-year window around the focal event), which permits states with
events outside the window to serve as controls. We use the strictest
specification: \emph{never-changed} (NC) munis, defined as those
whose rate $\tau^s$ never changes across 2002--2019. Since 95\% of PA
municipalities are NC on at least one rate side, the never-changed pool is
abundant and we do not need to relax to future-treated or
clean-within-window controls.

\textbf{Continuous treatment.} \citet{cengiz2019} is the closest
analogue on this dimension: their treatment is continuous
(log minimum wage) within each stacked sub-experiment. Their
identification logic---``aligning events by event-time, not calendar
time, equivalent to a setting where the events happen all at once''
\citep[Online Appendix C]{cengiz2019}---applies identically in our
setup. We extend their continuous-treatment design by extracting
\emph{cell-specific} dose-response coefficients via the
$i(\text{cell}, \Delta\tau)$ interaction, which their pooled
event-by-event spec does not produce.

\textbf{Cell decomposition.} The binding-state cell partitioning is
new relative to all three. \citet{cengiz2019} pools all minimum-wage
events without a within-design taxonomy of treated units;
\citet{fadlon2021} and \citet{deshpande2019} have homogeneous
treatments (death, closure). Our decomposition exploits the piecewise
PA EIT rule to partition treated munis into cells whose ex ante
predicted response differs by binding state, generating a
within-design placebo (Inc-nobind) that the standard stacked DiD
cannot produce.

\paragraph{Multi-event treated munis.}
A treated muni $i$ may have more than one rate-change event over
2002--2019. We classify each such muni by the binding-state transition
of its \emph{first} event and assign $i$ to the corresponding cell on
side $s$. The muni then appears in exactly one sub-experiment
(its first-event cohort $c^s_i$), not in subsequent cohorts triggered
by later events.

Subsequent rate changes within the same muni accumulate into
$\Delta\tau^s_{it}$, the \emph{cumulative} dose from the muni's
pre-event baseline. Concretely: suppose muni $i$ has $\tau^s_i = 0.50$
in years 2005--2009, $\tau^s_i = 0.70$ in 2010 (first event,
$c^s_i = 2010$, baseline $= 0.50$), and $\tau^s_i = 1.00$ from 2014
onward (second event). Then $\Delta\tau^s_{it} = 0$ for $t < 2010$,
$\Delta\tau^s_{it} = 0.20$ for $t \in [2010, 2013]$, and
$\Delta\tau^s_{it} = 0.50$ for $t \geq 2014$. The stacked-continuous
specification absorbs this trajectory through the dose-stack
interaction in a single regression on muni $i$'s 2005--2015 window
(its first-event cohort 2010 with $K = 5$). Muni $i$ is \emph{not}
duplicated as a 2014 cohort treated muni; it stays in its 2010
cohort. The cumulative dose path encodes the second event by raising
$\Delta\tau^s_{it}$ from $0.20$ to $0.50$ at year 2014.

This first-event classification has three deliberate consequences.
First, no treated muni serves as control to itself; each muni appears
in exactly one stack. Second, multi-event treated munis do not receive
disproportionate weight from appearing in multiple stacks. Third, the
per-pp coefficient $\beta^s_k$ is interpretable as a dose-response
elasticity over the cumulative rate change, which is the
welfare-relevant magnitude for fiscal calibration.

For each treated muni $i$ in stack $\mathcal{S}^s_c$, define the
cumulative dose-stack as
$\Delta\tau^s_{it} = \tau^s_{it} - \tau^s_{i, c-1}$, with
$\Delta\tau^s_{it} = 0$ for $t < c$ (pre-event) and for NC munis at
all $t$. The stacked continuous DiD specification, for outcome $Y$,
runs the regression on the stacked panel:

\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
Y_{\tilde{i}\tilde{t}} \;=\; \alpha_{\tilde{i}} \;+\; \gamma_{\tilde{t}}
&+ \sum_{k \in \mathcal{C}_{\backslash \text{NC}}} \beta^{\text{muni}}_k \;\Delta\tau^{\text{muni}}_{it} \cdot \mathbf{1}\{\text{cell}^{\text{muni}}_i = k\} \\
&+ \sum_{k \in \mathcal{C}_{\backslash \text{NC}}} \beta^{\text{SD}}_k  \;\Delta\tau^{\text{SD}}_{it}   \cdot \mathbf{1}\{\text{cell}^{\text{SD}}_i = k\}   \\
&+ \mathbf{X}'_{it}\boldsymbol{\phi}
\;+\; \varepsilon_{\tilde{i}\tilde{t}},
\end{split}
\label{eq:headline}
\end{equation}

where
\begin{itemize}
\item $\mathcal{C}_{\backslash \text{NC}} = \{\text{Inc-bind, Inc-becomes,
Inc-nobind, Dec-bind, Dec-becomes-non, Dec-nobind}\}$ is the set of
non-NC cells.
\item NC is the omitted reference category on each side; for each of
the two rate-setter sides (muni and SD), the regression estimates
six $\beta_k$ coefficients, each interpreted as the dose-response
coefficient \emph{relative to the NC trend on that side}, holding the
other side's cell $\times$ dose interaction fixed.
\item Each unit $i$ appears in exactly one sub-experiment for each
side where it is in a non-NC cell. NC units appear in every sub-
experiment on the side(s) where they are stable.
\item $\alpha_{\tilde{i}}$ absorbs all time-invariant features of the
muni-stack pair (so each muni has a separate FE for each cohort it
appears in); $\gamma_{\tilde{t}}$ absorbs cohort-specific time effects.
\item $\mathbf{X}_{it}$ is the share-based control vector specified
above.
\item Standard errors are clustered at the muni level (not the
muni-stack level), accommodating within-muni serial correlation across
sub-experiments.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Interpretation of coefficients.}
The cell coefficient $\beta^s_k$ is the per-pp dose-response of outcome
$Y$ to a 1 percentage-point change in rate $s$, restricted to munis
whose first event on side $s$ falls in cell $k$, with NC munis on
side $s$ as controls. Specifically, $\beta^{\text{muni}}_{\text{Inc-bind}}$
is the dose-response of $Y$ to muni-rate hikes among munis whose home
stack was binding both pre- and post-event, identified by comparing
their post-event trajectory to NC munis' contemporaneous trajectory.
The placebo within the design is
$\beta^{\text{muni}}_{\text{Inc-nobind}}$: under the institutional rule,
this should be zero (the rate hike does not move the bill).

\paragraph{Comparison structure.}
Within each sub-experiment, the comparison is
treated cohort vs NC controls in the same calendar-year window.
The cell decomposition partitions the treated set by whether the rate
change moved the bill. The reference for each cell coefficient is the
NC trend on the same side. The triple decomposition (muni $\times$ SD
$\times$ nr) lets us read off, in a single regression: which rate moves
which outcome, conditional on the muni's binding state on that side.

\paragraph{Identifying variation: dose trajectories and dose magnitudes.}
Figure~\ref{fig:dose_trajectory} traces the mean cumulative
muni-resident-rate change (with IQR) of Inc-bind units relative to
their pre-event baseline. The pre-event window is mechanically flat
at zero by construction (dose is measured relative to the unit's own
$c-1$ rate); the post-event window shows the realized cumulative
$\Delta\tau^{\text{muni}}$ rising to roughly $0.2$ percentage points
for townships, $0.3$ for boroughs, and $0.4$ for cities by $k = +5$.
Figure~\ref{fig:dose_distribution} reports the cross-unit
distribution of the cumulative dose at $k = +5$: most Inc-bind munis
experience cumulative changes of $0.2$--$0.4$ pp, with a small
right tail (Wilkes-Barre and Coatesville) at $0.8$--$0.9$ pp. The
realized-dose ES diagnostic exploits this across-unit variation to

\subsection{Pooled Aggregate Specification}
\label{subsec:pooled-spec}

Before the binding-state decomposition I report an aggregate
specification that pools all six binding-state cells per side into
two direction tags ($\text{Inc}$ for any rate increase, $\text{Dec}$
for any rate decrease). The pooled regression is the
literature-comparable baseline: it answers ``what is the average per-pp
effect of a muni-rate hike on residents, ignoring binding state?'',
which is the elasticity reported in prior local-tax-incidence work
\citep{yang2025,heim2017,bagchiEffectsIntroducingWithholding2022}. The
binding-state decomposition that follows in
Equation~(\ref{eq:headline}) then explains \emph{which} sub-population
of the treated muni cohort drives the pooled coefficient.

For each unit-time observation $(i, t)$ on the stacked panel, define
the per-pp pooled spec
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
Y_{\tilde i \tilde t} \;=\; \alpha_{\tilde i} \;+\; \gamma_{\tilde t}
&+ \beta^{M}_{\text{Inc}}\;\Delta\tau^{M}_{it}\,\mathbf{1}\!\left\{d^{M}_{i} = \text{Inc}\right\}
\;+\; \beta^{M}_{\text{Dec}}\;\Delta\tau^{M}_{it}\,\mathbf{1}\!\left\{d^{M}_{i} = \text{Dec}\right\} \\
&+ \beta^{SD}_{\text{Inc}}\;\Delta\tau^{SD}_{it}\,\mathbf{1}\!\left\{d^{SD}_{i} = \text{Inc}\right\}
\;+\; \beta^{SD}_{\text{Dec}}\;\Delta\tau^{SD}_{it}\,\mathbf{1}\!\left\{d^{SD}_{i} = \text{Dec}\right\} \\
&+ \mathbf{X}'_{it}\boldsymbol{\phi}
\;+\; \varepsilon_{\tilde i \tilde t},
\end{split}
\label{eq:pooled}
\end{equation}
where $d^{s}_{i} \in \{\text{NC}, \text{Inc}, \text{Dec}\}$ is the
direction tag on side $s \in \{M, SD\}$ (NC is the omitted reference),
$\Delta\tau^{s}_{it}$ is the cumulative dose defined in
Section~\ref{subsec:stacked-continuous}, and the fixed effects and
clustering follow the rule in
Section~\ref{subsec:three-units}: unit-by-stack and year-by-stack
fixed effects, with clustering at the policy-jurisdiction level.

The pooled coefficient $\beta^{M}_{\text{Inc}}$ is a weighted average
of the six binding-state cell coefficients on the muni side
(Inc-bind, Inc-becomes, Inc-nobind; with equivalent decomposition for
Dec). It is interpretable as the elasticity a prior-literature
specification would recover. The decomposition in
Section~\ref{subsec:results-block} then reveals that the pooled
coefficient is dominated by the Inc-bind cell at the block level and
attenuated toward zero in the Inc-nobind placebo cell---direct evidence
that the institutional binding rule, not the statutory rate \emph{per
se}, drives the response.

I also report a \emph{binary} analog of Equation~(\ref{eq:pooled}) in
which the four per-pp interactions are replaced by four
post-treatment indicators
$\{\mathrm{everInc}^{M}_{it}, \mathrm{everDec}^{M}_{it},
\mathrm{everInc}^{SD}_{it}, \mathrm{everDec}^{SD}_{it}\}$, each equal
to $1$ in the event year and after for the relevant direction on the
relevant side. The binary coefficients are comparable to the
event-study coefficients in Bagchi
(\citeyear{bagchiEffectsIntroducingWithholding2022}); both versions
appear together in Section~\ref{subsec:results-block}.

\subsection{Three Estimating Units}
\label{subsec:three-units}

The headline equation~(\ref{eq:headline}) is estimated at three units of
observation. The choice of unit interacts directly with how the two
rate-setting bodies (muni and school district) enter the regression.

\paragraph{Municipality level.}
Unit $= $ municipality $\times$ year. Workplace-panel and residence-panel
outcomes are constructed by aggregating LODES origin--destination flows
over blocks within the municipality. The municipal resident rate enters the
regression at its native level. The school district resident rate is
assigned to each muni-year as $\max(\tau^{SD})$ across the school
districts the muni reports to in DCED records; for the 97.6\% of munis
that report to a single SD this is exact, and for the 2.4\% that span
multiple SDs the max-aggregation is conservative. Fiscal share controls
are taken from the muni AFR. Fixed effects: muni-by-stack and year-by-stack.
Standard errors clustered at the muni level.

\paragraph{School district level.}
Unit $= $ SD $\times$ year. Outcomes are constructed by aggregating
LODES OD flows over blocks within the SD. The SD resident rate enters
at its native level. The municipal resident rate is assigned to each
SD-year as the within-SD aggregation across the SD's constituent
munis. Fiscal share controls are taken from Census F-33 SD-level
revenue and expenditure data, mirroring the muni AFR shares. Fixed
effects: SD-by-stack and year-by-stack. Standard errors clustered at
the SD level.

\paragraph{Block level.}
Unit $= $ census block $\times$ year. Each block is assigned its own
muni and its own SD, so both rate-setters' policy variation enters the
regression at its native level with no within-jurisdiction aggregation.
Outcomes are block-defined: log of workers living in the block, log of
workers working in the block, the share of block residents who work in
the block's own municipality, and the share of block residents who
work outside the municipality. Fiscal share controls include both muni
AFR shares and F-33 SD shares, since each block is exposed to both
governments. Fixed effects: block-by-stack and year-by-stack.
Standard errors two-way clustered at the muni and SD levels.

The block level is the headline causal estimate in this paper, for
two reasons. First, the block is the only level where both
rate-setters enter at their native level (no within-jurisdiction
aggregation step that the reader has to defend). Second, the
realized-dose pre-trend diagnostic (Table~\ref{tab:es_pooled_block})
is clean only at the block level; the municipality-level pre-trend
on the log count of residents working in their own municipality fails parallel trends
($\beta_{\text{pre}} = +0.149$, $|t| = 2.66$). The municipality
level is retained in the main text as a coarser cross-check and as
the magnitude-calibration target (the typical PA municipality has
$\sim$40 live-and-work workers, a meaningful per-jurisdiction
benchmark). The school-district aggregation produces direction-mixed
coefficients that do not survive the pre-trend diagnostic and is
reported in the Companion Appendix (\texttt{appendix.tex}) for
completeness, not for causal identification.

\subsection{Pre-Trend Diagnostics}
\label{subsec:pretrend-diagnostics}

Because the first-difference dose $\Delta\tau_{it}$ is mechanically
zero in all pre-event periods by construction, the headline
specification cannot estimate conventional pre-treatment lead
coefficients. I report two complementary event-study diagnostics
designed for this setting. The first tests \textit{strong} parallel
trends across treatment-intensity magnitudes (recommended by the
recent continuous-DiD literature for designs with a continuous dose).
The second tests \textit{average} parallel trends under a binary
cohort treatment and complements the first by capturing pre-event
mean differences regardless of dose.

\subsubsection{Realized-Dose Sun--Abraham Event Study}
\label{subsec:realized-dose-es}

To assess parallel trends in the cross-dose sense
\citep[][]{callawaygoodmanbaconsantanna2024,dechaisemartin2024}, I
estimate a separate event-study specification using each unit's
realized cumulative dose,
$\widetilde{D}_i = \tau^M_{i, c+5} - \tau^M_{i,c-1}$, which is
constant within unit and varies across units in proportion to
eventual treatment intensity. For each muni-side cell $c$, restrict
to the subset of units in $\{$NC$, c\}$ and run
\begin{equation}
Y_{it} = \alpha_{\tilde i} + \gamma_{\tilde t}
+ \sum_{k \neq -1} \beta^M_k\, \mathbf{1}\{\ell_{it} = k\}\,
\widetilde{D}_i
+ \mathbf{X}'_{it}\boldsymbol{\phi} + \varepsilon_{it},
\label{eq:realized-dose-es}
\end{equation}
where $\ell_{it} = t - c_i^M$ is event-time relative to the unit's
first rate event. The $\beta^M_k$ at pre-event leads ($k \leq -2$)
test whether units with larger eventual rate changes were on
differentially sloped pre-event trajectories; under parallel trends
in the strong sense, these coefficients are zero. The post-event
coefficients ($\beta^M_k$ for $k \geq 0$) recover the per-pp
dose-response at each event time and average to the corresponding
cell coefficient in the headline specification when the unit's rate
path is a single step at the event year.

\subsubsection{Binary Sun--Abraham IW Event Study}
\label{subsec:binary-sa-es}

The realized-dose diagnostic above tests whether \textit{larger}
eventual rate changes are associated with differential pre-trends. A
complementary diagnostic asks the simpler question of whether
ever-treated units differ in mean trajectory from never-treated units,
ignoring dose magnitude. For this I use the
Sun--Abraham interaction-weighted event study \citep{sunab2021}
implemented in \texttt{fixest::sunab()}:
\begin{equation}
Y_{it} = \alpha_i + \gamma_t
+ \sum_{e}\sum_{k \neq -1} \delta_{e,k}\,
\mathbf{1}\{c_i = e\}\, \mathbf{1}\{\ell_{it} = k\}
+ \mathbf{X}'_{it}\boldsymbol{\phi} + \varepsilon_{it},
\label{eq:sunab-binary}
\end{equation}
estimating a separate dynamic ATT $\delta_{e,k}$ per cohort $e$ and
event-time $k$, then interaction-weighting the $\delta_{e,k}$ across
cohorts to deliver a single cohort-robust path of pre- and post-event
coefficients. Binary cohort assignment is constructed per
binding-state cell: ever-Inc-bind munis are coded $\mathbf{1}$ in the
year of their first Inc-bind event and thereafter, NC munis are
coded $\mathbf{0}$ throughout, and other cells are excluded from the
cell-specific subset.

The two diagnostics are complementary: failure of the realized-dose
test indicates dose-magnitude selection (high-dose units were
trending differently), while failure of the binary test indicates
\emph{any} mean differential trajectory between treated and control
units. Passing both is required for clean identification under the
strong parallel-trends assumption. Block-level binary SA-IW is
unstable (thin cohort $\times$ event-time cells under stacking) and
is reported at the municipality and SD levels only.

\subsection{Sample Variants}
\label{subsec:sample-variants}

The DCED nonresident-rate panel has reporting gaps---55--60\% coverage
pre-Act 32, 72--80\% post---which affect the binding-state classification
when nonres is missing. We run three sample variants:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Strict:} drop muni-years with NA \texttt{muni\_nonres\_eit}.
\item \textbf{Filled:} impute \texttt{muni\_nonres\_eit = resident\_eit}
when missing (statute-anchored: PA's nonresident rate is bounded above
by the resident rate by Act 511).
\item \textbf{Drop-incomplete:} keep all years; drop NA at regression
time per outcome.
\end{enumerate}

The headline tables use the drop-incomplete sample (largest $N$). Filled
and strict sample variants are reported as robustness columns. Coefficients
are stable across variants.

\section{Results}

This section reports the continuous stacked-DiD dose-response

\paragraph{Pooled vs.\ binding-cell magnitudes.}
The pooled coefficients in
Tables~\ref{tab:pooled_per_pp_block}--\ref{tab:pooled_per_pp_sd} mask
substantial within-direction heterogeneity. At the block level, the
pooled-Inc post-event coefficient on the log count of workers living
in the block is approximately $-0.08$, while the realized-dose ES
post-event mean restricted to the Inc-bind cell alone is $-0.305$ (a
fourfold attenuation when Inc-bind is averaged with Inc-becomes and
the Inc-nobind placebo cells). At the municipality level the pooled-Inc
coefficient on the log count of residents working in their own
municipality is approximately $-0.23$, compared to an Inc-bind cell
coefficient of $-0.401$. The pooled coefficient is a sample-share
weighted average of the binding-state cell coefficients; the
decomposition in Sections~\ref{subsec:results-block}--
\ref{subsec:results-headline} shows that this aggregate is driven
almost entirely by Inc-bind, while Inc-nobind delivers the predicted
null behavioral response. This is the central point of the
pooled-then-decompose structure: the institutional binding rule, not
the statutory rate change itself, identifies the response.

\paragraph{Pre-trend pattern on Dec.}
Pooled-Dec coefficients deserve a separate caveat. The block-level
pooled-Dec ES coefficient on the log count of workers living in the
block has a post-event mean of $+0.22$ and a pre-event lead of $+0.17$;
the post/pre difference is small and the pre-trend fails parallel
trends. The Dec direction reflects selection of a small set of
declining or politically distinct munis into rate cuts, not a clean
binding-rule mirror of the Inc effect, and is reported here for
completeness. The Inc direction, with its clean pre-trend on the
count outcomes, carries the causal weight in the analysis.

\paragraph{Binary-event analog.}
The binary pooled-spec analog
(Companion Appendix, ``Pooled Inc/Dec Binary-Event Analog'')
replaces the per-pp dose interactions with four post-treatment
indicators, comparable to a Bagchi
(\citeyear{bagchiEffectsIntroducingWithholding2022}) binary DiD spec.
Coefficients agree in sign with the per-pp pooled estimates and are
deferred to the Companion Appendix in long form; we report them here
only as a robustness column at the headline outcome.

\paragraph{Within-design placebo.}
The Inc-nobind cell is the within-design placebo: munis whose home
stack remained below the muni's nonresident rate after the rate hike,
so the bill never moved for in-muni residents. The institutional rule
predicts no behavioral response, and the Inc-nobind coefficient on
the log count of residents working in their own municipality is statistically null ($-0.062$, SE
$0.169$); on log total jobs is $-0.201$ (SE $0.203$); and
on the share of workers living in their own muni is $-0.012$ (SE $0.008$). The placebo
passes on the headline outcomes. Two outcomes show small marginally
significant deviations (the log count of out-commuters $+0.217^{**}$
and log employed residents $+0.194^{**}$); with $n=26$ in this
cell these are imprecise and we disclose rather than claim a perfectly
clean placebo.

\paragraph{Channel separation across rate-setters.}
The joint regression includes both muni-resident-rate cells and
SD-resident-rate cells. The muni resident rate moves residents
(Inc-bind coefficient on log residents working in their own
municipality: $-0.401^{***}$; on log employed residents:
$-0.100^{***}$) and in-commuters ($-0.212^{**}$), consistent with the
local-services demand spillover in
Section~\ref{subsec:het-industry}. The SD resident-rate Inc-bind
coefficient on the same outcomes is $+0.177^{*}$ on log residents
working in their own municipality and $+0.063$ on log employed
residents: SD rate changes do not produce a clean sorting response
through the binding channel even when SD changes flip the binding
state. An earlier draft using tract-centroid LODES recovered a
positive SD-rate ``attraction'' effect; under block-level LODES with
the unified two-rate-setter design that artifact disappears. The
work-side nonresident rate $\tau^{\text{nr}}$ enters the
binding-state classification but is not itself a treatment in the
headline regression; its cell coefficients are imprecise because the
muni nonresident rate almost never exceeds the muni's own home stack
at the muni-self-pair level in PA.

\paragraph{Magnitude calibration.}
The municipality-level Inc-bind coefficient on
the log count of residents working in their own municipality is $-0.401^{***}$, larger in absolute
value than the block-level coefficient on the log count of workers living in the block
($-0.208^{***}$). The municipality-level magnitude is partly

the block-level pre-trend is clean). The municipality-level estimate
remains useful for back-of-envelope welfare calibration: the
dependent-variable mean of the log count of residents working in their own municipality is $3.68$,
so the typical municipality has $e^{3.68} \approx 40$ live-and-work
workers, and a 1 pp Inc-bind hike predicts
$40\cdot (e^{-0.401} - 1) \approx -13$ workers; actual Inc-bind hikes
average 0.27 pp, scaling to roughly $-3.6$ workers per typical event.
The block-level estimate is the defensible per-pp causal quantity;
the municipality-level estimate is the typical-jurisdiction
calibration target. Both directionally agree.

\subsection{Mechanism: Industry Decomposition}
\label{subsec:het-industry}

The local-services demand spillover hypothesis predicts that consumer-
facing jobs (retail, food, arts, other services---sectors whose demand
depends on local population) fall when the muni resident rate hikes,
while tradeable jobs (manufacturing, wholesale, information, finance,
professional services---sectors selling to broader markets) are insulated.
Using LODES Workplace Area Characteristics aggregated to the municipality
by NAICS supersector, Table~\ref{tab:industry_cousub} reports the
muni-resident-rate cell coefficients by industry bucket.

Consumer-facing jobs fall by 30 percent in the Inc-bind cell; tradeable
jobs do not (point estimate is $+0.236$, statistically indistinguishable
from zero). The composition shift is captured by the share of tradeable jobs,
which rises 11 percentage points. The total-jobs decline operates
\emph{via} local-services demand spillover, not via direct nonresident-tax
incidence.

The same decomposition at the SD level
(Table~\ref{tab:industry_sd}) is qualitatively different: aggregating
municipality-level industry counts to school district via the muni--SD
assignment produces a positive Inc-bind coefficient on
log consumer-facing jobs ($+0.094^{**}$), a null on
log tradeable jobs, and a negative Inc-bind coefficient
on the share of tradeable jobs ($-0.031^{*}$). The SD-level pattern
indicates that the consumer-services contraction documented at the
municipality scale is a within-municipality, muni-local response that averages
out (or even partially reverses) when aggregated to the school-
district level. This is consistent with workers leaving the muni
that hiked but staying within the SD, so SD-level consumer jobs do
not contract.

\subsection{Summary}
\label{subsec:results-summary}

The results follow a pooled--decompose--mechanism narrative.
Section~\ref{subsec:results-pooled} establishes the aggregate per-pp
effects of muni and SD rate changes, pooled by direction; the
block-level pooled-Inc coefficient on resident workers and the
cleaner block-level pre-trend identify the causal sign.
Sections~\ref{subsec:results-block}--\ref{subsec:results-headline}
decompose the pooled-Inc coefficient by binding state and show that
the response is concentrated in the Inc-bind cell at both block and
municipality levels, with the within-design Inc-nobind placebo null
on count outcomes.
Section~\ref{subsec:het-industry} traces the binding-cell job loss
to consumer-facing service industries, consistent with a
local-services demand spillover from departing residents rather than
direct nonresident-tax incidence. Robustness and pre-trend
diagnostics are in Section~\ref{sec:robustness}; SD-level
decomposition is in the Companion Appendix.

\section{Robustness}
\label{sec:robustness}
%==========================================================

This section reports robustness analyses of the headline dose-response:
sample-variant comparison across the strict/filled/drop NA-handling
specifications (Section~\ref{subsec:rob-sample}), the realized-dose
event-study pre-trend diagnostic (Table~\ref{tab:es_pooled_block}),
dose-magnitude linearity (Section~\ref{subsec:rob-dose-tercile}), and an
alternative control-group definition
(Section~\ref{subsec:rob-controls}). Note: the pre-/post-Act-32
decomposition (Section~\ref{subsec:rob-act32}) is a heterogeneity

\subsection{Sample Variant Robustness}
\label{subsec:rob-sample}

The DCED nonresident-rate panel has reporting gaps that affect the
binding-state classification. We run three sample variants: strict
(drop NA \texttt{muni\_nonres\_eit}), filled (impute
\texttt{nonres = resident\_eit} when missing, statute-anchored), and
drop-incomplete (keep all years; drop NA at regression time per
outcome). The headline Inc-bind coefficient on log residents working in own muni is
$-0.401^{***}$ in all three samples; the cell is robust to NA-handling
because Inc-bind classification requires both pre and post home\_stack
to exceed work\_nonres, which excludes munis where the binding state
cannot be determined.

\subsection{Pre/Post Act 32 Decomposition}
\label{subsec:rob-act32}

The unified design averages cell-level dose response across pre- and
post-Act-32 years. Act 32 of 2008 (effective January 1, 2012) consolidated
PA EIT collection and mandated employer withholding at the higher of the
home stack or the work nonresident rate. Before Act 32, the bill rule
existed in statute but withholding was fragmented and many munis did
not even publish a nonresident rate. The binding-rule mechanism in our
design only operates when the rate change actually moves a worker's bill,
which requires the rule to be enforced.

We test this by re-running the headline cell $\times$ dose specification
on subsamples defined by the cohort's first event year: pre-Act-32
cohorts ($c < 2012$) and post-Act-32 cohorts ($c \geq 2012$). The split
is run at all three estimating units. Tables below report the muni-rate
Inc-cell coefficients pre and post.

\paragraph{The binding-cell effect strengthens after enforcement
consolidation.}
At the municipality level, the Inc-bind coefficient on
the log count of residents working in their own municipality is $-0.339^{**}$ in the pre-2012
sub-sample and $-0.534^{***}$ in the post-2012 sub-sample---roughly
a 50\% larger response after Act 32 makes the EIT binding through
consolidated employer withholding. The same pattern holds on
the log count of in-commuters ($-0.156$ pre vs $-0.334^{***}$ post),
\texttt{log(employed residents)} ($-0.098^{**}$ vs $-0.104^{***}$),
and the share of workers living in their own muni ($-0.047^{***}$ vs $-0.040^{***}$). The
pre-Act-32 Inc-bind effects are non-zero and statistically detectable
on most outcomes, so the binding-rule mechanism was not entirely
inert before 2012; the enforcement break amplifies the response
rather than activating it from a null baseline. The pooled headline
($-0.401^{***}$ on the log count of residents working in their own municipality; Section
\ref{subsec:results-headline}) sits between the pre and post
estimates, as expected.

\paragraph{The within-design placebo (Inc-nobind) is null on the
headline outcomes in both periods} (the log count of residents working in their own municipality
$-0.051$ pre, $-0.094$ post; the share of workers living in their own muni $-0.013$ pre,
$-0.010$ post). The institutional rule's predicted-null cell is
satisfied regardless of the enforcement regime, which is consistent
with the binding-rule interpretation: cells where the bill never
moves should not respond in either period.

\paragraph{Block-level Inc-bind is roughly constant pre and post.}
At the block level, the muni-cohort Inc-bind coefficient on
the log count of workers living in the block is comparable in the two periods (the
block-level effect predates the post-2012 enforcement break,
contradicting a story in which all of the binding-channel response
is driven by withholding consolidation). One reading: at fine
geography, even pre-2012 statutory rate changes were salient
enough through other channels (taxpayer reporting, local awareness)
to induce sorting. The Act-32 amplification at the municipality
scale may reflect aggregation rather than a discontinuous mechanism
change.

\paragraph{Connection to Bagchi (2022).}
\citet{bagchiEffectsIntroducingWithholding2022} documents that Act 32
increased PA EIT compliance via consolidated employer withholding.
Our pre/post decomposition shows that the binding-cell residential
response also strengthens after 2012 at the municipality scale; the
two findings are complementary---compliance enforcement and behavioral
response both grow under Act 32.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
%==========================================================

The continuous stacked DiD design produces four substantive findings.
The block-level model is the headline causal estimate; the
municipality-level model is the cross-check and the
typical-jurisdiction magnitude calibration; the school-district-level
aggregation is reported in the Companion Appendix for completeness.

\paragraph{Sorting is identified through the binding channel.}
The Inc-bind cell---rate hikes that move the bill---reduces
the log count of workers living in the block at the block level by approximately 19
percent per pp ($-0.21$ log points, $\text{SE} = 0.054$). The
municipality-level analogue is larger ($-0.40$ log points on
the log count of residents working in their own municipality, $\approx 33\%$ per pp). The Inc-nobind
placebo---rate hikes whose post-event home stack remains below the
nonresident rate---is statistically indistinguishable from zero on the
block-level count outcomes ($-0.056$ on the log count of workers living in the block,
$-0.113$ on the log count of workers at the block) and on the municipality-level
the log count of residents working in their own municipality ($-0.057$). Inc-becomes lies between the
two. The pattern matches the institutional rule's prediction: the
same statutory rate change can produce a strong, moderate, or null
behavioral response depending solely on whether the rate moves the
bill. This is direct evidence that workers respond to taxes they
actually pay rather than to statutory rates per se.

\paragraph{Block-level identification has cleaner pre-trends than
the municipality aggregation.}
The realized-dose Sun--Abraham event study at the municipality level
shows a positive pre-event lead on the log count of residents working in their own municipality for
the Inc-bind cell ($\beta_{\text{pre}} = +0.149$, $|t| = 2.66$),
consistent with high-eventual-dose munis being on different
trajectories before their rate change. At the block level the same
diagnostic produces a near-zero pre-event coefficient on
the log count of workers living in the block ($\beta_{\text{pre}} = -0.027$, $|t| < 1$),
indicating that the gentrification-correlated pre-trend in the
municipality coefficient is absorbed at finer geography. The
block-level result is therefore the defensible per-pp causal quantity
under the strong-parallel-trends assumption required by the recent
continuous-DiD literature; the municipality-level estimate remains
useful for back-of-envelope welfare calibration but should be cited
with the pre-trend caveat.

\paragraph{Channel separation between muni and SD rate-setters.}
The muni resident-rate Inc-bind coefficient on resident outcomes is
large and consistent at both the block and municipality levels. The
SD-resident-rate cells, estimated jointly in the same regression,
produce smaller and direction-mixed effects: $+0.177^{*}$ at the
municipality level on the log count of residents working in their own municipality, $+0.097$ on
block-level log count of workers living in the block ($p=0.12$), and $+0.066^{*}$
at the SD level (sign-reversed). The SD rate is not the dominant
driver of residential sorting through the binding channel. This is a
substantive change from an earlier draft that found a positive SD-rate
``attraction'' effect, which was an artifact of tract-centroid LODES
aggregation; under block-level LODES with the unified two-rate-setter
design, SD-rate effects on residents are small or wrong-signed.

\paragraph{Mechanism: local-services demand spillover.}
Industry-decomposed outcomes at the municipality level show
consumer-facing jobs (retail, food, arts, other services) fall by
approximately 26 percent in the Inc-bind cell ($-0.30$ log points,
$\text{SE} = 0.15$) while tradeable jobs (manufacturing, wholesale,
finance, professional services) are insulated (point estimate
$+0.24$, statistically indistinguishable from zero). The composition
shift is captured by the share of tradeable jobs rising approximately
11 percentage points in the binding cell. The decline in total jobs
operates \emph{via} local-services demand spillover from departing
residents, not via direct nonresident-tax incidence.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
Three limitations merit acknowledgment. First, while the block-level
stacked headline on the count outcomes is large, negative, and
well-identified, the block-level share outcome
(the share of block residents working in their own muni) is null in the binding cell
($-0.035$, $p = 0.26$) and marginally significant in the Inc-nobind
placebo ($-0.037$, $p = 0.015$). The share outcome at block scale
appears to reflect mechanical denominator dynamics on very small
populations rather than binding-state-specific behavior, so the
share-based block evidence should be read as suggestive rather than
identified. The count-based block evidence remains the cleanest test.
Second, the school-district aggregation produces direction-mixed
coefficients and a marginally non-zero realized-dose pre-event lead;
the SD-level results should be read as confirming the directional
sign of the muni-rate effect under a coarser aggregation rather than
as standalone identification of an SD-channel response. Third, the
LODES OD data covers PA municipalities but not cross-state inflows
in the intra-state main file used here; the auxiliary file
(cross-state inflows) is downloaded but not yet incorporated, which
would tighten the estimates of in-commuters for border munis.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
%==========================================================

This paper develops a continuous stacked difference-in-differences
design for identifying the behavioral response to local income tax
changes. The design exploits the piecewise structure of the
Pennsylvania EIT
($T_{ji} = \max(\tau^{\text{res,muni}}_j + \tau^{\text{SD}}_j,\
\tau^{\text{nr,muni}}_i)$) by classifying each rate-setter's events
by direction and binding-state transition. The headline causal
estimate is at the census block level, the only level at which
both rate-setters' policy variation enters the regression at its
native level; municipality-level results are reported as a coarser
cross-check and as the typical-jurisdiction magnitude calibration;
school-district-level aggregation results are in the Companion
Appendix.

The headline finding is that a 1 pp muni resident-rate increase in
the binding cell reduces the block-level count of workers living in the block (in logs) by
approximately 19 percent ($-0.21$ log points, $\text{SE} = 0.054$)
with a clean realized-dose parallel-trends test
($\beta_{\text{pre}} = -0.027$, $|t| < 1$). The municipality-level
analogue is larger ($-0.40$ log points on the log count of residents working in their own municipality,
$\approx 33\%$ per pp) but its pre-trend fails parallel trends
($\beta_{\text{pre}} = +0.149$, $|t| = 2.66$), consistent with
selection of declining post-industrial cities into rate hikes; the
block-level specification absorbs that pre-trend at finer geography.
School district rate changes do not move residents through the
binding channel at any estimating unit, even when SD changes flip
the binding state. Industry decomposition at the municipality level
shows the total-jobs decline is concentrated in consumer-facing
sectors (retail, food, arts, other services), consistent with
local-services demand spillover from departing residents rather than
direct nonresident-tax incidence.

The design framework---a continuous stacked DiD spec estimated at the
worker-resolution unit of observation, with two rate-setters' cells
decomposed jointly by institutional binding state, a realized-dose
event study as a strong-parallel-trends diagnostic, and a
within-design placebo cell (Inc-nobind)---is portable to other
settings where a piecewise tax rule generates predictable
cross-sectional variation in whether a rate change moves the bill.
Local sales taxes with use-tax-credit rules, county-level income-tax
credits across state borders, and special tax districts with
overlapping jurisdictions are candidate extensions.

\section{Pre-Trend Tests on Fiscal Observables}
\label{app:pretrends}

We test the parallel trends assumption on eleven observables---six fiscal
variables from the PA DCED Annual Financial Reports, real estate market
values, total employment from LODES, Part~I index crime from the FBI
Uniform Crime Reports, and housing units permitted from the Census
Building Permits Survey---using the \citet{sunab2021} estimator.
For each outcome, we estimate Equation~\ref{eq:sunab-binary} with municipality
and year fixed effects, clustering standard errors at the municipality
level. Table~\ref{tab:pretrends} reports the maximum absolute
$t$-statistic among the pre-treatment coefficients at relative times
$\ell \in \{-5, -4, -3, -2\}$.

Pre-trends are flat for nine of eleven observables: total revenue
(max $|t| = 1.57$), EIT revenue ($1.40$), RE tax revenue ($1.69$),
streets expenditure ($0.70$), population ($1.09$), total debt ($1.36$),
total jobs from LODES ($0.83$), index crime ($0.68$), and building
permits ($1.36$). None of the pre-treatment coefficients for these
outcomes are individually significant at the 5\% level.
The jobs, crime, and permits results are particularly important: they
confirm that treated and control municipalities were on parallel
trajectories in employment, public safety, and housing development
before the rate change, ruling out differential economic trends as
confounds.

Police expenditure is an exception. The pre-treatment coefficients at
$\ell = -5$ and $\ell = -4$ are negative and significant ($t = -2.85$ and
$t = -2.42$, respectively), indicating that treated municipalities had
relatively lower police spending 4--5 years before the rate increase.
This pattern suggests that some EIT increases may have been associated with
anticipated public-safety expansions financed by the new revenue. Importantly,
the main results on the share of workers living in their own muni are robust to controlling for
log police expenditure (the robustness section (Section~\ref{sec:robustness})), confirming that this
pre-trend does not confound the estimated sorting response.

Market value of real estate also shows a pre-trend at $\ell = -3$
($t = -2.59$), but this variable has limited coverage (pre-2012 only,
$N = 9{,}338$) and the pre-trend may reflect composition effects in the
truncated sample rather than a genuine violation of parallel trends.
